Here's a thought that might make you look at your next pull request description differently: the tool your AI assistant uses to talk to you might be fundamentally broken. An engineer at Anthropic has been making a case that's creating quite a stir in developer circles — aitch! tee! tee! el! could be a superior interface for AI agents compared to Markdown, and the reasons why reveal something fascinating about where human and AI collaboration is heading.

Markdown became the default language of human and AI communication almost by accident. When AI tools started becoming mainstream, everything was Markdown — planning documents, specifications, prompts, architecture notes, pull request summaries, even agent memory. It was the natural choice because it was simple, portable, and readable. Early AI workflows operated on an assumption: humans would constantly read and edit files directly, so plain text made perfect sense.

But here's where things get interesting. That assumption no longer holds. Agents now generate larger, richer, more complex content than anyone imagined when Markdown became the standard, and Markdown struggles to handle it.

The real shift becomes clear when you look at what agents are actually producing. We're talking about SVG diagrams, interactive controls, annotated diffs that highlight exactly what changed and why, draggable prioritization boards where you can reorganise tasks with a click, animated prototypes that bring designs to life, implementation visualizations that show data flowing through a system, and custom editors for structured configs. None of this fits neatly into a Markdown document.

Now, the core argument isn't about aesthetics — it never was. It's about something deeper: the communication layer between humans and agents is evolving beyond plain text. This matters because of something I've been thinking about lately, which is the human attention bottleneck. Most engineers, if they're being honest, don't thoroughly read lengthy Markdown implementation plans anymore. There's simply too much being produced, and not enough hours in the day.

Agents have started compensating by building artifacts that convey understanding visually rather than linguistically. Instead of a wall of text describing how a system works, you get a diagram you can interact with. Instead of a paragraph explaining a data structure, you get a visual representation you can manipulate. The agent's output is no longer just code — increasingly, it's understanding itself.

And that brings us to the real question worth sitting with: if agents generate code faster than humans can review it, the bottleneck becomes the interface through which we stay cognitively connected to and understand what agents produce. Whether that interface is Markdown, aitch! tee! tee! el!, or something entirely different might determine how effectively we collaborate with AI systems going forward. The tools we use to communicate with these agents may shape not just our productivity, but our very understanding of what they're building on our behalf.
