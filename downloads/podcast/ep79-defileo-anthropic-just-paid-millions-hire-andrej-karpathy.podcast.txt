Andrej Karpathy, the man who helped build some of the world's most advanced eh! eye! systems, suddenly realised that the coding tools he'd been using were producing correct code more often than not — and it completely changed how he thought about programming.

This stark transition led him to embrace what he calls "vibe coding" — essentially trusting eh! eye! agents to handle the vast majority of his coding tasks. His side projects folder, he admits, became "extremely full" as a result. And this personal shift opens a window into something much bigger about where software development is heading.

Karpathy frames the evolution of programming across three distinct paradigms. Software one point oh is the traditional model we all know — writing explicit code and rules line by line. Software two point oh emerged with deep learning, where you programme through data sets and neural network training instead. Now we're entering Software three point oh, where programming happens through prompting, and the context window — that window of information you feed the model — becomes your primary lever for shaping what the eh! eye! produces.

What's fascinating is how this intersects with what he calls "verifiability." eh! eye! systems perform brilliantly in domains where you can easily check whether the output is correct — mathematics, code generation, chess. But they remain frustratingly unreliable in areas where verification is trickier. A model might refactor a hundred thousand line codebase without breaking a sweat, then confidently tell you to walk fifty meters to a car wash that doesn't exist. The capabilities are jagged rather than uniform, heavily shaped by what data the labs chose to focus on during training.

For founders, the implication is clear: focus on verifiable domains where you can build reinforcement learning environments. These settings allow effective fine-tuning, and the leverage is enormous. Ultimately, almost everything is automatable, but the difficulty varies dramatically depending on whether you can easily check the work.

The distinction between vibe coding and agentic engineering matters here too. Vibe coding democratises software creation — it raises the floor for anyone who wants to build something. Agentic engineering, by contrast, maintains quality standards while still accelerating delivery, coordinating multiple fallible but powerful agents to work together. The old idea of a ten times engineer gets magnified considerably in this world.

What remains distinctively human? Taste, judgment, aesthetics, and oversight. Understanding fundamentals without necessarily memorising every eh! pee! eye! detail. Writing specifications, shaping top-level design, ensuring proper architecture — like remembering to use unique identifiers for users. The ability to direct and coordinate agents effectively.

Karpathy's "animals versus ghosts" analogy is particularly useful for setting expectations. These systems aren't animals shaped by evolution with intrinsic motivation. They're ghosts — statistical simulations forged from data and reward functions. Once you internalise that distinction, their behaviour and limitations make much more sense.

Looking ahead, infrastructure itself will need redesigning for agents rather than humans. Installation instructions are already becoming "copy paste to your agent" rather than shell scripts. We might see future agents communicating and negotiating on behalf of users and organisations.

One parting thought that sticks with me: you can outsource your thinking, but you cannot outsource your understanding. Humans remain the essential bottleneck for knowing what to build and directing how it's done. The tools may change, but that fundamental role doesn't.
