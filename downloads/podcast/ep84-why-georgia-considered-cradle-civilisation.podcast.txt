What if I told you that some of the oldest evidence of what it means to be human was found not in Egypt, not in Mesopotamia, but in a small country roughly the size of Ireland, wedged between the Caucasus Mountains and the Black Sea? Georgia might not be the first place that springs to mind when you think of the origins of civilisation, but the archaeological discoveries emerging from this unassuming nation over the past few decades have been quietly reshaping everything we thought we knew about where we came from.

Let's start with something truly staggering. In nineteen ninety-one, archaeologists excavating the ruins of a medieval town called Dmanisi, in southern Georgia, stumbled upon something far older than the medieval structures they were actually looking for. Buried beneath those ruins lay fossilised skulls of Homo erectus dating back approximately one point eight million years. These weren't just any fossils — they were the oldest hominin remains ever discovered outside of Africa, predating every previous piece of evidence for humanity's great migration out of the continent by hundreds of thousands of years.

The implications were enormous. Before Dmanisi, scientists believed that our ancestors needed large brains and sophisticated tools before venturing beyond Africa's borders. But the individuals found at Dmanisi had remarkably small brains — roughly half the size of our own — and used only the most primitive stone implements. What this told us is that the impulse to explore, to move, to seek out new territory was wired into us far earlier than we imagined. Georgia, it turns out, was the literal gateway through which our distant ancestors first stepped beyond Africa and into the rest of the world.

Among the five skulls recovered at Dmanisi, one nicknamed "Skull 5" has particularly captivated researchers. This individual had lost most of its teeth years before dying, which suggests that others in the group had cared for it, helping it survive long after it would have starved in the wild. That kind of compassion and social cooperation, emerging nearly two million years ago, is a profoundly human trait that we perhaps didn't expect to find so far back in our family tree.

But Georgia's claim to fame doesn't end there. If this region gave humanity its first stepping stone out of Africa, it also gave us something equally transformative — wine. Archaeological evidence from the Shulaveri-Shomu culture, dating to around six to eight thousand years before our era, reveals that the people of ancient Georgia were the first to cultivate grapes and produce wine on a large scale. In two thousand seventeen, researchers analysing clay jars discovered at sites in southeastern Georgia found chemical residues confirming the presence of grape wine dating back eight thousand years. That's roughly a thousand years older than any similar evidence found in neighbouring Iran or Turkey, making Georgia the undisputed birthplace of winemaking.

What's remarkable is that this tradition never really died out. The traditional Georgian method of winemaking using large clay vessels called qvevri, buried underground to keep the wine at a constant temperature, is still practised today. U.N.E.S.C.O. has recognised this method as part of humanity's intangible cultural heritage, and when you consider that this unbroken tradition stretches back eight millennia, you'll understand why nothing quite like it exists anywhere else on Earth.

Georgia's significance extends well beyond wine. The fertile valleys of the South Caucasus were among the very first places where humans made the monumental shift from hunting and gathering to settled farming. The early Georgian cultures domesticated wheat, barley, and legumes, herded cattle and sheep, and built permanent villages with circular mud-brick houses. They were, in essence, pioneering the agricultural lifestyle that would eventually give rise to cities, writing, and everything we associate with organised civilisation. Archaeological sites across Georgia reveal communities with complex social structures, trade networks, and craft specialisation — the hallmarks of emerging civilisations that predate the famous cities of Mesopotamia and Egypt.

By the second millennium before our era, Georgia was home to sophisticated kingdoms that captured the imagination of ancient civilisations far beyond its borders. The kingdom of Colchis, along the Black Sea coast, was so renowned that it became the destination of Jason and the Argonauts in Greek mythology, the quest for the Golden Fleece. And here is where myth meets reality — the people of Colchis really were skilled gold miners, using sheep fleeces to trap gold particles from mountain streams, a technique reportedly still in use until quite recently. Colchis was no fairy tale. It was a prosperous, trading civilisation with advanced metallurgy and connections stretching across the ancient world.

Throughout history, Georgia's position at the crossroads of Europe and Asia has made it a meeting point of cultures, religions, and empires. The Silk Road passed through its territory, Christianity arrived in the fourth century making Georgia one of the earliest Christian nations, and its unique alphabet — one of only fourteen independent scripts still in use worldwide — has preserved its literary heritage for over a millennium.

So when we speak of the cradle of civilisation, perhaps it's time to look beyond the familiar narratives of Egypt and Mesopotamia. Georgia offers something remarkable — evidence of humanity's first great migration out of Africa, the invention of wine, the dawn of agriculture, and kingdoms wealthy and sophisticated enough to inspire the oldest myths of Western literature. It may not be a chapter in the story of civilisation — it might just be the prologue.
