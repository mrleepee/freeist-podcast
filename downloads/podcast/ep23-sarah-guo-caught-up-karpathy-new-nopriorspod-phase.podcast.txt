A software engineer spending sixteen hours a day working alongside artificial intelligence agents since December last year. That's not a glimpse of some distant future. That's Andre Karpathy's reality right now, and it's fundamentally reshaping how we think about coding, creativity, and the very nature of expertise in the digital age.

Karpathy, who you might know from his work at Tesla and OpenAI, has made a striking observation that deserves serious attention. He's shifted from writing roughly eighty percent of his code himself to relying on AI agents for the vast majority of it. In his own words, he hasn't typed code by hand in months. This isn't about laziness or novelty seeking. It's a deliberate response to what he describes as a significant capability jump that happened in December 2023. He's now running multiple agents in parallel, treating them as macro actions across his repositories, and the bottleneck he faces isn't access to computing power anymore. It's token throughput. The systems simply can't process information fast enough to keep up with demand.

What makes this particularly interesting is how he frames agent failures. When something goes wrong, when an agent produces poor code or misunderstands a task, he doesn't blame the technology. He calls it a skill issue on his part, meaning he hasn't yet found the right instructions or the optimal memory setup to get the desired outcome. This perspective treats AI agents less like tools and more like collaborators with whom you need to develop a working relationship. It's a subtle but important shift in mindset.

One of the agents he's developed is called Claw, and it's worth exploring what this looks like in practice. Claw is an agentic layer with sophisticated memory persistence that goes well beyond what you'd get from default context compaction. Karpathy has built something he calls WDelf Claw, which manages his entire home through WhatsApp. Think about that for a moment. He can control his Sonos speakers, lights, heating and cooling systems, window shades, pool and spa equipment, and security cameras, all through a conversation interface. The agent didn't just connect to these systems on demand. It autonomously scanned his network, reverse engineered the various APIs, and created a unified dashboard. What's striking is his emphasis on personality. He believes agents should feel like teammates rather than dry, utilitarian tools. That's a design philosophy that more developers might do well to consider.

This approach extends into research itself. Karpathy has been experimenting with autonomous discovery, where systems run overnight with minimal human intervention. He let an autonomous researcher loose on GPT-2 training and it found improvements he had missed, specifically around weight decay and beta tuning. The implications are significant. He argues that frontier laboratories should have automated scientists pulling from idea queues, running experiments, and merging successful changes. Research organisations could be described by something like a program.md file, essentially a set of instructions that could be optimised and tuned just like code. That's a provocative way to think about how scientific discovery might be industrialised.

Of course, we shouldn't gloss over the limitations. Karpathy uses the term jaggedness to describe how large language models oscillate between what he calls a brilliant PhD systems programmer and something closer to a ten year old. They're excellent at verifiable tasks, particularly code and mathematics with unit tests, but they struggle with nuanced communication, inferring intent, and asking the right clarifying questions. Joke generation, for instance, has been stuck for years, producing the same tired gags about atoms and scientists. This isn't optimised by reinforcement learning in the way we'd hope. Not everything improves uniformly with scale, and understanding these blind spots is crucial for anyone building products or making strategic decisions.

There's an interesting debate emerging about model design. Currently, laboratories tend to build single monolithic models intended to handle every conceivable task. Karpathy suggests we should expect more specialisation, smaller models with a cognitive core augmented by domain specific expertise. The science of fine tuning without losing existing capabilities is still underdeveloped, and context windows represent cheap manipulation compared to the complexity of modifying weights directly.

On the question of open source versus closed models, there's currently an eight to eighteen month gap, with closed models leading the frontier. Karpathy expects this dynamic to continue, with closed systems acting as what he calls oracles at the frontier while open source fills other niches. He argues this is healthy. Centralisation has a poor historical track record, and having more frontier laboratories competing would benefit everyone through greater diversity of thinking.

The labour market implications follow a pattern you might recognise from economic history. Software engineering demand is still rising despite, or perhaps because of, cheaper tools. This is the Jevons paradox in action. When something becomes more affordable and accessible, demand tends to increase rather than decrease. Digital work will transform first, with the physical world lagging behind. AI currently operates as ghosts in the digital space, manipulating information without physical form. Healthcare workers remain in high demand according to Bureau of Labour Statistics projections, suggesting that certain human centred roles have more resilience than others.

Physical world automation will inevitably come, but the complexity of atoms compared to bits means it lags significantly. Karpathy sees the first transformation happening in digital space, then interfaces that bridge the digital and physical. Information markets could emerge where people pay for physical world data, photographs, experiments. Biology and material science stand out as domains where AI might accelerate discovery dramatically.

Education itself is undergoing a subtle but profound shift. Karpathy developed something called MicroGPT, just two hundred lines of Python that distil the essence of how large language model training works. The interesting question this raises is whether education is increasingly about explaining concepts to agents rather than directly to humans. If agents can understand code, they can explain it to humans with infinite patience at appropriate levels. The human value add shifts to crafting the curriculum itself, identifying the few key insights that matter most. That's a fascinating reorientation of what expertise looks like.

The picture emerging from all of this is one of rapid, uneven transformation. AI agents are becoming genuine collaborators in software development, smart home management, and scientific research. But they're uneven tools, brilliant in some domains and bafflingly limited in others. The organisations and individuals who'll thrive are those who understand both the capabilities and the jagged edges, who can work with the technology rather than simply around it.
