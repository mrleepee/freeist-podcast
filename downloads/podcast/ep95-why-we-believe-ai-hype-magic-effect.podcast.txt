Twelve million tokens. That's the headline a Miami startup called SubQueue dropped onto the tech world in May twenty twenty-six, promising a model that could handle a context window of twelve million tokens, run fifty-two times faster than anything currently available, and cost a fifth of the price. The claim spread like wildfire across social feeds and news aggregator sites, sparking a frenzy of excitement.

The only trouble is that nobody outside the company has been able to test those numbers, and the benchmarks that have circulated cover only a one-million-token version. No public technical report exists, leaving observers to speculate that SubQueue's Sparse Attention layer might be an optimisation sitting on top of an existing model rather than a ground-up architecture.

The team's pedigree—former Meta, former Google—adds a sheen of credibility, but pedigree alone does not replace proof, and this is a textbook case of hype outrunning verification.

A paper that landed in the Journal of Consumer Research offers a neat explanation for why stories like this take off before the ink is dry on any technical report.

Tully, Longoni, and Appel ran seven separate studies across different countries and found a consistent pattern: the less people understand about A I, the more enthusiastic they are about using it.

The driving force behind that enthusiasm is what the researchers call magical thinking. When A I does something that feels like it requires a human touch—like drafting a poem, offering emotional advice, or diagnosing a medical condition—people with lower A I literacy are far more likely to experience a sense of awe. That awe, in turn, fuels a desire to adopt the technology.

Crucially, the effect is not because low-literacy users think A I is more capable or because they are unconcerned about ethics or job displacement. It is specifically the perception of A I as a kind of magical entity that fuels their receptivity.

The link is strongest when the task is framed as requiring a distinctly human attribute—creative writing, emotional support, or medical diagnosis. When the task is presented as something mechanical or computational, even people who understand A I quite well are comfortable with it, so the gap narrows.

Across seven studies, the pattern held. In one study, national levels of A I literacy predicted how receptive that country's population was to A I. In another, undergraduates with lower A I literacy were more likely to say they would use A I for their coursework. A six-month tracking study showed that A I literacy predicted actual usage. A large, representative US sample of over a thousand people demonstrated that the relationship was mediated by magical perceptions. A follow-up added awe as an intermediate step, and the final two studies showed the effect held across a range of task types.

For companies, the message is counterintuitive. Marketing A I to the technically savvy early adopters might be the wrong strategy. The biggest receptive audience is often those who understand A I the least. That suggests firms should consider targeting lower-A I-literacy consumers and designing products that lean into that sense of wonder rather than trying to win over the sceptics.

There is an uncomfortable trade-off hidden in those findings. If demystifying A I—explaining how it actually works—removes the aura of magic, it might also reduce its appeal. A company that invests in education and transparency could inadvertently shrink its market. So there is a tension between the goal of an informed public and the commercial pressure to keep a little mystique alive.

The buzz around SubQueue's unverified claims is a textbook illustration of that tension. People who have spent years wrestling with transformer architectures and the quadratic attention bottleneck are rightly sceptical of a headline figure with no independent benchmarks. But the moment you hear "fifty-two times faster" and "twelve million tokens", the magic kicks in, awe follows, and the next thing you know, the claim is trending. The technology may or may not deliver, but the pattern of uncritical excitement is exactly what the research predicts.

So the next time a breathless claim about A I performance pops up in your feed, it might be worth asking not just whether the numbers add up, but also whether your own sense of awe is doing the heavy lifting. Understanding the magic behind the curtain might make us less dazzled, but it also makes us better judges of what is real.
