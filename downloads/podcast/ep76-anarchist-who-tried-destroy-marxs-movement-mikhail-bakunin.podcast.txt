What if the very tool you use to destroy oppression becomes the new oppression itself? It's a question that haunted the nineteenth century, and one that remains unsettlingly relevant today. At the heart of this debate were two men — Mikhail Bakunin and Karl Marx — whose rivalry didn't just split a revolutionary movement, it fundamentally shaped the course of political thought for the next century and beyond.

Bakunin was born into Russian nobility in eighteen fourteen, yet he discarded every privilege to pursue revolutionary activism across Europe. He took part in uprisings in Prague, Dresden, and Lyon, was imprisoned, and famously escaped from Siberia to London. His philosophy was bold: the state itself was the problem, not a tool to be repurposed. He once wrote that "the passion for destruction is also a creative passion," and he meant it as something hopeful — tearing down what crushes humanity to make room for something freer.

Marx saw things entirely differently. He believed the working class must seize the state, use its machinery to dismantle capitalism, and then let the state wither away. For Marx, the state was a weapon waiting to be picked up. For Bakunin, it was a trap — take hold of it and you inevitably become the very thing you claimed to fight against. He captured this brilliantly when he wrote, "When the people are being beaten with a stick, they are not much happier if it is called the people's stick."

These two visions collided inside the International Working Men's Association, founded in London in eighteen sixty-four. Marx quickly dominated its founding documents and direction. When Bakunin joined in eighteen sixty-nine, he tried to build his own faction, but Marx launched a campaign against him, circulating confidential accusations about secret subsidies and covert networks — a campaign Marx later admitted to Engels had spiralled beyond his intentions.

The most damaging evidence came from Bakunin's association with a Russian radical named Sergey Nechayev. They'd collaborated on a radical manual, but Nechayev was a manipulator who murdered a fellow conspirator and sent a blackmail letter using Bakunin's name without his knowledge. Bakunin severed ties immediately, calling Nechayev's methods "Jesuit-like," but the letter became centrepiece evidence against him.

At the Hague Congress in eighteen seventy-two, Marx read that blackmail letter aloud to the inquiry commission. Bakunin was expelled by twenty-seven votes to seven. The International effectively collapsed — formally dissolved in eighteen seventy-six, just two weeks after Bakunin's death. Historians confirm the documents were genuine, but Marx had selectively framed them, portraying loose allies as a disciplined conspiracy directly tied to Bakunin's character.

The divergence in their legacies is striking. Bakunin's anarchist tradition took root most powerfully in Spain, where the C.N.T. grew to over a million members by nineteen thirty-six. During the Spanish Civil War, workers seized factories and collectivised land across Aragon — a remarkable experiment that lasted roughly three years before Franco crushed it. Marx's Marxist tradition, meanwhile, produced the Soviet state and countless revolutionary parties that captured power in the name of the working class but held it in their own name — exactly the new class Bakunin had warned against.

The question Bakunin posed at the Hague — can state power truly abolish state power? — was never settled then and remains unanswered now. What we can say is this: the twentieth century did not prove him wrong.
