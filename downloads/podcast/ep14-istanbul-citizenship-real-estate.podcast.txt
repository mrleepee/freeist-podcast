They already own property in Dubai, so they’re looking for a smart way to diversify, earn a steady rental income, and, let’s be honest, have a little more freedom to travel on a Turkish passport.

Our investor’s first stop was a compact little flat tucked away in the Meshruchad neighbourhood, just a five‑minute stroll from the chic Nisantasi district. We’re talking about a 50‑ to 55‑square‑metre apartment that’s listed at around $150 k. What makes it so appealing? For a start, it’s perfectly positioned for short‑term rentals—if you furnish it, you can comfortably ask for about $800 a month. The building’s maintenance fees are refreshingly low, hovering around $20‑30 each month, and there’s no immediate need for renovations. You’ll also find a metro station nearby, which means your future tenants can zip around the city with ease. Bright and airy, the place is expected to hit near‑full occupancy, and because it’s a relatively modest unit, it should be a breeze to sell on if you ever decide to cash out.

But the investor isn’t the type to dive head‑first without a proper feel for the city. Over a few days on the ground, they wandered the streets, hopped on public transport, and met with a local lawyer to get a sense of Istanbul’s rhythm. That on‑the‑ground scouting gave them the confidence to then make purchase decisions remotely, with the help of Keith, a seasoned property expert, and the lawyer who knows the ins and outs of Turkish paperwork. After that initial taste, the investor cast their eyes toward a larger apartment right near Taksim Square—only 200 metres away. This one comes with a full suite of amenities: a pool, a spa, a gym, underground parking, and a higher monthly HOA bill of about $400, which already covers heat, water, and internet. The unit is essentially Airbnb‑ready. With a nightly rate hovering around $150 and an expected occupancy of roughly 65 percent, after the typical 20 percent property‑management fee, the net pre‑tax yield lands somewhere between 6 and 12 percent. That’s a decent bump above the long‑term rental yield of around 4.5 percent you might get on a traditional lease. And here’s a little insider tip: recent Turkish regulations now require short‑term rentals to operate only in licensed buildings, which has tightened supply and helped keep nightly rates firm.

Of course, any conversation about Istanbul real estate inevitably brings us to the Turkish lira. Rental income is paid in TL, and the currency can be a bit of a roller‑coaster ride. The good news is that rents are reviewed annually, with allowable increases that tend to keep pace with inflation. Meanwhile, the value of the property itself tends to track the US dollar over the long run, which means many investors treat real estate in Istanbul as a hedge against currency swings.

It’s worth noting that about half of the clients we work with end up purchasing remotely after that initial on‑the‑ground scout.
