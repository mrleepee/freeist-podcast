Around two hundred million people use ChatGPT every single week, which makes it one of the fastest-growing consumer products in history. But despite those staggering numbers, I reckon most of us are only scratching the surface of what these language models can actually do. Today I want to take a proper look at practical applications of large language models, moving beyond the novelty of asking ChatGPT silly questions and into territory that could genuinely transform how you work, learn, and solve problems. Whether you're a complete beginner or someone who's been playing with these tools for a while, I think you'll find something useful here.

Let's start with the landscape, because it helps to understand what you're actually choosing between. OpenAI's ChatGPT is the incumbent and arguably the most feature-rich option available right now. It's the product that brought generative AI into mainstream conversation, and it remains the one most likely to receive new capabilities first. But it's far from the only player in town. Anthropic's Claude has carved out a strong reputation, particularly among developers and anyone who values longer, more nuanced responses. Google's Gemini integrates deeply with Google's ecosystem of products. There's xAI's Grok, which has its own distinctive personality. China's DeepSeek has made waves with capable models at competitive price points. And France's Mistral has been gaining traction, especially in European contexts where data sovereignty matters. The important thing to grasp is that different providers offer different model tiers with different capabilities and pricing structures. What works brilliantly on one platform might be mediocre on another, or simply not exist. It's worth keeping an open mind and matching the tool to the task rather than defaulting to a single favourite.

Now, understanding a bit about how these systems work under the bonnet can genuinely help you use them more effectively. At their core, language models process text by breaking it into something called tokens. A token is roughly equivalent to four characters of text, so a typical short query might consume around fifteen tokens, while a response might run to nineteen tokens or more. This matters because every model has a context window, essentially a one-dimensional stream of tokens that serves as the model's working memory. Think of it like the amount of desk space you have to spread out your papers while working on a problem. You can only keep so much information visible at once before things start getting cluttered. Understanding this helps you appreciate why very long conversations can sometimes produce less coherent responses, and why starting a new chat when you switch topics often gives better results than continuing an old one.

The training process for these models happens in two distinct stages that are worth knowing about. Pre-training is where the heavy lifting occurs. The model is exposed to vast quantities of text from across the internet, essentially compressing human knowledge into its parameters. One way to think about this is as a kind of compression algorithm, like a zip file containing the summarised essence of billions of documents. This is why models have a knowledge cutoff date. They know what existed in their training data up to a certain point, but nothing beyond that. The second stage, called post-training, is where the model learns to behave as an assistant. It shapes the personality, the communication style, and the tendency to be helpful rather than simply regurgitating information. Understanding this two-stage process helps explain why a model might give you a beautifully written response about historical events while being completely wrong about something that happened last week.

Moving on to practical usage, there are some guidelines that can make a real difference to your experience. Language models excel at knowledge-based queries where the information is well-established and unlikely to change rapidly. Asking about the caffeine content of different coffee drinks, the side effects of common medications, or the key arguments in a philosophical text are all reasonable queries. But here's where it gets important. When accuracy genuinely matters, you should verify the information against primary sources rather than taking the model's word for it. These systems can and do hallucinate, which is a polite way of saying they confidently produce incorrect information. This is especially true on free tiers, which often run smaller models that are more prone to making things up. It pays to be aware of which model tier you're using and to calibrate your trust accordingly. Starting a new conversation when you switch topics isn't just a suggestion to keep things organised. It actually helps maintain performance by preventing token bloat, where the context window fills up with irrelevant earlier conversation, leaving less room for the actual task at hand.

One of the most significant developments in recent times has been the emergence of thinking or reasoning models. These are models trained using reinforcement learning techniques that allow them to exhibit more human-like problem-solving approaches. OpenAI's O1 and O3 series are prominent examples. Rather than immediately producing an answer, these models actually work through problems step by step, sometimes taking several minutes to arrive at a response. The payoff is substantially higher accuracy on difficult problems, particularly in domains like mathematics, coding, and complex logical reasoning. If you've ever been frustrated by a language model giving you wrong answers to a tricky maths problem, reasoning models are the answer. The practical tip here is to start with non-thinking models for speed on straightforward tasks, and only switch to thinking models when you hit a problem that genuinely requires more careful analysis. It's a bit like using a calculator for simple arithmetic but reaching for pen and paper when the problem gets complicated.

Tool use has become increasingly sophisticated and opens up possibilities that go far beyond simple text generation. Internet search integration allows models to access recent information, which is built into ChatGPT's search button and available in dedicated tools like Perplexity. This is brilliant for recent news, schedule information, niche topics that might not have made it into training data, or rapidly changing information where the knowledge cutoff would otherwise leave you stuck. Then there's the Python interpreter capability, which enables calculations, data analysis, and even visualisation. Imagine being able to ask a model to analyse a dataset you provide, run statistical tests, and generate charts, all within the same conversation where you're discussing the findings. At the more powerful end, there's Deep Research, which combines thinking processes with search capabilities to produce comprehensive, multi-source reports. This is available on ChatGPT Pro at two hundred dollars per month, so it's not for everyone, but if you regularly need thorough research summaries, it can be genuinely valuable.

Document and file analysis is another area where these tools shine. You can upload PDFs, books, and other documents and then engage in question and answer, summaries, or explanations about the content. This works beautifully for reading academic papers where the dense technical language makes comprehension difficult, or historical texts where context matters. Picture being able to work through Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations with an AI assistant that can explain the economic concepts, put them in historical context, and answer questions as they arise. Models like Claude 3.7 have particularly strong support for large file uploads and extended thinking, making them excellent choices for this kind of work.

For those interested in coding and application development, the capabilities have become remarkably powerful. ChatGPT's Advanced Data Analysis feature can generate figures, fit trendlines, and run code. Claude Artifacts creates interactive React applications, diagrams in Mermaid format, and flashcards, all inline within the conversation. For professional developers, tools like Cursor and Composer offer IDE integration that brings AI assistance directly into the coding environment, a practice sometimes called vibe coding. The caveat here is that not all language models have strong programming tools built in, and those that don't may confidently produce code that looks plausible but doesn't actually work. Checking whether your chosen platform has proper coding capabilities before diving into a development task will save you considerable frustration.

Multimodal capabilities have expanded what we can do with these models beyond text alone. Voice input and output have been available for a while, essentially providing speech-to-text and text-to-speech for easier interaction. But Advanced Voice Mode, available in ChatGPT and Grok, takes this further by processing audio directly without converting it to text as an intermediary step first. The difference in naturalness is quite striking. Image input allows you to upload photos for optical character recognition, analysis of nutrition labels, interpretation of blood test results, or even analysis of internet memes. Image generation through DALL-E 3 in ChatGPT enables creation of images from text prompts, which has obvious applications for content creation, design brainstorming, and illustration. On the mobile side, camera streaming allows real-time visual question and answer, which opens up possibilities for accessibility tools, shopping assistance, or educational applications where pointing your phone at something and asking questions feels natural.

Personalisation features help these tools fit into your life more smoothly. ChatGPT's Memory function allows it to remember preferences across conversations, so you can simply tell it to remember something and it'll be available in future sessions without re-explaining. Custom Instructions modify how ChatGPT speaks and behaves globally, so you can set persistent preferences for tone, format, or expertise area. Custom GPTs take this further by saving reusable prompts for specific tasks. You might create a language learning translator that always provides example sentences in a particular language, or a vocabulary extractor that pulls out unfamiliar words from texts you upload. These features transform the AI from a generic tool into something that can feel genuinely personalised to your workflow.

Looking at the key takeaways from all of this, a few things stand out. The LLM ecosystem is evolving at a tremendous pace, with feature parity varying significantly across providers. What I've described today may have already shifted by the time you listen to this. ChatGPT remains the most feature-rich default option, but other apps genuinely excel in specific areas. Perplexity is strong for search-focused tasks. Claude has particular strengths in coding and artifact creation. The smart approach is to combine different tools based on task requirements rather than trying to force everything through a single platform. Finally, treat LLM outputs as first drafts that need verification, particularly when you're making decisions with real consequences. These tools are extraordinarily capable, but they're not infallible, and healthy scepticism combined with practical familiarity will serve you far better than either blind trust or blanket dismissal.
