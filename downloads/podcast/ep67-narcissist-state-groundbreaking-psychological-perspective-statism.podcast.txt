What if the most powerful force shaping our politics isn't ideology at all, but something far more intimate, something we usually associate with dysfunctional families rather than governments? A UK-qualified barrister, IT developer, and former standup comedian named Philip Bodessell, who spent time in prison for refusing to wear a COVID mask, has developed a framework that does exactly this. He calls it "The Narcissist State," and it's available at thenarcissistate.net. The idea is striking in its simplicity: Bodessell argues that the State doesn't just act like a bully or a thief, which libertarians have long argued, but that it operates using the precise psychological dynamics of narcissistic abuse. And once you see it, he believes, you cannot unsee it.

This isn't a conspiracy theory in the traditional sense. Bodessell is at pains to emphasise that he's not suggesting a secret cabal of individual narcissists has hijacked the machinery of government. Instead, he's proposing something more unsettling: the system itself generates these toxic dynamics naturally, the same way a family system produces roles like the Golden Child, the Enabler, and the Scapegoat. He maps these familiar patterns onto political structures. The Golden Child becomes the state's favourite industries or institutions, lavished with subsidies and protection. The Enabler is the compliant citizen who normalises abuse, who says things like "the government has to do something" every time there's a crisis. And the Scapegoat? That's anyone deemed too dangerous to ignore but too inconvenient to solve: the whistleblower, the dissident, the troublemaker who must be discredited and silenced.

Alongside these roles, Bodessell identifies mechanisms that Narcissistic Personality Disorder produces in families and argues they appear, almost identically, in statecraft. There's Gaslighting, of course, which takes its name from the 1944 film where a husband manipulates his wife into doubting her own sanity. But Bodessell illustrates this with something far more contemporary. Think of the economic messaging you've encountered over the past few years. Headlines declare that inflation is low, that everything is under control, while at the same time the price of eggs has doubled and your weekly shop feels like a minor heist. This creates a profound disorientation in the population. The lived reality of ordinary people contradicts the official narrative, and rather than trusting their own perceptions, people are pushed toward external authorities to tell them what's real. That's Gaslighting in action, and it's not accidental. It's structural.

There's also DARVO, an acronym that stands for Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender. When someone in a family system points out the abuse, the abuser doesn't engage with the criticism. Instead, they deny it, attack the person making the accusation, and somehow end up positioning themselves as the real victim. Bodessell argues this is precisely what happens when states face accountability. Criticism is framed as hostility, whistleblowers are prosecuted, and the machinery of power presents itself as the injured party deserving protection. And then there's Intermittent Reinforcement, the psychological mechanism where rewards and punishments arrive unpredictably, keeping the subject in a state of anxious attachment and dependence. The state rewards compliance with stability, punishes defiance with hardship, and does so inconsistently enough that no one can ever feel truly secure. This is how you keep a population compliant without ever having to explain yourself.

But Bodessell's framework isn't just a diagnosis. The primary goal, he says, is to provide people with what he calls a compass and a map. When you can recognise these toxic patterns, when you can spot a coworker acting as an Enabler or see the Scapegoating mechanism at work in a media campaign, something shifts. You stop wasting energy trying to win arguments with people who are structurally incapable of acknowledging the harm they cause. You stop engaging with debates that were designed to be unwinnable. And this reduction in mental clutter and stress is not trivial. Bodessell argues that this saved energy can then be redirected toward concrete exit strategies: enhancing your digital privacy, researching alternative financial systems like cryptocurrency, exploring relocation to jurisdictions that align more closely with your values. These are practical steps, not abstract philosophical arguments, and that's the point. The framework is meant to be functional, not merely intellectual.

Bodessell deliberately wrote The Narcissist State as what he describes as a modern pamphlet, drawing explicit inspiration from Thomas Paine's Common Sense. He wanted something short, digestible, and accessible, something that could spread in the way pamphlets once did, before the internet was cluttered with think pieces and lengthy treatises. He builds on existing libertarian scholarship, on thinkers like Michael Huemer and Larken Rose, but argues that what was missing from their work was the psychological layer. They've dismantled the ethical arguments for state authority, but Bodessell wants to explain why people keep accepting it anyway, why they keep returning to the abuser, so to speak, even when they know the relationship is destructive. The answer, he suggests, lies in the emotional architecture of narcissism itself.

At the heart of Bodessell's argument is a hidden truth that he believes the state's ultimate vulnerability: its reliance on the myth of political authority and the underlying threat of violence. Every law ultimately rests on the implicit promise that force will be used against those who disobey. The narcissistic dynamic is designed to obscure this, to make the violence invisible, to frame the state's coercion as care, protection, or necessity. But once you see through it, the whole edifice looks different. It's not an impenetrable fortress; it's a fragile construction built on shared delusions.

Bodessell has faced resistance, though perhaps not the kind he expected. His framework was actually banned on major libertarian subreddits, including communities like GoldandBlack and RonPaul, because he was transparent about using AI in the writing process. This is a revealing obstacle. Even within communities built around radical scepticism toward authority, there are gatekeepers, orthodoxies, and mechanisms for enforcing conformity. It suggests that dissemination of new ideas is never straightforward, that the very people most likely to appreciate a critique of state power are still capable of replicating the Enabler role within their own circles.

What Bodessell offers, ultimately, is a lens. Whether you find it convincing or not, whether you agree with his mapping of family dynamics onto political structures, the value lies in the practice of looking at power through a psychological eye. We spend so much time arguing about policies and personalities. Perhaps what's been missing is an understanding of the emotional machinery that makes it all possible.
