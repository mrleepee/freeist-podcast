Did you know that most people trying to improve their A I agents are going about it completely wrong? They're spending hours rewording prompts when the real problem is often something far more fundamental.

Anthropic recently published what they're calling their Agent Evaluation Playbook, and it fundamentally challenges how we think about building reliable A I systems. Rather than chasing better prompts, they argue that systematic evaluation frameworks are the actual secret to getting agents to behave consistently. This is not just theoretical advice either — they've released a practical guide showing exactly how to build, test, and debug agent workflows using structured evaluations.

The core idea is elegantly simple: without evaluations, you have no way of knowing whether a change you made actually improved your agent or made things worse. An evaluation suite is essentially a collection of test cases that run automatically against your agent, giving you measurable insights into its performance. It is like having a quality control system that never sleeps.

These test cases come in three distinct varieties. First, you have what they call control cases — the baseline scenarios that must always work. These are the simple, straightforward tasks that form the foundation of your agent's reliability. If your agent cannot handle these basic interactions, everything else becomes irrelevant. Second, there are edge cases — the tricky scenarios where the model has previously failed and needs explicit, targeted instructions to handle correctly. These are not random failures but specific situations discovered through testing and iteration. Third, and perhaps most overlooked, are capability boundaries — knowing precisely when your agent should hand off to a human or politely decline a request. An agent that tries to do absolutely everything will inevitably stumble on the harder parts.

To make this concrete, Anthropic walks through a demonstration involving a telecom customer support bot. This fictional Meridian Mobile support agent has five distinct test cases, each targeting a different capability. There's a control case for straightforward account queries, a scenario testing pro-rata calculations for mid-month plan changes, policy adherence checks, escalation procedures for billing errors, and handling of hotspot data questions. What becomes clear from this example is that each test case is carefully designed to probe a specific slice of the agent's competence.

One of the most counterintuitive insights from the playbook is that prompt structure matters far more than prompt length. Anthropic recommends using clear structural markers, like X M L tags, to visually separate the agent's role, guidelines, policy, and tone. Their point is striking: if you cannot visually distinguish your guidelines from your policy from your data when reading your own prompt, then the model cannot either. Making this hierarchy explicit transforms how well the agent follows instructions.

The debugging workflow they propose is refreshingly methodical. Run your evaluations, identify the specific failure mode, target one failure at a time, fix it, then re-run the evaluations. The temptation is to try fixing everything at once, but that rarely works. By isolating problems and addressing them sequentially, you build a more robust agent incrementally.

Beyond this framework, Anthropic highlights practical improvements that make the biggest difference in real-world implementations. Remove irrelevant information from prompts — dead references and outdated instructions just muddy the waters. Create output contracts that specify exactly what format responses should take. Use stop sequences in A P I calls to prevent models from generating unnecessary text. For complex response requirements, structured outputs like J S O N schemas keep everything organised and predictable.

Here is the crucial insight that ties everything together: most prompt failures are structural, not logical. The natural instinct when something goes wrong is to rewrite the instructions. But often, simply reorganising the prompt so the model can clearly distinguish instructions from context from data produces far bigger gains than rewriting the instructions themselves.

So the next time an agent fails spectacularly, resist the urge to immediately write new instructions. Instead, take a step back and ask whether the hierarchy of your prompt is clear. Sometimes the path to a dramatically better agent is not a better prompt, but a better structured one.
