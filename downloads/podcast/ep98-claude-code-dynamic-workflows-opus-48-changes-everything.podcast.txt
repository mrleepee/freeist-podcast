What if I told you that an entire programming runtime, nearly three quarters of a million lines of code, was recently rewritten from one language to another in just eleven days? And not by a team of dozens of engineers grinding through nights and weekends, but largely by artificial intelligence working through the night in parallel. That is exactly what happened when Jarred Sumner, the creator of the Bun runtime, used a brand new feature in Claude Code to port the entire project from Zig to Rust. Almost eight hundred thousand lines of code, ninety-nine point eight percent of the test suite passing, and the whole thing merged in less than a fortnight. If that does not make you sit up and pay attention to what Anthropic has just released, I am not sure what will.

Anthropic dropped Opus version four point eight this week, and the headline feature is called dynamic workflows. Now, that name might sound a bit dry, but stick with me because this genuinely represents a fundamental shift in how A I coding assistants can tackle complex engineering tasks. Until now, even the most capable A I coding tools have operated in a fairly linear fashion. You give them a job, they work through it step by step, and you get a result. Dynamic workflows flip that on its head. When you trigger one, Claude becomes an orchestra conductor. It analyses your prompt, breaks the problem down into dozens or even hundreds of independent subtasks, fans those tasks out to parallel subagents working simultaneously, and then collects, verifies, and synthesises everything into a single coherent answer. Crucially, the system includes adversarial checking. One set of agents produces the work, another set tries to tear it apart and find the holes, and the whole run keeps iterating until the answers converge. It is like having a code review and testing process built in, but running at a speed humans simply cannot match.

There is also a clever new setting called ultracode hiding in the effort menu. When you enable it, Claude automatically decides whether a task warrants the full dynamic workflow treatment or whether something simpler will do. This means developers do not have to constantly second-guess whether they should be triggering workflows manually. The A I recognises when a problem is complex enough to benefit from parallel processing and acts accordingly. For most developers on the Max or Team plans, dynamic workflows are switched on by default, so you will likely start seeing them appear without any extra effort on your part.

Let us talk more about that Bun rewrite because it really is the most staggering proof of concept. The workflow mapped the correct Rust lifetime for every single struct field across the entire Zig codebase. Then, with hundreds of agents working in parallel and two reviewers assigned to each file, it wrote every Rust source file as a behaviour-identical port of its Zig counterpart. A fix loop then ran the build and test suite repeatedly until both were clean. After the initial port landed, a follow-up workflow ran overnight to identify and fix unnecessary data copies, opening pull requests for each optimisation for human review. This was not some toy demonstration with a few files. This was a production-scale runtime being ported between two systems-level programming languages, almost entirely by A I, in under two weeks.

The practical applications Anthropic is highlighting fall into three main categories. First, comprehensive bug hunts, profiler-guided performance audits, and security reviews. Claude can search a codebase in parallel across hundreds of subagents, verify every finding independently, and surface only genuine issues. Second, large-scale migrations and modernisation work. Framework upgrades, A P I deprecations, language ports spanning thousands of files, all handled end to end. Third, and perhaps most critically, high-stakes work where a wrong answer is costly. When accuracy really matters, dynamic workflows give Claude multiple independent attempts at the problem with agents actively working to break the results before they reach you.

Early access users have already been putting this through its paces. Klarna found dynamic workflows particularly valuable for discovery and review tasks across large codebases, identifying dead code and cleanup opportunities that traditional static analysis tools missed entirely. CyberAgent noted that workflows fill the gap between sending off a single subagent and building out a full custom agent team, giving developers the best of both worlds without the overhead.

Of course, there is a trade-off to consider. Dynamic workflows consume substantially more tokens than a typical session, and Anthropic is refreshingly transparent about that. The first time a workflow triggers, Claude Code shows you exactly what is about to run and asks for confirmation. Organisation administrators can also disable workflows entirely through managed settings if they want tighter control over usage.

What Anthropic seems to be saying with this release is that the future of A I-assisted development is not really about smarter autocomplete or better chat responses. It is about A I systems that can plan, execute, verify, and deliver genuinely complex engineering work at scale. The Bun rewrite proves this is not theoretical speculation. It is happening right now, and it is going to change how every developer thinks about what is possible with A I tooling.
