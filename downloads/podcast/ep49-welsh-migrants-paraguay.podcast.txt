In this episode you'll learn three things: why a group of Welsh people crossed the Atlantic to settle in the middle of South America, how their colony in Paraguay survived against all odds, and what remains of Welsh culture in Paraguay today.

The story begins in the nineteenth century. Wales was under enormous pressure. The English language was displacing Welsh in schools and public life. The land was poor, industry was harsh, and the Welsh Nonconformist chapel culture felt under siege. A movement emerged calling for a new Wales — a place overseas where the Welsh language, religion, and way of life could survive free from English domination.

Some went to Patagonia in Argentina in 1865, and that's the better-known story. But a smaller, equally remarkable group chose Paraguay.

In 1881, a Welsh settler named John Evans — or Ieuan ap Evan Jones to give him his Welsh name — led a group to Paraguay. The Paraguayan government, desperate to repopulate the country after the devastating War of the Triple Alliance, was offering free land, tax exemptions, and freedom of religion to any immigrants willing to settle. For a group of Welsh Nonconformists looking for a fresh start, this was appealing.

They established a settlement called Nueva Wales, or New Wales, in the region of Villa Rica, in what is now the Guairá department of Paraguay. The land was fertile, the climate was subtropical, and the Paraguayan government kept its promises. The Welsh settlers built homes, established farms, and set up their chapels.

Life was not easy. The tropical climate was brutal for people accustomed to the cold hills of Wales. Diseases like malaria and yellow fever took their toll. The soil was different. The wildlife was unfamiliar. And they were isolated — very isolated — from the rest of the Welsh-speaking world.

But they adapted. They learned to grow crops suited to the climate. They raised cattle. They traded with their Guaraní-speaking neighbours, and over time, the two cultures began to influence each other. Some Welsh men married Guaraní women. Children grew up speaking Welsh at home and Guaraní in the street. It was an extraordinary cultural intersection.

The Welsh community in Paraguay was never large — perhaps a few hundred people at its peak. But it was determined. They maintained their chapel services in Welsh. They taught their children to read and write in Welsh using the Bible and hymnals they had brought with them. They kept the Eisteddfod tradition alive — the Welsh festival of literature, music, and performance — even in the Paraguayan heat.

Over the decades, though, assimilation was inevitable. Spanish and Guaraní became the languages of business and daily life outside the home. Welsh-speaking children grew up and moved to Asunción for work. Intermarriage with the local population continued. By the mid-twentieth century, the community had largely blended into Paraguayan society.

But traces remain. There are Paraguayan families with Welsh surnames — Evans, Jones, Davies, Hughes — who may not speak a word of Welsh but know their ancestry came from across the ocean. There are architectural traces in older buildings. And there are stories, passed down through generations, about the strange decision their ancestors made to leave the green valleys of Wales for the red earth of Paraguay.

The Welsh-Paraguayan connection occasionally resurfaces. In recent years, Welsh cultural organisations have visited Paraguay to explore the history. There have been academic studies and documentaries. The Welsh government has shown interest in the diaspora story as part of its broader effort to connect with Welsh communities worldwide.

What makes this story compelling is what it says about human determination. A small group of people, driven by the desire to preserve their language and faith, crossed an ocean and settled in a country that had nearly been wiped off the map a decade earlier. Two wounded cultures — Wales, stripped of its autonomy, and Paraguay, stripped of its population — found each other and built something new.

The Welsh in Patagonia kept their language alive for generations. The Welsh in Paraguay took a different path — they melted into the Guaraní-speaking nation, leaving behind surnames, stories, and the faintest echo of chapel hymns sung in Welsh under a South American sun.

Today we learned that the story of the Welsh in Paraguay is really a story about what happens when cultures collide, adapt, and eventually merge. It's not a story of loss. It's a story of transformation — two small nations finding each other in the most unexpected corner of the world.
