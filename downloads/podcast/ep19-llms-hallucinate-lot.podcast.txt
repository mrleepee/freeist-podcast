One of the most alarming trends in modern software development is that Large Language Models, the very tools we rely on to generate code and prose, have a habit of making things up. The phenomenon is known as hallucination, and it is far more common than most users realise. Even seasoned developers who have spent years tuning pipelines and writing clean architecture now find themselves double‑checking the output of an AI that sounds perfectly confident. In practice, that confidence is often misplaced, and the consequences can range from a minor inconvenience to a serious breach of trust.

The problem is not a one‑off glitch; it is systemic. A recent survey of engineers working on production AI products revealed that the majority had been caught out by a hallucinated fact at least once a month. The issue has become so prevalent that teams have started treating every response from an LLM as a draft that must be verified before it reaches a user. This shift in mindset is a clear signal that the industry is waking up to a fundamental limitation of the technology.

Hallucinations come in several distinct flavours, each with its own pitfalls. First, there are outright factual errors, where the model asserts something that is simply wrong. A well‑known example is Google’s early BARD chatbot, which confidently declared that the James Webb Space Telescope had taken the first pictures of exoplanets—a claim that never occurred. Second, we see intrinsic hallucinations, where the model contradicts information that has been provided within the same conversation. For instance, a developer might paste a piece of code and ask the model to explain why it crashes, only to receive an explanation that ignores the code entirely. Third, there are extrinsic hallucinations, where the model invents details that were never part of the dialogue, such as non‑existent software packages, fictitious legal statutes, or invented government departments. Fourth, there is contextual inconsistency, a particularly sneaky variant that occurs when the model ignores or misrepresents explicitly supplied context. The infamous Air Canada chatbot case illustrates this perfectly: the bot invented a refund policy that did not exist, leading to a public relations nightmare for the airline.

Why does this happen? At its core, training an LLM is an exercise in compression. The model ingests an enormous corpus of text and squeezes it into a much smaller set of parameters. In that process, nuances and exact facts can be lost, leaving the model with a fuzzy picture that it then tries to fill in with plausible-sounding guesses. Moreover, the benchmarks used to evaluate these models reward correct answers far more than they penalise confident mistakes. A model that admits it does not know something often scores lower than one that fabricates an answer, so developers have an incentive to push the system toward bold, even reckless, responses. The pressure to top leaderboards adds another layer: “you miss all the shots you don’t take” becomes a corporate mantra, encouraging teams to chase higher scores at the expense of reliability.

The good news is that there are practical strategies to mitigate the risk without abandoning these powerful tools altogether. The most effective approach is to treat the model as a collaborator that works best when you supply its own context. Instead of asking it to recall facts from its training data, provide the relevant documents, code snippets, or data files directly. This turns the model into an analyst of supplied material rather than a fallible memory. When you need up‑to‑date information, explicitly instruct the model to use its search tool and to cite sources, for example by saying, “Use your search tool and give me a link.” For any critical decision—legal, health, financial, or safety‑related—always cross‑verify the output with independent sources. A simple sanity check can prevent a costly mistake.

In the end, LLMs remain incredibly valuable assistants for tasks such as drafting code, summarising documents, and brainstorming ideas. They excel when they can work on material you provide and when you guide them with clear, constrained prompts. The key is to never trust them implicitly as authoritative knowledge sources. Think of them as a colleague who is brilliant at pattern matching but occasionally confabulates when the data is thin. With that perspective, you can harness their strengths while keeping the hallucinations in check.
