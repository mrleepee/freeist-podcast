Did you know London's oldest food market has been operating for a thousand years? That's not a misprint. While we might associate Borough Market with artisanal cheeses and sourdough loaves, this Southwark institution has witnessed Roman soldiers crossing the Thames, medieval pilgrims trudging towards Canterbury, Shakespeare's audiences spilling out of the Globe, and Victorian trains thundering overhead on a railway viaduct built straight through the middle of the trading halls. And today, it draws thousands of visitors every week who come to eat, browse, and soak up an atmosphere unlike anywhere else on earth. What makes this story remarkable isn't just the market's longevity, but the fact that it very nearly vanished entirely in the 1980s. Borough Market's survival is a tale of near-death, resurrection, and the enduring human appetite for really good food.

The story begins with a bridge. The Romans founded Londinium on the north bank of the Thames around 43 AD and soon constructed the first crossing to connect their new city with the road network heading south. Archaeological excavations at Borough Market in 1961 uncovered Roman artefacts, including evidence of a land surface from the 1st century sitting roughly five feet above current sea levels. That bridge made this spot a natural trading point. Farmers, merchants, and travellers crossing the Thames would stop, buy provisions, and sell goods. The instinct to trade here is quite literally as old as London itself.

By the 12th century, London Bridge had become a heaving municipality in its own right, crammed with houses and shops balanced on stilts over the river. The road leading to it, now Borough High Street, was the main artery into the City from the entire south of England. When Thomas Becket was murdered in Canterbury in 1170, that road became the route thousands of pilgrims took each year. Where there are travellers, there are people wanting to sell them things. Two markets eventually operated in the area. A smaller corn market operated from St Thomas' Hospital grounds near the bridge's southern entrance, while a larger one sold a wider range of produce near the foot of the bridge under the Guildable Manor. The City of London authorities were furious, seeing these markets as dangerous competition. In the 1270s, they forbade their citizens from crossing the river to buy goods in Southwark. But the market persisted. Henry IV granted formal recognition in 1406, giving London the right to regulate trade and inspect goods. Edward VI sold Southwark to the City outright in 1550 for just over 1,000 pounds, and the market became an extension of London itself.

The Southwark of Shakespeare's time was London's entertainment district, packed with coaching inns, alehouses, theatres, and brothels. The Globe stood nearby. The market was chaos, regulated only imperfectly by bailiffs who enforced price controls and collected fees. Traders had to set up in a fixed sequence along the drainage channel, with fish sellers closest to the bridge, followed by butchers, poulterers, bakers, and more. Butchers were a particular handful, driving live oxen through the streets. One regulation complained that animals were "so wild, that they will not enter but run away."

By the mid-18th century, the congestion on Borough High Street had become unbearable. The market, with its stalls and wandering livestock, was blocking the only southern route into the City. In 1754, Parliament declared that "the market obstructs much trade and commerce" and ordered it to cease by March 1756. Local residents petitioned for a new market away from the high street, raising 6,000 pounds by selling annuities to buy an area called The Triangle. Borough Market as we now know it was born, and it remains a charitable trust run by volunteer trustees to this day.

The 19th century transformed the market into an institution of national significance. South London's population exploded, and the arrival of the railway made Borough perfectly positioned as a wholesale hub. The distinctive iron-and-glass buildings were designed by Henry Rose in 1851, giving the market its Gothic architectural character. Then, in 1862, the South Eastern Railway Company constructed a viaduct straight through the middle of the market to connect London Bridge station with new stations at Cannon Street and Charing Cross. This extraordinary arrangement, a railway bridge suspended above a working food market, gave Borough its most distinctive feature. The trustees negotiated cleverly, securing compensation each time the railway was widened. By the early 20th century, Borough was handling 1.75 million bushels of fruit and vegetables annually, with hundreds of porters working through the night to feed millions of people across the southeast.

The decline was swift and brutal. New Covent Garden Market opened in Vauxhall in the 1970s, drawing wholesale business away. But the real killer was the supermarket chains, which destroyed independent greengrocers and the entire ecosystem in which fruit and vegetable wholesaling had thrived. By the 1980s and early 1990s, the magnificent Victorian buildings stood half-empty, iron frameworks rusting, glass panels cracked. There were serious discussions about demolition. The future looked genuinely bleak.

The rebirth began with the artisan food movement of the 1990s. Pioneering businesses including Neal's Yard Dairy, Brindisa, and Monmouth Coffee Company moved into empty warehouses, attracted by cheap rents. The turning point came in November 1998, when food writer Henrietta Green held a three-day Food Lovers' Fair, officially opened by the Two Fat Ladies. It was a roaring success. Traders sold out within hours. The trustees were persuaded that a specialist retail market could offer a brighter future. A regular market began monthly, soon became weekly, and now operates six days a week with over 100 stalls, restaurants, and shops offering everything from hand-made charcuterie and single-estate olive oil to fresh oysters and French-style patisserie. Neal's Yard Dairy became the world's most influential cheesemonger. Monmouth Coffee Company pioneered artisan roasting in Britain. Turnips remains a cornerstone, selling extraordinary produce from small growers.

The market's atmospheric architecture has made it a favourite film location. The Globe Tavern at its edge served as Bridget Jones's flat exterior. Borough Market and its surrounding streets were used extensively in Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban, said to have inspired Diagon Alley. Lock, Stock and Two Smoking Barrels filmed there too.

Today, the smells hit you first: roasting meats, fresh bread, ripe cheese, coffee grinding, the mineral tang of oysters. Then the sounds: the clatter of pans, the calls of traders, the rumble of trains overhead, the polyglot chatter of visitors from every continent. Then the sheer visual abundance: pyramids of perfect fruit, whole legs of jamon iberico hanging from hooks, wheels of cheese the size of wagon wheels.

The market survived the 2017 London Bridge attack, closing for eleven days before reopening with defiance. It operates as a charitable trust, runs educational programmes, and has become a powerful voice for sustainable food production. In 2023, it celebrated its official thousandth year. From Roman merchants haggling over salted fish to Instagram influencers photographing artisan doughnuts, Borough Market has been continuously reinventing itself for a millennium. It is, in the most literal sense, a thousand years of London on a plate.
