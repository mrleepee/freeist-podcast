Did you know that the biggest reason A I prompts fail isn't because the model isn't clever enough, but because the prompt itself is an absolute shambles? It's true. Most people spend ages agonising over which model to use while their prompt reads like a ransom note cobbled together from six different documents. That changes today.

Prompting is one of those skills that separates genuinely useful A I systems from expensive disappointments. Whether you're migrating prompts to a new model or building agent workflows from scratch, getting the prompting right matters enormously. And here's the thing — you can't just wing it and hope for the best. You need something called evaluations, or evals as the cool kids say. These are structured tests that tell you, quite rigorously, whether your prompt changes actually make things better or worse. Without evaluations, you're essentially flying blind.

Now, not all evaluations are created equal. There are three types worth knowing about. First, control cases — these are your absolute basics that should always pass. If your A I support bot can't handle "What's my account number?" every single time, you've got bigger problems. Second, edge cases — the gnarly bits where the model has previously stumbled and needs explicit, hand-holding instructions to get right. And third, capability boundaries — knowing when to gracefully hand things over to a human or simply say no. That's harder than it sounds.

Let me paint you a picture with a real example. Imagine a customer support bot for a mobile company called Meridian Mobile. Their evaluation suite has five test cases covering everything from straightforward account queries to pro-ration calculations for mid-month plan changes, policy adherence, escalating billing errors, and handling hotspot data questions. Each test case targets a different part of the bot's brain. Get this right and you've got a genuinely helpful assistant. Get it wrong and you're essentially building a very expensive way to annoy your customers.

So what does good prompting actually look like? Well, structure is your best friend. Using X M L tags to separate out the role, the guidelines, the policy, and the tone keeps everything crystal clear. Strip out anything that isn't relevant — that reference to the old website banner nobody cares about is just noise. Output contracts are brilliant for keeping things consistent, and stop sequences in your A P I calls ensure the model knows when to shut up and stop generating waffle.

Here's the bit that really stuck with me: if you can't tell the difference between guidelines, policy, and data when you're reading your own prompt, then guess what? The model can't either. That's the fundamental truth underlying everything else. Structure your prompt like you'd structure an instruction manual for a slightly confused but intelligent colleague.

The debugging process follows a satisfying rhythm. Run your evaluations, spot where things are falling apart, pick one failure at a time, and fix it systematically. Structured outputs — J S O N schemas and the like — can help enormously when you need consistent, predictable responses from complex interactions.

The beautiful irony is that often you don't need to rewrite your entire prompt logic. Simply cleaning up the structure and making things clearer can dramatically improve how the model performs. Sometimes less really is more.
