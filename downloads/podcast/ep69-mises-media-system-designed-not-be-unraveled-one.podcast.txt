What if I told you that the most expensive congressional primary in American history just happened in Kentucky — and the incumbent who lost was not undone by scandal, corruption, or even a policy disagreement? Thomas Massie, a congressman who built his reputation on civil liberties and fiscal conservatism, was defeated by a first-time candidate who barely ran a campaign. Ed Geller won despite spending an astronomical amount of money while campaigning almost nothing, and the whole episode reveals something quite troubling about how the Republican Party operates today.

The key factors here were Eh! eye! pay! ack! funding, Trump's opposition, and Massie's vote against the so-called big beautiful bill. Trump's vendetta against Massie was sparked by that single vote, and once the former president decided to target someone, the machinery swings into action. Eh! eye! pay! ack! poured resources into the race, and suddenly Geller — who hardly seemed interested in actually talking to voters — had more money than he knew what to do with. It's a stark reminder that in American politics today, ideology can take a back seat when enough outside money decides someone needs to go.

The generational divide in that district was fascinating. Younger voters, the ones getting their news from podcasts and independent media rather than traditional outlets, went heavily for Massie. Meanwhile, older Fox News viewers backed Geller. Some commentators claimed this proves Em! ay! gee! ay! dominance, but that interpretation seems wide of the mark. The demographics actually favour Massie's side of the argument over time, even though he lost the vote. It's a case where winning the narrative might matter more than winning the seat.

When we think about what libertarian congressmen can actually achieve, Ron Paul's story comes to mind. He used his committee positions to bring obscure monetary policy debates into the mainstream conversation. That strategy worked because he found leverage within the system. Massie never had the same leverage, and it shows how difficult it is to shift anything from the inside when you lack those structural advantages.

The truth about Congress is that it runs on interest groups, earmarks, and transactional relationships. You scratch my back, I scratch yours. Libertarians face a particular problem because they fundamentally object to the entire arrangement. They're not trying to negotiate better terms within the system — they want to dismantle the system itself. That makes the usual political horse-trading almost impossible.

Perhaps the answer lies not in championing individual liberty-focused politicians but in building networks of special interest groups dedicated to specific liberty-oriented causes. Working at the state level on concrete issues — whether that's Defend the Guard campaigns or eliminating taxes on gold and silver — might achieve more tangible results. There's also a counter-intuitive argument that being out of power could actually serve liberty-minded people well as the broader political situation deteriorates. The Mises Institute continues its work on ideas and scholarship while others pursue direct political action, and that division of labour might be exactly what's needed.

Massie's defeat, following Justin Amash's earlier departure, marks the end of the Ron Paul era's presence in Congress. The Koch network and those libertarian organisations from the early twenty-tens are now completely defunct, which is a significant loss. What remains is smaller but perhaps more resilient. The movement needs both intellectual foundations and practical political engagement, and the key is ensuring these efforts support rather than undermine each other. The Republican Party's base is drifting toward libertarian positions even as the party machinery works against individual champions, which suggests the ideas are winning even when the elections are not.
