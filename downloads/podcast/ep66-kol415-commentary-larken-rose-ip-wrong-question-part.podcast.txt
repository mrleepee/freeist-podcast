Imagine someone spent years making a film, only to find it appearing on torrent sites the same day it launched. You'd expect them to be furious, right? But what if that person also claims copyright law is unjust and intellectual property shouldn't exist? That's exactly the situation Stefan Kinsella found himself unpacking when he sat down to analyse Larkin Rose's thirty-five minute video on intellectual property. The irony, it turns out, runs rather deep.

Rose, who's perhaps best known for his libertarian film Jones Plantation, has built quite a reputation for arguing against intellectual property rights. He maintains that copyright and patents are fundamentally coercive. Yet when copies of his movie started circulating without his permission, he didn't simply shrug and let it happen. Instead, he issued dee! em! see! eh! takedown notices to have those pirated copies removed from the internet. Kinsella found this genuinely fascinating, partly because the two had already crossed paths on a podcast where Rose dismissed pirates as simply being jerks, regardless of whether copyright law itself is just. Kinsella thought that characterisation deserved serious scrutiny, and so he set about examining Rose's arguments more carefully.

The heart of the disagreement comes down to what we actually mean when we talk about owning things. Rose has suggested that self-ownership extends to one's time, effort, and creations. Kinsella disagrees quite strongly here. Self-ownership, he argues, means owning your body. That's it. Your body, and by extension your physical actions. But labour is not a thing you can own. Labour is something you do. You can't own the act of writing or filming or composing any more than you can own the wind. And ideas themselves? They emerge from the mind, often influenced by countless other ideas that came before. Trying to claim ownership over a concept or a story feels rather like trying to claim ownership over a sunrise.

This connects directly to a broader point about what property rights actually are. Kinsella draws on classical libertarian property theory to make a crucial distinction. Property rights exist to resolve conflicts over scarce, rivalrous resources. Land is scarce. A bicycle is scarce. If I use a bicycle, you can't use it at the same time. That's what makes ownership meaningful in the first place. Information, however, works completely differently. When you copy a file, you don't remove it from the original holder. Both parties end up with the information. It can be duplicated infinitely without being depleted. In other words, information isn't conflictable. Two minds can possess the same idea simultaneously without any clash occurring.

According to this framework, the only legitimate ways to acquire property are through original appropriation, sometimes called homesteading unowned resources, or through voluntary contract with a previous owner. You can claim a piece of land nobody has touched before, or you can receive something from someone who legitimately owned it. But creation itself doesn't grant ownership. This is where Rose seems to go astray. He treats the act of making something as automatically conferring property rights over that thing, particularly when it's intangible. Kinsella sees this as a fundamental conceptual error that undermines much of Rose's position.

There's also the practical dimension of what happens once information escapes into the world. Kinsella points out that when a creator releases their work publicly, they're no longer in a contractual relationship with everyone who might eventually encounter it. A third party downloading a leaked file hasn't signed anything. They haven't agreed to pay. They've simply come across something that's now circulating freely. While the original creator might feel wronged, there's no actual contract being violated.

This critique extends to Rose's use of the dee! em! see! eh! takedown system itself. Here's someone who publicly opposes intellectual property, who argues copyright is unjust, who claims the state shouldn't be enforcing these monopolies on ideas. Yet when his own work gets copied, he turns around and uses that very same legal machinery to have it removed. Kinsella finds this difficult to defend. The takedown process only functions because copyright law exists. Terms of service on platforms only exist to protect those platforms from copyright liability. None of this represents free market principles at work. It's the opposite, in fact. It's leveraging state power to enforce a monopoly that Rose claims to oppose.

That said, Kinsella is careful not to suggest creators owe the world free access to everything they produce. He's not arguing anyone must give their work away. People have every right to charge what they like and restrict access however they see fit. His point is more nuanced. For libertarians especially, those who want their ideas to spread and influence society, treating intellectual property as sacred might actually work against their own goals. Restricting access to wealthy Western audiences while people in developing countries can't afford to pay seems somewhat counterproductive if your aim is actually to change minds. There's evidence, Kinsella notes, that making a pee! dee! eff! freely available doesn't destroy book sales and might even boost them.

Kinsella admits he's only scratched the surface here, having worked through about eight minutes of Rose's thirty-five minute video. He intends to return to the remaining material in future episodes, continuing to examine the philosophical foundations and practical implications of this debate. It's a reminder that intellectual property remains one of those areas where even people who share broad political commitments can disagree profoundly about fundamental principles.
