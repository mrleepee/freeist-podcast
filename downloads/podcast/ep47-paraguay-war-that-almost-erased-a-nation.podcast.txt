In this episode you'll learn three things: how Paraguay went from being South America's most prosperous nation to near extinction in a single war, why the War of the Triple Alliance remains the deadliest conflict in Latin American history, and how a second war decades later shaped the modern border.

In 1864, Paraguay was an anomaly in South America. Under the rule of Carlos Antonio López and then his son Francisco Solano López, the country had built its own railway, its own iron foundry, its own telegraph system, and one of the largest standing armies on the continent. It was self-sufficient, industrialising, and had zero foreign debt. By the standards of the era, Paraguay was doing well.

But Francisco Solano López made a fatal miscalculation. When Brazil intervened in Uruguay's civil war in 1864, López saw it as a threat to the regional balance of power and to Paraguay's access to the sea via the Río de la Plata. He sent his army through Argentine territory to attack Brazil — without Argentina's permission. Argentina responded by joining Brazil and Uruguay's new government in an alliance against Paraguay.

What followed was absolute catastrophe. The War of the Triple Alliance lasted from 1864 to 1870. It was fought on Paraguayan soil almost entirely. The allied forces — Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay — had vastly larger populations and resources. Paraguay fought ferociously but was slowly crushed.

The numbers are staggering. Before the war, Paraguay's population was estimated between four hundred and five hundred thousand people. By the end, it may have been as low as two hundred thousand — and only about twenty-eight thousand of those were men. Some estimates put male survival at just ten percent. The war didn't just defeat Paraguay. It hollowed it out.

López himself was killed in battle on the first of March 1870, at Cerro Corá, in the northeast of the country. He reportedly declared he would die with his homeland. He did. His last words were said to be "I die with my country." Whether that's romantic or delusional depends on your perspective, but the destruction was real.

After the war, Paraguay lost roughly forty percent of its territory to Brazil and Argentina. It was occupied by Brazilian troops for six years. The economy was destroyed. The population was devastated. The recovery would take generations.

The war's legacy is still debated. In Paraguay, López is a complex figure — some see him as a patriot who refused to surrender, others as a dictator whose ambition destroyed the country. Monuments to him exist alongside criticisms of his leadership. The war is taught in schools as both tragedy and defining moment of national identity.

Then there was the Chaco War, from 1932 to 1935. This time Paraguay fought Bolivia over the Gran Chaco, a vast, harsh, supposedly oil-rich plain in the west. Paraguay won, largely through superior tactics and knowledge of the terrain, and retained most of the Chaco region. It was a rare moment of military redemption after the devastation of the Triple Alliance.

But the Chaco War was also brutal. Thirty thousand Bolivians and roughly the same number of Paraguayans died, many from disease, heat, and thirst rather than combat. The Chaco is one of the most inhospitable environments on earth — temperatures above fifty degrees, almost no water, thick thorny vegetation. Soldiers on both sides suffered enormously.

These two wars explain much about modern Paraguay. The trauma of near-annihilation in the 1860s created a deep sense of national identity and resilience. The Chaco War reinforced that identity with a victory that mattered. Together they shaped a country that, despite its small size and difficult history, maintains a fierce sense of independence.

Today we learned that Paraguay's history is defined by survival against overwhelming odds. Two wars, one that nearly erased the nation and one that restored its pride, left scars that still shape the country's character today.
