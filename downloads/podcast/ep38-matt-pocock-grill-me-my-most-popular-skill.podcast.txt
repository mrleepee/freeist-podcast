In this episode you'll learn three things: how a popular AI prompting technique evolved into something even more powerful, why keeping a shared vocabulary transforms AI-assisted coding, and when to use which approach depending on your project. Let's dive in.

The story begins with a skill called "Grill Me." This prompt makes an LLM interview you relentlessly until you reach a shared understanding on whatever you're working on. Users genuinely love it, sending roughly five messages daily singing its praises, and most people find it saves them considerable time once the initial grilling phase is complete. The trouble is, the skill had some frustrating limitations. The model would often become unnecessarily verbose, repeating information you'd already provided. More annoyingly, domain-specific jargon caused problems – if your codebase contained terms like "standalone video," the AI simply didn't know what you meant, so it kept asking for clarifications again and again. And here's the real issue: there was nowhere to capture the shared language you were building together, meaning you'd re-explain concepts repeatedly across different sessions.

The solution came from an unexpected direction – Domain-Driven Design. The creator combined "Grill Me" with something called Ubiquitous Language, the practice of establishing a single, consistent vocabulary that developers, domain experts, and even code can all share. This hybrid became "Grill with Docs."

At its heart are two simple but effective tools. First, there's "context.md" – a lightweight markdown file sitting at your repository's root that stores your project's shared glossary. Think of it as a living document where you define entities, relationships, and business rules. The skill reads this file at the start of every session, updates it as terminology gets sharpened, and checks new statements against it to ensure consistency. For larger projects, you can maintain separate context files per bounded context. Second, there are Architectural Decision Records – markdown notes specifically for decisions that are non-obvious, surprising, or costly to reverse. These capture the reasoning behind choices that context.md alone can't hold.

The workflow is elegant. The skill reads your context.md to pull in existing definitions, then challenges any fuzzy language it spots, asking for clarification on ambiguous terms and suggesting concrete scenarios. It cross-references your definitions against the actual code and updates context.md on the fly. When it encounters a decision that involves a real trade-off or would be hard to undo, it creates an ADR to document that reasoning.

In a practical demonstration, the skill helped add a "pitch" entity to a video-course application. It recognised the already-defined "standalone video" term and immediately asked whether a pitch could have zero videos, whether status transitions should be free-form, and so on. Once agreed, it wrote precise definitions into context.md – things like "Pitch to Standalone Video equals one-to-many" and "Pitch status equals idle, scheduled, or shipped."

The benefits are substantial. Replies become far more concise because the AI can rely on the shared glossary rather than re-establishing context each time. Its reasoning becomes shorter and more aligned with what you're actually trying to achieve. Code navigation improves dramatically too, because variable and file names match the documented language, so searching for a concept actually finds the relevant code.

For your own projects, use "Grill with Docs" whenever you're working with a codebase, even early on – establishing shared language early pays dividends. Keep "Grill Me" for generic tasks like brainstorming or creative writing where no codebase exists.

So today we learned that combining relentless questioning with a shared vocabulary eliminates repetitive clarification, produces terser and more aligned AI output, and yields code that mirrors how we actually talk about our domain – making AI-assisted development genuinely faster and more coherent.
