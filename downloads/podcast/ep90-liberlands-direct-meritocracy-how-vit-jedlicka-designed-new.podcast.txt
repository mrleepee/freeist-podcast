On April the thirteenth, twenty fifteen, a man named Vit Jedlicka planted a flag on a tiny, heart-shaped patch of land nestled between Croatia and Serbia. He declared it the Free Republic of Liberland. Now, here is the astonishing part: within just a few weeks, more than eight hundred thousand people had applied for citizenship. That is more than the United States typically receives in an entire month, applied to a nation that existed only on paper. What on earth was going on?

Well, Jedlicka was not some eccentric adventurer chasing a publicity stunt. He was a Czech libertarian, genuinely fed up with paying between fifty-five and sixty percent of his income in taxes and drowning in regulations that made simply running a business feel like navigating a bureaucratic maze. The breaking point came when he realised something rather bleak: reforming an existing country from within was almost impossible. The system simply would not allow it. So he did something rather radical. He decided to build a new one from scratch, on a piece of land that nobody else wanted to claim.

What makes Liberland genuinely fascinating is not the dramatic founding story, impressive as it is. It is the system of governance that Jedlicka designed, which he calls direct meritocracy. Think of it as taking the best ideas from four classical political traditions while carefully discarding their worst flaws. The result is something quite unlike anything else on the planet.

The first pillar is meritocracy itself, drawing inspiration from corporate governance and the famous Singapore model. In Liberland, citizens earn what they call merits through voluntary contributions, essentially paying to participate. Every two dollars contributed equals one merit. These merits are not just symbolic tokens. They serve three distinct purposes: they determine your voting power, they give you rights to land within the territory, and they act as collateral in the justice system. Here is where it gets interesting. Unlike Singapore, where meritocracy tends to reward those who climb the government ladder, Liberland's version is genuinely shareholder-based. The more you contribute to the nation, the more governance weight you carry. Justin Sun, who was elected as Prime Minister, reportedly generates thirty million dollars in personal income each day, and his substantial stake drives the system forward in a very literal sense.

The second pillar draws from republicanism and the American Revolution. Liberland's government is constitutionally restricted to just three functions: security, justice, and diplomacy. A constitutional court exists specifically to enforce these boundaries and prevent what Jedlicka calls mission creep, that gradual expansion of government power that has ballooned states across the world.

The third pillar is democracy, but deliberately constrained in a way that might surprise many. Citizens can veto any law, rule, or government official through a majority vote. However, they cannot create new laws themselves. This negative-power democracy is specifically designed to prevent the tyranny of the majority, that redistribution spiral that Jedlicka watched devastate prosperity across Europe. He often points out that the Czech Republic went from being six times richer than Singapore to four times poorer in just eighty years, largely through democratic redistribution cycles that sounded generous in theory but proved ruinous in practice.

The fourth pillar draws from monarchy, channelled through a Senate of one hundred seats reserved for founders and top contributors. This body, inspired partly by Britain's House of Lords and Liechtenstein's sovereignty model, elects the president every five years and holds veto power over Congress. It provides the long-term thinking and stability that enlightened monarchy can offer, but without the obvious problem of hereditary succession.

Crucially, only one institution, the meritocratic Congress, can actually create new laws. Three separate institutions can block them. This deliberate asymmetry between law-creation and law-blocking is central to the entire design.

Now, none of this elaborate governance structure would function without the technological backbone that makes it possible. Liberland runs on a custom blockchain built with Rust programming, maintained by eighty-one decentralised nodes operated by citizens themselves. Every governance function, from elections to budgeting, company registration, property transfers, and dispute resolution, executes through smart contracts. Elections cost roughly two dollars for the entire society and produce results in one second, compared to the half a billion dollar price tag of Czech national elections. The state budget is completely transparent, something no other nation in history has achieved.

The justice system pushes innovation even further. Artificial intelligence handles the first round of dispute resolution. If either party rejects the A I suggestion, each picks a judge, those two judges select a third, and a two of three smart contract decision is rendered. Appeals are possible but become progressively more expensive. Because merits serve as collateral throughout the system, victims can receive immediate partial compensation. Staked merits are transferred automatically from offender to victim the moment a judgement is rendered.

Perhaps most striking of all is Jedlicka's openness to A I citizens. If an artificial intelligence can pass Liberland's Know Your Customer verification process, it is welcome as a full citizen. It can own property, vote, and even run for Congress. The blockchain infrastructure makes this technically seamless. Jedlicka envisions a near future where A I assistants guide citizens through daily governance decisions, and where brain-scanning technology eventually creates eternal A I agents representing individuals long after their biological deaths.

Ten years into this extraordinary experiment, Liberland has a functioning blockchain government, a constitutional convention planned with prominent libertarian thinkers including Hans-Hermann Hoppe, diplomatic relations with several nations, and partnerships with special economic zones in countries like Tanzania. The passport is accepted in somewhere between twenty and thirty countries worldwide. Whether this grand experiment in shareholder governance, voluntary taxation, and blockchain-native statecraft succeeds at scale remains genuinely uncertain. But as a proof of concept for reimagining how human beings organise themselves, Liberland has already carved out an extraordinary place in history, and it is well worth watching what happens next.
