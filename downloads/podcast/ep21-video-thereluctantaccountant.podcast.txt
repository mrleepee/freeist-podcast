A single Oracle‑linked data‑centre project is currently backed by roughly $38 billion in loans – a figure that gives you a sense of just how much capital is flowing into the AI infrastructure boom.  

In the past few years, the race to build ever‑larger language models has sparked a massive construction spree. Tech firms and specialised data‑centre operators are erecting facilities that can each consume as much electricity as a small town. The price tag for these mega‑centres often runs into the billions, and that is where the banks step in.  

JP Morgan Chase, Morgan Stanley and Japan’s MUFG have become the main financiers, collectively providing hundreds of billions of dollars in credit to companies like Oracle and CoreWeave. Their appetite for these deals is understandable – the returns look attractive, at least on paper, and the borrowers are household names. But the sheer size of each loan means the banks are sitting on a very concentrated pool of risk.  

To guard against that concentration, the lenders have started to use what the industry calls “significant risk transfers.” They slice the loans into tranches, keep the senior pieces on their books, and sell the riskier junior pieces to private‑credit funds, insurers and other investors. In the case of the Oracle‑linked project, reports suggest banks are trying to offload roughly $38 billion of exposure, and some are even accepting a discount to move the pieces faster. The secondary market for these loan pieces is already humming, and analysts expect the trend to accelerate as banks look to free up capital.  

Why the rush to shed that risk? For one, the deals are heavily concentrated – just a handful of borrowers are taking on enormous amounts of debt. Many of the projects are still under construction, which means the cash flows are not yet predictable. Add to that growing public opposition to the land, water and energy demands of these centres, and you can see why banks feel uneasy about holding such large, undiversified exposures.  

If banks were to pull back or slow their lending, the consequences could ripple through the whole AI ecosystem. New data‑centre builds could stall, delaying the next wave of AI services. In response, borrowers might turn to less‑regulated “shadow‑banking” entities that are willing to take on the risk for a higher return, raising concerns about financial stability.  

In short, the AI boom is being turbo‑charged by a huge debt overhang. Massive borrowings, uncertain returns and a concentration of risk together create a classic “too much debt, not enough certainty” problem. It is a story that investors, regulators and anyone watching the AI sector should keep a close eye on.  

That’s the picture right now – a massive build‑out funded by big banks, a race to shift risk, and a set of uncertainties that could reshape the AI landscape. Keep an eye on those numbers.
