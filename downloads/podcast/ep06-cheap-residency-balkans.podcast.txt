If you’ve been keeping an eye on Europe’s investor‑residency scene, you’ll have noticed that a few of the old favourites are tightening up fast – Spain, Portugal, Greece, Ireland and the UK have all dialled back or shut their golden‑visa doors altogether. But there’s a pocket of opportunity that’s still wide open and, frankly, rather under‑the‑radar. I’m talking about three Western Balkan countries that are both affordable and on the EU‑candidate track: Albania, Montenegro and Serbia. All three already give you visa‑free travel in the Schengen area, and they’re all chasing full EU membership, which means the investment bar could start rising as accession talks progress.

Let’s start with Albania. The programme is a bit of a moving target – there’s no officially confirmed minimum investment figure, though you’ll hear whispers of around €100 k for a business stake or €300 k in real estate, but those numbers aren’t set in stone. What you do need is an active role in the investment and proof that you’re meeting tax obligations. The path to citizenship is fairly straightforward: you get a one‑year residence permit that can be renewed, then after five years you can apply for permanent residency, and after seven years you’re eligible for citizenship. One of the biggest draws is that Albania allows dual citizenship, so you can keep your original passport while you’re building up your new European status. The tax system is progressive, ranging from 0 % to 23 % for individuals, a flat 15 % on corporate profits and a modest 8 % on dividends. The only real downside is that the programme lacks a codified, precise legal framework – rules can shift, so it pays to stay up to date.

Now, Montenegro. The country is often seen as the most advanced of the three when it comes to EU talks, with a target of joining the Union by 2028, and it already uses the euro, so there’s no currency‑risk to worry about. To qualify, you need to buy property with a taxable value of at least €150 k and actually use that property – you can’t just leave it empty. Business owners also have to pay an annual tax of about €5 k, and you’re not allowed to take up employment in Montenegro while you hold the permit. The downside is the timeline: it takes a full ten years from the start of the programme to become a citizen, making it the longest route of the three. And a heads‑up – Montenegro previously ran a citizenship‑by‑investment (CBI) scheme that was pressured to close by the EU back in 2022, so the current programme is more modest, but it’s still live and accepting applicants.

Finally, Serbia. If cost and speed are your priorities, Serbia is pretty hard to beat. You can either invest a minimum of €50 k in a registered Serbian company or buy any real estate of any value. Either way, you’ll need to deposit €50 k into a Serbian bank account as proof of funds. The tax regime is refreshingly simple: a flat 10 % personal income tax and a 15 % corporate tax – the lowest rates in Europe at the moment. The processing is swift, typically one to two months, and the route to citizenship runs about six years in total – three years on a temporary residence permit followed by three years on permanent residency. Serbia’s passport is a strong talking point: it grants visa‑free access to 138 countries, including Russia, China and the entire Schengen area. The trade‑off is that Serbia’s EU candidacy is moving more slowly than Montenegro’s, and dual‑citizenship exceptions are limited, so you’ll need to weigh how much you value that eventual EU membership versus the immediate benefits.

So, what’s the bottom line? These three Balkan programmes are still relatively inexpensive compared with the closed‑off western European options, and they’re actively taking applications right now. But keep an eye on the horizon – as any of these countries gets closer to EU accession, investment thresholds are likely to climb. The key is to match the programme to what matters most to you: Albania if you want the flexibility of dual citizenship, Montenegro if you’re betting on a stronger EU future and the stability of the euro, or Serbia if you want the quickest, cheapest route with low taxes and a surprisingly powerful passport.
