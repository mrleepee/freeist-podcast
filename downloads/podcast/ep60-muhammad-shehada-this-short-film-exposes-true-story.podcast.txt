What if the story we've been told about the founding of Israel isn't quite what we thought? That might sound like a bold claim, but when you dig into historical documents and statements from the key figures themselves, some uncomfortable questions start to emerge.

The standard narrative suggests that when the Romans expelled the Jews from Jerusalem in seventy C E, that was essentially it for Jewish presence in the region. But some historians argue the population decline was far more gradual, and that in the seventh century, Arabs actually invited Jews to return to Jerusalem. This challenges a core element of how the history of the region is often presented, and raises questions about whose version of events became dominant and why.

Looking at what Israel's own founding fathers said behind closed doors makes for fascinating, if troubling, reading. In nineteen thirty-eight, David Ben-Gurion apparently indicated that partition was merely a temporary strategy, a stepping stone toward eventually expanding to what he called the whole of Palestine. Chaim Weizmann, in a letter from the same year, was even more explicit, suggesting the partition arrangement was only intended to last the next twenty-five to thirty years. These statements rather contradict the picture of a permanent, immutable solution that was being sold to the international community.

The events of nineteen forty-eight remain deeply contentious. Some accounts argue that Palestinian Arabs largely did not want to fight and were trying to maintain calm, while Zionist forces deliberately provoked incidents to create justification for expulsion. Specific villages like Deir Yassin, Tantura, and parts of Haifa are cited as sites of atrocities. There are allegations of systematic brutality, including sexual violence, and claims of direct orders from Ben-Gurion to act without mercy, including against women and children. Whether these accounts are fully accurate or somewhat exaggerated, they certainly complicate the cleaner versions of events we often hear.

Propaganda played a significant role too. The concept of whisper propaganda, designed to spread fear among Arab populations, is cited as a deliberate tactic. And the assassination of the United Nations mediator Count Folke Bernadotte by the Lehi group, often dismissed as an aberration, raises questions about who truly controlled events on the ground.

What's perhaps most striking is how these historical framings echo into the present. The same patterns of contested narratives, selective interpretation, and resistance to scrutiny remain evident in how the current conflict is discussed. Understanding this history isn't about assigning total blame to one side, but rather about recognising that the stories we accept about the past shape how we see the present, and that questioning those stories is never really optional if we want to understand what's actually happening.
