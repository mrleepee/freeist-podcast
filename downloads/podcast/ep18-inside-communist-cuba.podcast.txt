Well, hello there and welcome back to the show. Today I want to take you on a bit of a journey, and I promise it won't be a comfortable one. Imagine stepping off a plane into a country where the streets look frozen in time, where classic cars from the 1950s still rumble down streets that haven't changed in decades, and where the people you meet are too frightened to tell you the truth about their own lives. That's exactly what happened to a documentary filmmaker recently, and what they uncovered is nothing short of heartbreaking.

Cuba, my friends, is in the grip of one of the most severe humanitarian crises the world has ever seen. Can you believe that seven out of ten people there are going without the meals they actually need? Not occasionally, but regularly. We're talking about a country where food shortages are the norm rather than the exception, where medicine and basic necessities are so scarce that people are truly struggling to get by. And if that's not enough, daily blackouts are disrupting everything from daily life to education. The national university, for instance, has actually had to shut its doors because the power situation has become so dire. Just let that sink in for a moment.

Now, let's talk about money, because the economic reality there is absolutely staggering. The average monthly salary works out to something like fourteen pounds, which is barely enough to buy a few groceries, let alone cover any unexpected expenses. And here's something that really got me. petrol stations are completely dry because oil supplies have been disrupted, and this is apparently linked to goings-on with Maduro and his regime. On the black market, a litre of petrol costs around ten pounds, while the government price is only a pound. Sounds cheap, right? But here's the catch – to get petrol at that government price, you'd need to join a queue and wait for your turn, possibly being number twelve hundred or more in line. That's not a typo. People are spending days waiting for fuel that costs them a week's wages to buy on the black market.

The Cuban peso, I should mention, has become virtually worthless in terms of purchasing power. It's a dire situation that affects absolutely everyone.

But what really struck me about this documentary was the atmosphere of fear that hangs over everything. Under the communist system, there's no freedom of speech or press. None whatsoever. If you speak out against the government, you risk imprisonment. Literally. And it's not just the authorities you have to worry about. Neighbours are encouraged to report on each other, which means trust has completely broken down in communities. The filmmaker described being followed throughout their entire trip by Cuban intelligence agents. When they arrived, their camera equipment was seized. They were interrogated by a two-star general at their hotel, and even the president apparently knew they were there. Can you imagine the pressure that puts on ordinary people? When the documentary team tried to interview locals, everyone was terrified to speak openly about politics. You could see the fear in their eyes, the hesitation before they spoke. That's no way to live, is it?

The infrastructure situation is equally telling. Buildings and streets in Havana and other cities look much as they did in the 1950s. The US embargo combined with the communist economic model has essentially killed entrepreneurship and innovation. No new cars have entered the country since 1959. Think about that. Sixty-five years without any new vehicles. Those beautiful vintage American cars that tourists love to photograph? They're still running, but they're government-controlled now, part of the state apparatus rather than private enterprise.

What really got to me were the conversations the filmmaker had with ordinary Cubans. Despite the fear, some people did open up a little, and what they said was quite revealing. Many desperately want change. They want better living conditions, more freedom, the chance to build a decent life for themselves and their families. Some even expressed hope that perhaps the United States might intervene militarily, which tells you something about how desperate things have become. People drew parallels between what's happened in Cuba and the failures of communist systems elsewhere. There's a real anger, too, that the government elite seem to have resources and privileges while ordinary citizens suffer. You could hear the frustration in their voices, the sense that they're trapped in a system that offers them no way out.

The filmmaker's own experience was quite dramatic, to say the least. Despite having proper visa documentation, they were targeted from the moment they arrived. Multiple spies monitored them throughout their stay. They conducted interviews with citizens who were clearly afraid for their safety. And then, in what must have been a rather tense moment, they made a dramatic four a.m. escape from their hotel, only to be interrogated again at the airport before finally being allowed to leave the country.

So where does all this leave us? Well, I think this documentary offers a sobering glimpse into a world that most of us know very little about. It shows us what happens when a government prioritises control over the wellbeing of its people. It shows us the human cost of political ideology gone wrong. And perhaps most importantly, it reminds us that behind every statistic, every policy, every political decision, there are real people – people who want the same things we want – safety, freedom, enough to eat, a future worth looking forward to.
