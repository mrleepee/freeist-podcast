Here's a thought: by the time you hit forty, there's a seventy percent chance that a small, pea-sized gland sitting right in the centre of your brain has started to turn to stone. Not metaphorically — literally. Calcium deposits, accumulating like tiny pebbles in a fountain, and most of us have no idea it's happening.

That gland is the pineal gland, and while neuroscience has spent decades unravelling the mysteries of the cortex and hippocampus, the pineal gland has remained something of an enigma. By age fifty-five, over eighty percent of adults show measurable calcification on brain scans, yet most mainstream medicine regards this as an inevitable part of ageing rather than something we might actually do something about. Today I want to challenge that fatalism, because some genuinely fascinating research suggests there's a remarkably simple practice — just three minutes of daily humming — that could reverse this process and restore this curious little gland to something closer to its natural state.

The technique is called Brahmari, and it comes from yogic traditions, but what makes it genuinely compelling is the hard science that's emerged over the past couple of decades. Researchers at the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm discovered something rather startling: when you hum, you amplify nitric oxide production in your paranasal sinuses by roughly fifteen times compared to normal breathing. Nitric oxide is a signalling molecule, and it just so happens that the pineal gland has the second-highest blood flow per gram of tissue in the entire body. That high blood flow means those nitric oxide molecules can actually reach the gland itself, diffusing through a perforated bone at the base of the skull called the cribriform plate and entering the gland's vascular territory.

So what does nitric oxide actually do once it gets there? The research from cardiology is actually quite instructive here, because the same mineral that deposits in your pineal gland — hydroxyapatite — also forms arterial plaque and kidney stones. And forty years of cardiology research has shown that nitric oxide can help dissolve these calcified deposits through vasodilatory pathways. The implication is striking: the same mechanism that helps clear arteries might also help clear your pineal gland.

The practice itself is straightforward. You sit upright with a long spine, plug your ears with your index fingers, and hum on the exhale through barely parted lips with teeth slightly separated. The key is to feel the vibration resonating in your skull rather than your throat. Three minutes daily is the recommendation, and the idea is that while calcification does slowly return over the years, a consistent daily practice outpaces that redeposition rate.

What's particularly elegant about this approach is that it tackles both sides of the problem. Nitric oxide doesn't just dissolve existing deposits; it also inhibits something called NFKB signalling, which is essentially the inflammatory trigger that drives new calcification in the first place. So you're addressing the symptoms and the root cause simultaneously.

Now, you might be wondering why this isn't common knowledge, why your GP isn't prescribing humming exercises. The honest answer is that nobody can sell you a pharmaceutical product from this prescription. The research sits at the intersection of respiratory physiology, cardiology, and neurovascular science — fields that don't often talk to each other. The studies are peer-reviewed, but they're scattered across journals and disciplines, which means no single paper has captured mainstream attention.

Three minutes a day, no equipment, no appointments, no supplements. The mechanism is well-documented; the application to the pineal gland is the piece that hasn't quite made it into clinical practice yet. Whether that changes probably depends on whether enough people decide to try it for themselves and notice the difference.
