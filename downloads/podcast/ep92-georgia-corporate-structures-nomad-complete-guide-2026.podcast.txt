Here is a bold number to start with: ninety-five nations can walk into Georgia today, stick around for a full year, and the only thing standing between them and legally earning money there was until recently a simple state registration and a willingness to hustle. That is a remarkable pitch, and thousands of digital nomads have taken Georgia up on it. But here is the catch that trips most people up, and it is a genuine doozy. That famous one percent tax you have probably heard about? It is not a company structure. It is a tax status that sits on top of one specific structure, and that confusion leads a lot of people to set things up in ways that do not actually serve them. Let us clear that up, because the rules also changed in twenty twenty-six in a way that affects anyone thinking about basing themselves in Tbilisi or Batumi.

The first thing to understand is that the one percent applies to turnover under something called Small Business Status, and that status only works with an Individual Entrepreneur, which is a sole proprietorship. There is no separate legal entity here, no charter, no shareholders. You register as yourself, you are the business, and your personal assets sit right next to the business liabilities. That sounds alarming, but for a freelance designer, developer, or writer with no inventory and no staff, it is usually an acceptable trade. Registration happens at the House of Justice, usually by the next business day, and through a local agency you are looking at roughly one hundred and forty dollars all in. The one percent kicks in on turnover up to about one hundred eighty thousand US dollars per year, at which point it rises to three percent on the excess. There are no deductible expenses, so if you bill one hundred thousand dollars but spent forty thousand running the business, you still pay one percent of the full hundred thousand. For a service business with low costs, that is an extraordinary deal. For a business with thin margins and high costs, it can actually work against you. Worth checking the excluded activities list carefully before you commit. And here is the new bit for twenty twenty-six: you now have a strict fifteen-day window after registering to apply for Small Business Status. Miss it and any income earned in that gap can get taxed at the full twenty percent. The offsetting good news is that status now applies from the day you apply rather than the first of the following month, which means the old dead zone for new freelancers is finally gone.

The second main option is the limited liability company, a separate legal entity that is not you, with its own assets and liabilities. You can bring in partners, raise money, or sell it, which makes this the structure to aim for once you move beyond solo freelancing. It costs more to set up, usually around eight hundred dollars through an agency, and the bookkeeping runs significantly higher, something like five hundred lari a month rather than the one hundred and fifty or so for a straightforward Individual Entrepreneur. The tax model is where it gets interesting. Georgia uses what people call the Estonian model: a Georgian LLC pays fifteen percent corporate income tax, but only on profits it actually distributes. Money you leave inside the company and reinvest is not taxed at the corporate level when it is earned. The tax event is the dividend, the moment profit leaves the company and lands in your pocket. For someone who is ploughing earnings back into growth, that is a powerful incentive.

For IT companies and software businesses serving clients abroad, there are a couple of special regimes worth knowing about. The Virtual Zone offers zero percent corporate tax on qualifying IT income earned from outside Georgia, though the qualifying definition is narrow and the authorities increasingly want to see real substance rather than a nameplate address. Setup typically starts around fifteen hundred dollars and takes a couple of weeks. International IT Company status is a heavier designation requiring documented IT experience and genuine physical presence, an actual office and people on the ground. That one starts around five thousand dollars and moves slowly. Free Industrial Zones exist too, mainly for trading and manufacturing, so less relevant for the typical remote worker, though there is a Virtual Asset Service Provider license for crypto businesses that starts at around forty thousand dollars, firmly in the not-casual-nomad territory.

Now for the change that reshapes the whole picture in twenty twenty-six. As of the first of March, Georgia introduced a mandatory work permit for most foreign nationals without permanent residence. The era of simply showing up, registering, and invoicing is over. The right to be in the country and the right to earn money there are now two separate documents. If you are registering as a self-employed Individual Entrepreneur, you fall under the permit system and need to submit either a turnover document if you have an existing business or a business plan if you are starting fresh. Processing takes up to thirty calendar days, and working without the permit now carries a fine of around two thousand lari. There is a transition cushion for those already in the system, giving until the start of twenty twenty-seven to get permits and residence documents aligned. One genuinely grey area remains: if you work purely remotely for foreign clients with no Georgian clients and no local registration at all, several legal commentators suggest you may be exempt, but the government has not fully clarified this, so the sensible approach is to assume the permit applies and factor the timeline into your plans.

So where does that leave things practically? For a solo freelancer with low costs and revenue comfortably under the one hundred eighty thousand dollar ceiling, the Individual Entrepreneur with Small Business Status is almost certainly the right call. One percent on turnover, cheap setup, straightforward accounting. Just make sure you treat the work permit and the fifteen-day application window as genuine planning constraints, not afterthoughts. For anyone with a partner, employees, real liability exposure, or ambitions to raise money or sell the business, the LLC is the better vehicle, and you accept the fifteen percent distribution tax and proper bookkeeping as the price of being a proper company. Georgia remains one of the most accessible places in the world to set up and run a business, but the days of treating the rules as optional are firmly behind us, and doing it properly now means doing it with your eyes open to every step.
