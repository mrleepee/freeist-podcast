Welcome to the podcast. Today we're diving into one of the most talked-about jurisdictions for digital nomads and location-independent entrepreneurs: the country of Georgia, and specifically the corporate structures available to foreigners who want to set up shop there.

Georgia has built a reputation as a low-tax, low-bureaucracy haven at the crossroads of Europe and Asia. But as of 2026, there are important changes you need to know about before packing your bags. Let's walk through the options.

We'll start with the simplest and cheapest route: the Individual Entrepreneur, or IE. This is Georgia's version of a sole proprietorship. Registration costs about 320 lari in notary fees plus a 61 lari state fee, which comes to roughly 140 US dollars total. If you're doing this remotely through a law firm or service provider, expect to pay around 400 dollars all in. The best part? Registration is typically completed by the next business day.

The catch with the IE is that there's no liability shield. You and your business are the same legal entity. Your personal assets are on the line if something goes wrong. For many freelancers and developers doing low-risk work, that trade-off is acceptable.

Now here's where it gets really interesting. When you register as an IE, you can apply for Small Business Status. This gives you a flat 1 percent tax on gross turnover up to 500,000 lari, which is roughly 180,000 US dollars. Above that threshold, the rate jumps to 3 percent. There's no separate corporate income tax, no dividend tax, no capital gains tax on business income. For a freelance developer earning 100,000 dollars a year, your total tax bill in Georgia could be under 1,000 dollars. That's not a typo.

But there's a critical new rule for 2026. You now have a 15-day window after registration to apply for Small Business Status. Miss that deadline and you're bumped up to the standard personal income tax rate, which can reach 20 percent. This is part of a broader regulatory tightening that we'll get to shortly.

For those just starting out, there's also Micro Business Status, which offers a 0 percent tax rate. But the ceiling is only 30,000 lari in annual turnover, about 11,000 US dollars. Above that, you must transition to Small Business Status. It's a useful stepping stone but not a long-term solution.

If you want proper liability protection, the Georgian LLC is your next option. Setup costs run about 700 lari for registration plus 244 lari in state fees, roughly 300 to 350 US dollars total. Remote setup through a provider typically costs around 800 dollars. The LLC uses what Georgia calls the Estonian model of taxation: a 15 percent corporate income tax that's levied only on distributed profits. Keep the money in the company and reinvest it, and you pay zero corporate tax. This makes the LLC a powerful vehicle for growing a business without the tax drag.

Now let's talk about the headline act for tech entrepreneurs: the Virtual Zone. A Virtual Zone company pays 0 percent corporate income tax on revenue from IT services delivered to clients outside Georgia. Setup costs through a service provider run around 1,500 dollars. The requirements are straightforward: you need to be a registered Georgian IT company, and your clients need to be outside the country. This structure has been enormously popular with software agencies, SaaS companies, and freelance developers serving international clients.

There's also the more rigorous International IT Company designation, which requires real substance in Georgia: actual office space, local employees, and genuine operations. Setup costs around 5,000 dollars. It's more defensible under international scrutiny but overkill for most solo nomads.

Georgia also operates Free Industrial Zones in Poti, Kutaisi, and Tbilisi. Companies in these zones enjoy 0 percent tax on virtually everything: corporate income, property, VAT, and dividends. Setup is comparable to a Virtual Zone at around 1,500 dollars, but you need to physically operate within the zone, which limits flexibility.

For the crypto crowd, Georgia offers a Virtual Asset Service Provider license. It's not cheap at roughly 40,000 dollars, but it provides a regulated framework for running crypto exchanges, wallet services, and other digital asset businesses.

Now, the elephant in the room: the 2026 regulatory changes. Effective March 1, 2026, foreign nationals who want to run a business or work as freelancers in Georgia must obtain a mandatory work permit called the Right to Labour Activity. The permit fee is about 500 lari, and failure to obtain it can result in fines of around 2,000 lari. The 15-day window for Small Business Status is part of this broader crackdown. These changes don't eliminate Georgia's tax advantages, but they add a compliance layer that didn't exist before.

Finally, a quick note on residency pathways. Georgia offers a 12-month digital nomad visa requiring proof of at least 2,000 dollars per month in income. There's a 3-year residence permit specifically for IT professionals, but watch out for the 183-day tax residency trap. And for investors, purchasing at least 150,000 dollars in Georgian real estate grants residency, which has made cities like Batumi and Tbilisi popular with foreign buyers.

So where does that leave us? Georgia remains one of the best-value jurisdictions for digital nomads, but you can no longer just show up and start working. The IE with Small Business Status is still the fastest and cheapest entry point for solo operators. The LLC with the Estonian tax model is the smart play for those building something bigger. And the Virtual Zone continues to offer an unbeatable zero percent rate for IT companies. The key is to plan ahead, secure your work permit early, and choose the structure that matches your actual business, not just the one with the lowest number on paper.
