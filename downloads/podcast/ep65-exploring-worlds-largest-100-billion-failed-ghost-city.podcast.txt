How does a city built for a million people end up with fewer than ten thousand residents? That's the story of Forest City, a sprawling development in Malaysia that sits just across the water from Singapore. Originally conceived by the Chinese developer Country Garden, this was supposed to be a master-planned utopia, a gleaming new city where people would live, work, and raise families. The reality, however, is rather different. Only one of the four planned islands has seen any real development, and even that island remains mostly empty. Walking through its pristine streets and immaculately maintained landscaping, you could be forgiven for thinking you'd wandered onto a film set. The infrastructure is impeccable, but most windows are dark, and the only people you might spot are security guards and a handful of foreign workers going about their day. It's genuinely eerie.

So what went wrong? Well, this is a cautionary tale about property bubbles and over-ambitious development. After China's land-use reforms in the nineties, developers got into the habit of selling properties before they were even finished, using that money to buy more land and rack up enormous debts. Then, in 2017, the Chinese government cracked down on overseas property purchases by its citizens, which effectively cut off the main market of buyers for Forest City. Tighter credit for developers and the pandemic further stalled construction and sales. The project now carries around one hundred and ninety billion dollars in debt, leaving Country Garden legally obliged to maintain a city that almost nobody lives in.

Among the tiny population that does call Forest City home, there's a retired local chap named Alan who rather enjoys the quiet and solitude. Occasionally you'll spot short-term visitors or curious tourists wandering through, but for the most part the place feels like a ghost town. Filming is heavily restricted and guards outnumber residents, adding to that slightly unsettling sense of being watched.

Now here's where things get interesting. An experimental community called the Network School has set up shop inside Forest City, operating as a special economic zone with tax incentives and government approval. Their goal is to attract global entrepreneurs, digital nomads, and what they call "opt-in" citizens—people looking for an alternative to traditional Western institutions. The concept is rooted in Balaji Srinivasan's "Network State" idea, which envisions building a new society from scratch, one that could eventually become its own nation with the power to issue passports. If this works, the thinking goes, those empty towers could be filled with innovators and dreamers, transforming the ghost city into a thriving hub of creativity and commerce.

Whether that's realistic, though, remains to be seen. Observers suggest it could take a few years to fill the buildings if the experiment succeeds, but the obstacles are significant. The massive debt, the tarnished reputation, the lack of everyday amenities, and those restrictive security measures could all undermine any revival. Forest City stands as a striking example of how grand visions can collapse under the weight of speculation and shifting markets. Whether it becomes a revived urban centre or simply a monument to over-ambition, only time will tell.
