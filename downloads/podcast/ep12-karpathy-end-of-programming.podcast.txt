Right, let's have a proper chat about something that's been making quite a few waves in the tech world lately. So, Andrej Karpathy—who, for those who might not know, is essentially one of the founding fathers of modern AI—has declared that programming as we know it might be coming to an end. And before you switch off thinking this is just another breathless tech prediction, stick around, because the reasoning behind it is actually rather fascinating.

Karpathy's framework centres around what he calls the Three Ages of Software. Now, the first age will be familiar to anyone who's ever written a line of code—it's what he terms Software 1.0. That's your traditional programming, where you sit down with Python or C++ or whatever takes your fancy, and you write explicit instructions telling a computer exactly what to do. Every semicolon, every bracket, every logic gate—written by hand by an actual human being who understood what they wanted.

Then comes Software 2.0, which is where things get interesting. Instead of writing code, you're now training neural networks. The "code" becomes the weights in those networks—the values that get adjusted as the system learns from data. An image classifier, for instance, isn't programmed line by line; it's trained until it learns to recognise patterns on its own.

But here's where it gets truly mind-bending. Software 3.0 is when you programme these systems using plain old English. You have a conversation with a large language model, you describe what you want, and it writes the code for you. The programmer becomes more of a prompt engineer than a traditional coder.

Now, Karpathy draws some rather useful analogies to help us understand where large language models sit in the grand scheme of things. He suggests they're a bit like utilities—you've got centralised training which requires significant capital expenditure, then metered access through APIs, and the whole thing demands low latency. It's remarkably similar to how we think about electricity or water, actually. You can switch providers fairly easily, like flicking a transfer switch, though there's obviously some friction involved.

He also likens them to operating systems. The model itself functions as the CPU, and the context window—that's the amount of information it can work with at any given moment—acts like memory. We're essentially building new computing paradigms, though Karpathy reckons we're at roughly the 1960s stage of this revolution. Cloud access and time-sharing are established, but the personal computing moment hasn't quite arrived economically yet. One intriguing twist is that technology diffusion seems reversed this time around—consumers adopted these tools before governments and corporations really caught on.

Of course, these systems have their peculiarities. On the plus side, they've got encyclopedic knowledge that would make a librarian weep with joy—almost Rainman-like in their ability to recall information. But they come with significant caveats. Hallucinations are a genuine problem; they'll confidently tell you absolute nonsense and sound utterly convinced. They also have what Karpathy calls "jagged intelligence"—extraordinarily capable in some domains while failing spectacularly in others. You might have seen examples where they insist that 9.11 is greater than 9.9, which is, well, rather concerning. And much like characters in Memento, they have no long-term memory between conversations. They're also vulnerable to prompt injection attacks, which opens up security concerns we haven't fully grappled with yet.

The new wave of applications is embracing what's called "partial autonomy." Take Cursor, which is transforming how people write code, or Perplexity, which is reimagining web search. These tools manage the model's context, orchestrate multiple models working together, and provide graphical interfaces so humans can verify outputs quickly. The clever bit is this: the AI generates, the human verifies, and that loop just keeps spinning faster and faster. Visual comprehension through a GUI turns out to be far more efficient than reading through reams of text. The guidance seems to be keep the AI on a short leash—small, incremental changes rather than letting it run wild with autonomous modifications.

Then there's what Karpathy charmingly calls "vibe coding." The idea is that anyone can now create software simply by describing what they want in natural language. No formal training required. He himself built iOS apps and something called Menu Gen using this approach. The caveat, though, is that making apps genuinely production-ready—with authentication, payments, deployment, all that tedious DevOps stuff—remains stubbornly difficult. But it serves as a gateway, a way for people to dip their toes into software creation who might never have considered it before.

For those building things that need to work well with AI agents, there are some practical considerations. Think about creating LLM.txt files for websites instead of just HTML. Provide documentation in markdown. Replace "click here" instructions with actual curl commands that agents can execute directly. Companies like Vercel and Stripe are already moving in this direction. Anthropic's Model Context Protocol is enabling direct communication between agents and services, which is rather exciting.

Looking ahead, Karpathy sees a massive rewrite of software underway. The autonomy slider—that control that determines how much the AI does versus how much the human does—will gradually shift rightward over the coming decade. But he offers this lovely metaphor: we're building Iron Man suits, not fully autonomous robots. The human remains essential, in the loop, overseeing things. It's augmentation rather than replacement, at least for now.

So there you have it. Whether you're a seasoned developer or simply curious about where technology is heading, it's undeniably an exhilarating time to be paying attention. The rules are being rewritten as we speak, and the only certainty is that the next few years will be rather extraordinary to witness.
