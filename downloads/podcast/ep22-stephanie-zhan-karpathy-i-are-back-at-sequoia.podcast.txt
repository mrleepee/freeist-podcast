Software development is undergoing its most significant transformation since the advent of compiled languages. The shift began, for many developers, the moment AI code generation became reliable enough that human intervention became the exception rather than the rule.

Take Andrej Karpathy, for instance. He describes December as a turning point when he realised he could essentially "vibe code" – writing software by trusting AI suggestions almost entirely and rarely needing to correct the output. This isn't just a productivity hack; it represents something deeper about how we should think about programming itself.

Karpathy frames this through a compelling three-stage evolution. Software 1.0 is what we've always known – explicit code written line by line. Software 2.0 emerged with machine learning, where we train neural networks using datasets instead of handcrafting algorithms. And now we're entering Software 3.0, where we programme through natural language prompts, with the context window serving as our primary lever over large language models.

The practical implications are remarkable. Installing software that once required complex shell scripts now amounts to copy-pasting a prompt to an AI agent. Services like MenuGen, which enhance restaurant menus from photos, can be replaced entirely by a single prompt to Gemini, with the neural network handling most of the heavy lifting without traditional app backends.

What's interesting is why some tasks automate beautifully while others lag behind. The key driver is verifiability. AI excels when you can easily check whether the output is correct – code, mathematics, data processing. This creates what Karpathy calls "jagged capabilities": impressively strong in verifiable domains but surprisingly weak elsewhere, like answering simple questions about distances between places.

For founders eyeing AI opportunities, the advice is clear: target verifiable problems and build reinforcement learning environments that let you fine-tune models for your specific needs. Even if major AI labs haven't prioritised your domain, the underlying fine-tuning frameworks are accessible enough to leverage.

There's an important distinction between vibe coding and agentic engineering. Vibe coding raises the floor, enabling anyone to produce functional software regardless of technical background. Agentic engineering, on the other hand, maintains professional quality standards by thoughtfully coordinating multiple AI agents that, individually, remain fallible and stochastic.

The truly valuable human skills in this landscape are taste, judgment, and high-level system design. AI agents handle API details, boilerplate code, and repetitive tasks admirably, but humans must define specifications, ensure security considerations, and manage logic across interconnected systems.

Looking ahead, infrastructure will need to become "agent-first" – documentation and services designed explicitly for AI comprehension, sensor-actuator frameworks, and eventually agent-to-agent representation for both individuals and organisations.

Finally, a thought on education: you can outsource the thinking, but you cannot outsource understanding. Deep comprehension of goals, context, and system architecture remains the true bottleneck, which makes tools that enhance personal knowledge processing increasingly precious as AI handles more execution-level work.

The message is clear – the future of software isn't about replacing human creativity, but about finding the right balance between human judgment and AI capability.
