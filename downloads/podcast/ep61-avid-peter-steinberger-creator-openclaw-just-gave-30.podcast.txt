What if the best programmer on your team never sleeps, never gets tired, and costs roughly the same as your morning coffee? I'm not talking about some distant future sci-fi scenario. I'm talking about AI coding agents, and there's a growing number of developers who have quietly rebuilt their entire workflow around them. But here's the thing most people get wrong: they think it's about typing prompts and watching magic happen. It isn't. It's a craft. And like any craft, there are principles that separate the people who get stuck from the ones who reach what insiders call the "zen" level.

Let's start with something fundamental. When you hand a task to a human developer, they bring years of context. They know the codebase, they understand the team's conventions, they can read between the lines. An agent starts with absolutely nothing. Zip. Zero. So rule one is this: you must orient them. Don't assume an agent will magically know where to look or how to approach a problem. Tell them explicitly. Point them to the relevant files, explain the structure, give them a sense of what "good" looks like in your project. Think of it like onboarding a new team member on their first day. You'd never just say "fix the login bug" without a bit of orientation, would you? The same applies here.

Now, here's where it gets interesting. Rule two is about managing that context you just gave them. Agents have limits, and a common mistake is overwhelming them with your entire codebase all at once. It's like drowning someone in information rather than teaching them to swim. The better approach is to give them exactly what they need for the immediate task and let them explore further if required. Think of it as feeding them a series of focused meals rather than one enormous buffet. Context management isn't just a technical skill, it's an act of empathy. You're respecting the agent's limitations while still giving them enough to be useful.

Which brings us to rule three, and this one trips up a lot of people. When something goes wrong, fix forward. Do not revert, do not redo, do not spend an hour trying to undo what just happened and start again. The instinct to hit the reset button is human nature, but in an agentic workflow it becomes a trap. Agents generate a lot of output quickly, and some of it will be wrong. That's not a failure, that's the process working as intended. The magic is in iterating. You see what's broken, you give feedback, you nudge the agent in a new direction, and you move forward. Each iteration is information. Each correction sharpens the outcome. This is fundamentally different from the way most developers were trained to work, where clean commits and pristine histories were the goal. Let go of that perfectionism. You're not building a museum, you're building a product.

Rule four flips the script entirely. Treat agents as capable engineers, not as obedient tools. This sounds counterintuitive, but hear me out. When you assume an agent will fail, you micromanage every step. You second-guess their choices, you override their decisions, you turn a potentially brilliant collaborator into a glorified autocomplete. But here's what years of experimentation have shown: agents actually make surprisingly good decisions much of the time. They're trained on vast amounts of code and patterns. They spot solutions you might miss. So give them space to think, to propose, to surprise you. That doesn't mean you abdicate responsibility, it means you trust the process while staying engaged. Gentle direction beats tight control every single time.

Now let's talk about scale, because this is where things get genuinely exciting. Rule five: run multiple agents simultaneously. Not one agent doing one task at a time like some digital workhorse, but several agents working in parallel on different aspects of the same project. One might be building a new feature while another is fixing a bug over in the documentation. A third might be refactoring something in the background. You're not managing them in the traditional sense, you're orchestrating a small team that happens to exist entirely in software. The throughput is staggering compared to sequential human work. And because each agent is focused on a narrow problem, the quality tends to be higher too. It's a bit like having a kitchen where one chef isn't trying to do everything at once, but instead each station has its own specialist.

Rule six is a philosophical one, and it took me a while to fully embrace it. Accept that code won't be perfect. This is hard for developers because we've been trained to care deeply about craft, about elegance, about doing things the right way. But here's the trade-off: agents push projects forward in ways that perfectionism never can. A perfect piece of code that never ships is worth nothing. An imperfect piece of code that ships and works and gets iterated on is infinitely more valuable. When an agent produces something that's eighty percent of the way there, you have a decision to make. You can spend hours polishing it yourself, or you can accept it's good enough for now, ship it, and move on. Most of the time, the right answer is to move on. Your time is better spent on the next thing than refining the last thing.

Rule seven extends this philosophy into version control. Commit to main. No long-lived feature branches. I know, I know, this sounds heretical if you've spent years adhering to GitFlow or similar methodologies. But hear me out. Feature branches were designed for human teams where parallel workstreams could create merge nightmares. Agents don't have that problem. They can work in shared context, they can handle merge conflicts without drama, and they generate commits so frequently that long branches become unnecessary anyway. When you're satisfied with an agent's output, you commit it. You test it locally. You keep moving. The rhythm is completely different from the commit, branch, pull request, review, merge cycle that most developers know by heart.

Which brings us to rule eight. Make your codebase easy for agents to navigate. This isn't just about clear folder structures and sensible naming conventions, though those help enormously. It's about embracing the names and conventions that agents naturally gravitate towards. When an agent creates a file or a function, they often choose names that make sense to them based on their training data. You could fight this and insist everything match your personal style, but why would you? You're only making the agent's job harder and slowing yourself down. Accept the names agents choose. Let them shape the codebase in ways that feel natural to them. It's a small surrender that pays dividends in speed and coherence.

Now, here's the part that might surprise you. Despite all this, there's still a uniquely human role that no agent can replace. Rule nine: keep the human touch. Vision, prioritization, design decisions, adding delight, security review. These are things that require a human being with preferences, values, and intent. You decide what to build and what to reject. You choose the architectural direction, the ecosystem, the trade-offs that define your product's character. You add the small touches that make software feel alive, the easter eggs and personality that agents simply wouldn't think to create. And crucially, you review code for security, not just functionality. You read the pull requests with human eyes because you understand the context that an agent cannot. Some developers have developed something called a Soul dot MD, a document that captures their values, personality, and philosophy for working with AI. It sounds whimsical, but it's actually deeply practical. It ensures that the software you produce reflects not just technical competence but human thoughtfulness.

What does all of this add up to? Agentic programming isn't about replacing developers, it's about augmenting them in ways that compound over time. The skills required are learnable. The mindset shifts are doable. And the results can be genuinely transformative. But it requires letting go of old habits, trusting new processes, and accepting that the best work often comes from a partnership between human creativity and machine efficiency. So if you've been experimenting with agents and feeling frustrated, maybe the problem isn't the technology. Maybe it's the approach. Try a few of these principles. Orient your agents, manage their context, fix forward, trust them, scale them up, accept imperfection, streamline your version control, make their lives easier, and hold onto the things only humans can do. The future of coding isn't human or machine. It's both, working together.
