For years, there were programmes that allowed you to buy citizenship in the European Union, no strings attached. If you had a few million pounds spare, you could literally purchase an EU passport, complete with all the freedom of movement and benefits that came with it. Malta, Cyprus, and a handful of other countries had turned citizenship into a commodity, and the market was thriving. Then, in 2025, the Court of Justice of the European Union stepped in and changed everything.

The ruling was unambiguous. Citizenship, the court declared, is not a product you can sell over the counter. It's a status that carries profound meaning, rooted in genuine connection to a place, its culture, and its people. The so-called Golden Passport schemes were deemed incompatible with the fundamental values of the European Union. Countries that had been profiting from these programmes were given a very clear message: the era of transactional citizenship was over. No more shopping around for the best deal on an EU passport as though you were comparing smartphones.

What's fascinating, though, is what happened next. You might expect that killing off these programmes would satisfy the EU's desire to protect the integrity of citizenship. And in a sense, it did. But here's the thing – the demand didn't disappear. If anything, it intensified. People still want that freedom of movement, that layer of security for their families, the ability to structure their affairs more favourably, or simply the peace of mind that comes from having options. So when the official channels closed, where did all that demand go?

The answer lies in something far older and considerably more discreet than those glossy investment programmes ever were. It's called Citizenship by Merit, and it operates on a completely different principle. Rather than opening a wallet, you're expected to open doors – to bring something genuinely valuable to your chosen country. This isn't some loophole or grey market arrangement, either. It's what legal scholars call a sovereign prerogative, which simply means that a head of state, government, or parliament has the absolute discretion to grant citizenship to individuals who've made, or are likely to make, a significant contribution to the nation.

We're talking about business leaders who've created jobs and driven economic growth, scientists whose research has pushed boundaries, artists whose work has brought recognition internationally, or athletes who've put a country on the map. These aren't people buying their way in. They're people being invited in because of what they represent and what they can offer. The process isn't standardised, there are no fixed application forms or published fee schedules, and frankly, most people will never qualify. But for those who do – the exceptional, the accomplished, the genuinely remarkable – there's still a path to European citizenship that has nothing to do with investment and everything to do with merit.

This shift from investment to merit raises some genuinely interesting questions about what citizenship actually means. Is it about money, or is it about contribution? The EU's decision suggests it should be the latter, and Citizenship by Merit programmes reflect that philosophy. They remind us that belonging to a community, whether it's a city, a country, or a continent, shouldn't be transactional. It should be earned through what you bring to the table.

Of course, Critics will argue that this system favours the already privileged, and there's some truth in that. Not everyone has the platform to make themselves known to governments or the connections to catch a minister's attention. But defenders would counter that at least this approach is honest about what it's seeking – exceptional individuals rather than wealthy ones. There's a certain integrity in that distinction, even if the practical outcomes sometimes look similar.

As the dust settles on the EU's landmark ruling, one thing is clear: the world of citizenship is shifting. The flashy Golden Passport era may be over, but the underlying desire for international mobility, security, and opportunity isn't going anywhere. Citizenship by Merit offers one answer to that demand, and whether you see it as fair or flawed, it's likely to become an increasingly prominent feature of how nations think about who belongs to their club in the years ahead.
