In this episode you'll learn two things: how a simple cheese bread called chipa became Paraguay's most iconic food, and why drinking tereré is less about thirst and more about social ritual.

Let's start with chipa. If you've spent any time in Paraguay, you've seen chipa everywhere. Sold by vendors on street corners, at bus stations, outside churches, at every family gathering. It's a small, ring-shaped bread made from manioc flour — also called cassava or mandioca — mixed with eggs, cheese, milk, and anise. The result is dense, chewy, slightly salty, and utterly addictive.

Chipa has deep indigenous roots. The Guaraní people were making bread from manioc flour long before the Spanish arrived. What colonisation added was cheese and milk and eggs — European ingredients layered onto a native tradition. The result is something entirely Paraguayan, a fusion that tells the story of the country itself in a single bite.

There are many varieties. Chipa almidón is the classic version made with starch from the manioc root. Chipa guazú is a larger, softer corn-based version often served at asados. Chipa so'o has meat filling. Chipa caburé is cooked on a stick over an open flame. Every family has its own recipe, passed down through generations, and every Paraguayan will tell you their grandmother's chipa is the best.

Chipa is more than food — it's a cultural institution. During Semana Santa, the week before Easter, chipa production goes into overdrive. Families gather to make enormous batches. It's traditional, communal, and taken very seriously. Not making chipa during Holy Week in Paraguay would be like skipping Christmas dinner.

Now let's talk about tereré, because if chipa is Paraguay's food, tereré is Paraguay's soul. Tereré is a cold herbal infusion made by pouring ice-cold water over yerba mate, the same plant used for hot mate in Argentina and Uruguay. But in Paraguay, they drink it cold — and it's everywhere.

You cannot walk through Asunción without seeing people carrying their guampa, the cup, usually made from a hollowed ox horn, and their bombilla, the metal straw used to drink through. Tereré is consumed on the street, in offices, in parks, in cars, at construction sites, everywhere. It is Paraguay's national drink, and the country takes it so seriously that in 2020, UNESCO recognised tereré as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The herbs mixed into tereré are called remedios yuyos, and there are hundreds of them. Each has a different purpose — some for digestion, some for energy, some for relaxation, some for headaches. The person who prepares the tereré chooses the herbs based on what the group needs. It's part pharmacology, part social bonding.

And that's the real point. Tereré is fundamentally social. You don't drink it alone, or if you do, it's unusual. It's shared. One person pours, everyone drinks from the same guampa in a circle. There are unspoken rules: don't move the bombilla, don't say thank you until you're done drinking, pass it back promptly. Breaking these rules is a faux pas.

The government actually had to pass a law guaranteeing workers the right to drink tereré during the workday. That's how embedded it is. Imagine a country so devoted to a drink that parliament legislates your right to have it at work.

Paraguay's climate makes tereré practical too. Summer temperatures regularly exceed forty degrees. A hot cup of mate in that heat is punishing. Cold tereré is survival.

Together, chipa and tereré represent something important about Paraguayan culture: the ability to take simple, available ingredients — manioc, cheese, herbs, cold water — and transform them into rituals that bind communities together. They're not fancy. They're not expensive. But they're irreplaceable.

Today we learned that in Paraguay, the simplest things carry the deepest meaning. A ring of cheese bread and a cup of cold herbs tell you more about a country than any guidebook ever could.
