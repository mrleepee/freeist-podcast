At Jane Street, a quantitative trading firm that operates at the intersection of finance and software engineering, developers write almost exclusively in OCaml—a programming language most of us in the broader tech world rarely encounter. This isn't a minor detail. It shapes everything about how they've approached integrating large language models into their development workflow, and it turns out to be rather fascinating.

The challenge is that OCaml is obscure. There's limited public training data for it, which means Jane Street probably has more OCaml code in their repositories than exists in the entire public domain. They're operating in an environment where standard approaches simply don't apply. Their infrastructure is equally unconventional. They run a monorepo stored in Mercurial rather than Git, they've built their own build systems and a distributed build environment from scratch, and their internal code review system is called—rather charmingly—iron. Oh, and sixty-seven percent of their developers use EMACS as their primary editor. When you're working with these conditions, off-the-shelf solutions fall apart pretty quickly.

The team looked at Meta's Code Compose paper and thought, right, fine-tuning for company-specific languages does work. Their goal seemed straightforward: generate multi-file diffs from natural language prompts, sometimes up to a hundred lines, with clean application and type-checking. But here's the thing—they assumed it would be simple, and it absolutely wasn't. The breakthrough came when they realised they needed training examples in the exact shape of the outputs they wanted. Getting the format right turned out to be far more important than they initially thought.

Collecting suitable training data proved equally tricky. Feature branches were too verbose for editor use. Commits didn't work either—they're checkpoints, not isolated changes. Their solution was workspace snapshotting: capturing developer state roughly every twenty seconds, along with build status. They watched for patterns. Green to red to green suggests an isolated change. Red to green shows an error fix. These patterns helped them identify which snapshots represented the kind of clean, focused modifications worth training on. They even used language models to generate concise descriptions from more detailed change notes.

For evaluation, they built something called CES—the Code Evaluation Service. This pre-warms builds and tests model-generated diffs against their actual codebase. It checks parsing, type-checking, and whether tests pass. The same infrastructure serves both training and evaluation, which keeps everything consistent. There's a telling anecdote here: a code review model was asked to evaluate something and simply replied, "I'll do it tomorrow." That's a reminder of why meaningful evaluations matter so much. You need systems that catch laziness as well as errors.

The actual architecture is called AID, the AI Development Environment. It's a sidecar application that handles prompt construction, context building, and build status. Then there are thin integration layers for VS Code, EMACS, and NeoVim—write once, deploy everywhere. This design means you can swap models, change context strategies, or run A/B tests without restarting your editor. The UI differs per editor, naturally: a sidebar for VS Code, a markdown buffer for EMACS.

Looking ahead, they're interested in RAG implementations in editors, multi-agent workflows at scale, and integrating reasoning models. The focus remains on maintaining that pluggable architecture and building domain-specific tooling that actually works for their particular environment.

What's compelling about Jane Street's approach is that they've refused to accept "good enough." They've built infrastructure specifically for their needs, accepted that their unusual circumstances require unusual solutions, and created evaluation systems rigorous enough to catch problems before they reach production. For anyone working in specialized technical environments, their journey offers both inspiration and practical lessons.
