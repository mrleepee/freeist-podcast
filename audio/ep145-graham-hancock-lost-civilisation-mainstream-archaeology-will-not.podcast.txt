What if everything you were taught about the dawn of human civilisation was only half the story, and the other half was deliberately buried by an academic establishment that simply refuses to dig it up? The journalist and author Graham Hancock has been making exactly this case for roughly thirty years. In a long-form interview on the Diary of a Chief Executive podcast with Steven Bartlett, Hancock argued that a lost, advanced civilisation was wiped out around twelve thousand eight hundred years ago. He claims that mainstream archaeology has spent years actively refusing to take this idea seriously. He is the author of the book Fingerprints of the Gods and the presenter of the Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse. His theories are enormously popular. They are also strongly contested by professional archaeologists. The honest way to evaluate his claims is to look at exactly what Hancock argues versus what the physical evidence independently supports.

The spine of Hancock's argument rests on a period called the Younger Dryas. He claims that a comet impact around ten thousand eight hundred years before the Common Era erased a high prehistoric culture and thousands of years of human history along with it. We have to separate two things here to find the truth. The Younger Dryas itself was a sharp cold snap that occurred roughly twelve thousand nine hundred years ago. This is a real, well-established climatic event that mainstream science completely accepts. What is not established is Hancock's further claim that this specific event destroyed an advanced, globe-spanning civilisation. That second step is his thesis, not the field's conclusion, and that is where the controversy actually lives.

Hancock looks at the Great Pyramid and argues it encodes knowledge that humans should not have possessed for another two and a half thousand years. He points to certain old maps that appear to depict the coastline of Antarctica before it was officially discovered. He presents these as evidence that an earlier, sophisticated culture passed down information that survived a global catastrophe. Professional Egyptologists and historians of cartography have rebutted both of these lines in exacting detail. The maps in particular have well-documented, conventional explanations. These are Hancock's contentions, not settled facts.

But here is the catch. Hancock's portfolio is not entirely fringe. By the standards of mainstream research, his strongest argument is that parts of the Amazon rainforest are actually a human-made landscape. He argues that beneath the forest lie massive earthworks and dark, fertile soils created by large, organised pre-Columbian populations. This is not pseudoscience. There is a genuine and growing archaeological consensus that the pre-contact Amazon was far more densely populated and deliberately shaped by human hands than older assumptions ever allowed. Hancock is absolutely right to highlight this, even if he uses it to support a broader theory that the archaeological field does not share.

We have to look at his method and the pushback it generates. Hancock has openly said that roughly eighty ayahuasca experiences shaped his worldview. His method leans heavily on pattern-finding across distant cultures and alternative interpretations of ancient sites and myths. Critics argue this is precisely the problem. Connecting scattered anomalies into a single global narrative is exactly the kind of reasoning archaeology has learned to distrust. It is a framework that can explain anything, and therefore it explains nothing in a testable way. The mainstream counter-position is not that early humans were primitive. The position is that the physical evidence for a single lost global civilisation is simply not there. Extraordinary claims require evidence that survives excavation and rigorous dating, not just argument by analogy.

Why do people listen to him anyway? The interview reached a massive audience because Hancock is a gifted storyteller, and the underlying question is genuinely interesting. How much of early human history is actually missing, and how would we even know? The gaps in the historical record are real. Much of the human past was never written down. Coastlines have changed. Entire landscapes were lost when sea levels rose at the end of the last ice age. A careful person can acknowledge how much we genuinely do not know without accepting that a specific, advanced civilisation was erased by a comet.

The reason this matters is the structural question his popularity raises. When an unpopular thesis is dismissed as heresy by institutions, how do we tell the difference between an idea that is being suppressed and an idea that is simply unsupported? Hancock says orthodoxy closes ranks to protect its funding and authority. The alternative is that fields like archaeology have earned their consensus through methods specifically designed to resist persuasive storytelling. The honest work is holding both of those possibilities open at once and refusing to let either side declare the matter settled.
