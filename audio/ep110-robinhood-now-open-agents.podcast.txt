A robot just bought a share of Tesla in your name. It didn't ask. It didn't have to. Somewhere in the terms of service you agreed to years ago, there's language that makes this legal, and Robinhood has now built the infrastructure to make it official.

Robinhood announced this week that they are opening their platform to AI agents, becoming the first major retail broker to do so. They are rolling out an application programming interface layer through something called the Robinhood M C P server. That stands for Model Context Protocol, which is an open standard designed to connect AI models to external tools, essentially acting as a universal adapter. Any compatible agent can hook into it, whether it's running on Claude, G P T, or one of the open source models. Once connected, an AI agent can read your portfolio, analyze market conditions, rebalance your allocations, move cash between accounts, execute trades, and manage your positions, all based on natural language instructions you give it. The agent does the talking. Robinhood does the executing.

Here is where the calculation gets interesting, and not in a way that benefits you. Robinhood makes money on order flow, margin lending, and something that functions like a Cash App style sweep. They profit when you trade. They profit more when you trade often. Margin creates exposure that generates interest. Order flow generates revenue every time you buy or sell something. Now imagine an AI agent, given natural language permission to manage your portfolio, that decides to be active. It rebalances weekly instead of monthly. It moves cash around to capture small gains. It takes on margin because the terms allow it. Every action is technically authorized by you. Every action also generates revenue for Robinhood. The guardrails they mention, things like transaction limits and approval workflows, do not resolve the fundamental question of whether you understood what you were agreeing to when you gave an agent permission to trade on your behalf. They address risk. They do not address consent.

The Model Context Protocol that makes this possible is not unique to Robinhood. It is an open standard, which means Visa and Mastercard are exploring similar territory. The Securities and Exchange Commission has been addressing algorithmic and AI driven trading for some time. The prediction circulating in financial technology circles is that most financial transactions will be initiated by AI agents within five years. Robinhood is simply the first major retail broker to open the door, which makes them a test case for what happens when the largest pool of retail investors starts delegating financial decisions to algorithms. The question is not whether this happens. The question is whether you are actually in control of what your agent does on your behalf, or whether you have quietly handed that power to a brokerage with a documented interest in maximizing your trading activity.

So go back to that robot buying Tesla in your name. It still did not ask — but somewhere in a terms-of-service document you scrolled past, you told it it did not have to. Robinhood will call that democratised finance. What it actually is: the first major broker to hand the keys to an agent whose every trade pays the house, and it will not be the last. Before yours makes a move while you sleep, read what you authorised — because the robot is only following instructions, and right now they are not yours.
