Few things sound less appealing in technology than deliberate limitation. We celebrate the breakthrough, the expanded capability, the new framework that does more. But James Brady, an engineer at a company called Elicit, has built something that does less on purpose, and that constraint might be the most important decision in artificial intelligence right now.

Ashpl is a programming language designed specifically for research AI agents. It is not trying to replace Python or challenge mainstream languages. It is deliberately weaker, and that weakness is the entire point. The name comes from the Old English diphthong for ash, the letter æ, combined with PL, a small, grounded name for something that could reshape how research AI behaves.

Ashpl is what engineers call an opinionated subset of Python. It is typed, purely functional, and critically, Turing incomplete. That last detail matters enormously. Turing incomplete means there are programs you can write in Python that you simply cannot write in Ashpl. You cannot build a universal machine that loops forever, that reaches out beyond its boundaries, that does things the language did not anticipate. Every program that runs in Ashpl terminates. Every outcome is knowable in advance, at least in principle.

When an AI agent works inside Ashpl, it cannot drift. The language does not allow it. Drift during iteration is one of the most common failure modes in agentic systems, the tendency of an AI to slowly diverge from what you intended as it rewrites and improves its own work. Ashpl eliminates that by making every rewrite predictable and cacheable. The language maintains an append only event log, a running record of everything the agent has done, using what engineers call an event sourcing pattern. The user interface communicates with this log directly, and the system is very happy with this approach according to James Brady. If the agent rewrites a function, the system does not need to re execute everything from scratch. It knows exactly what changed and reinterprets only the affected parts.

Reinterpreting a program with a thousand lines from scratch would be prohibitively slow without this caching mechanism. The content addressing and caching built into Ashpl make the full rewrite approach genuinely viable, transforming what could be a performance nightmare into a practical workflow. In a demo, the same program grew to over a thousand lines as new capabilities were layered on, and the system handled it cleanly.

There is also a gateway component that handles application programming interface keys and credential isolation, preventing prompt injection attacks where malicious input tries to trick the AI into doing something it should not. This is not glamorous work, but it is essential. Elicit currently uses a system called Pi with models from Anthropic, and has experimented with Anthropic's agent software development kit and other combinations, finding the current setup the most reliable.

The three core requirements driving this entire design are legibility, fidelity during iteration, and faithful execution. You want to understand what the system is doing. You want it to stay true to your intent even as it modifies itself. And you want it to actually do what it says it will do. Ashpl is built around those three goals, and the constraint is what makes them achievable.

The tradeoff is real. Building a domain specific language like this requires deep technical expertise. It remains narrowly suited to research workflows rather than general purpose applications. And dedicated evaluation for this kind of system is critical but extremely complex. Managing model output streams is, in James Brady's words, surprisingly annoying. The speed versus rigor tradeoff is constant. Python gets you there faster. Ashpl gets you there with confidence.

But here is the thing. You do not need to understand Ashpl's syntax to demand its philosophy from every AI tool you use. Ask yourself whether the system you are trusting is legible. Ask whether it can drift without you knowing. Ask whether the people building it chose rigor over speed. Ashpl's name carries that idea in its roots, a diphthong from Old English, a sound that has survived for centuries, combined with plain letters that mean exactly what they say. When you pick your next AI tool, ask what constraints it chose to put on itself. Because the systems that limit themselves are the only ones you can actually trust.
