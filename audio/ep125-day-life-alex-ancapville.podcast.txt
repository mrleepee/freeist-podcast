Imagine waking up in a town where nobody pays taxes. Not a single penny. Not to fund roads, schools, or police. That place is called AnCapville, and today we're going inside one ordinary day there, through the eyes of someone called Alex. What makes this thought experiment genuinely unsettling isn't the absence of government, it's how much of what you already accept would simply stop making sense.

Let's start with the morning. Alex walks to a coffee shop, but this isn't just any coffee shop. Every purchase flows through an online reputation platform, a system where buyers and sellers rate each other publicly. The coffee tastes good because the roaster has an incentive to maintain a five-star rating. Nobody from a regulatory agency inspects the beans. The market does it instead, through insurance incentives and repeat custom. If a roaster delivers a bad product, their reputation score tanks, their insurance premiums spike, and they lose access to the platform entirely. The Federal Drug Administration doesn't exist here. Neither does its budget, its staff, or its revolving door with the industry it supposedly oversees.

Now, the roads. AnCapville's streets are privately owned, which means they operate like toll highways even when they're neighbourhood lanes. Risk-based pricing means reckless drivers pay higher premiums, and if someone damages your car, you receive actual restitution from their insurer. Not a traffic ticket payable to the state. Not a fine that vanishes into general revenue. Real money, from the person who broke your vehicle. The incentive structure is completely inverted. State traffic enforcement punishes behaviour to fund the state. Private roads reward safe driving because safe drivers are cheaper to insure, which makes every road owner more profitable.

Alex's children don't attend a state school. They go to a micro-school co-operative run by parents. The curriculum isn't mandated by a central authority. It's assembled by demand. Parents who want rigorous academics choose one provider. Families who prefer project-based learning choose another. Teachers compete on outcomes and reviews, not on compliance with a bureaucratic syllabus designed by people who haven't been inside a classroom in twenty years. If a micro-school underperforms, parents withdraw their children and their funding. The school closes. Accountability without a ministry.

The afternoon brings a dispute. Alex's neighbour claims a fence was built on the wrong boundary. In the state system, this means lawyers, court fees, potentially years of proceedings, and a judge who may or may not understand property law. In AnCapville, both parties already chose an arbitration firm when they bought their properties, as part of the contract. They present evidence. The arbitrator issues a binding ruling. The loser pays. No endless appeals, no tactical delays, no industry of litigation funded by people who just want their actual problem solved.

Security works the same way. Multiple private protection agencies compete for business. Their performance is public knowledge. Bad agencies get dropped by their insurers, which makes them financially unviable. Good agencies expand. Nobody has a monopoly on force, so nobody can bully their way into your neighbourhood. The moment an agency misbehaves, its reputation score collapses and clients cancel their contracts. This is not theoretical. It is market discipline applied to something nobody wants to think about until they need it.

Money in AnCapville is mostly gold-backed digital currency. No central bank printing money to fund deficits. No gradual erosion of purchasing power through inflation. The purchasing power you earn this year is roughly the same purchasing power next year. The inflation tax, which is a polite name for the government quietly stealing value from your savings, simply doesn't exist here. Your savings are yours.

Now, the difficult part. Alex's cousin Jake makes a terrible mistake and commits a violent crime. In AnCapville, this is treated as a civil matter, a tort against the victim. The victim's insurer pays damages immediately, then pursues restitution from Jake. Jake is apprehended non-lethally, because shooting him eliminates his ability to earn income and pay back what he owes. He works off his debt in a private labour centre that competes with other centres on humane conditions, because families won't patronise facilities that mistreat their relatives. This is not punishment in the traditional sense. It is accountability through restitution. The victim is made whole. The offender works to satisfy the debt. Nobody profits from warehousing human beings.

There are obvious problems. Reputation scores can be manipulated. Communities with strict covenants can become exclusionary. People who refuse to participate in the contractual system find themselves frozen out, their access to services and property evaporating because nobody will insure them or trade with them. Sovereignty requires discipline, and not everyone arrives with the knowledge and resources to build it. The system works best for people who already understand contracts, insurance, and market mechanics.

That is precisely why it remains rare. Information asymmetry is the state's greatest recruiting tool. The more complex a system appears, the more people beg for someone to manage it for them. AnCapville is not a utopia. It is a different set of trade-offs, where the responsibility you shed in a state system comes with new obligations and new choices. Whether that exchange appeals to you depends on whether you trust yourself more than you trust the people who currently hold the monopoly. That is the real question this place forces you to answer.
