Some developers are already spending one point three million dollars per month on AI coding tools. That is not a typo or a rounding error. It is the kind of number that makes you stop and wonder what exactly they are building, and more importantly, why the rest of us are not there yet. The answer, it turns out, is a fundamental shift in how we think about getting software written.

For the past couple of years, the dominant approach has been prompt engineering. You give an AI agent a task, it responds, you refine, you iterate, and eventually something useful emerges. It is conversational, it is hands-on, and it scales poorly. The next evolution goes by a few names, but loop engineering captures it best. Instead of guiding AI step by step, developers now design autonomous loops that continuously work toward a defined goal until that goal is met. You set the target, the AI runs, checks its own progress, adjusts, and keeps going.

The mechanics are straightforward. Every loop needs a trigger and a goal. The trigger is what starts everything rolling, whether that is a pull request being opened in a repository, a scheduled time, or someone manually kicking things off. The goal is the verifiable end state. That might mean tests passing, a feature being complete, or a specification being fulfilled. Once the loop starts, agents receive a high-level objective and iterate autonomously. The critical difference between this and simple automation is the decision point built into every cycle. Did we reach the goal? If not, continue iterating. That feedback loop, powered by either deterministic checks like test results or more abstract determinations made by the language model itself, is what separates a loop from a simple script.

In practice, tools are already implementing this. Cursor, the AI-enhanced code editor, has an automations tab that lets developers set up trigger-based loops, for example automatically reviewing pull requests the moment they open. Google Cloud Code offers a loop command that runs prompts on recurring intervals. These are early examples, but they point toward where things are heading.

Here is the catch, and it is a significant one. Defining abstract end goals turns out to be remarkably difficult. Something like build this feature sounds simple until you try to encode it in a way that lets the AI know when it is actually done. Then there is the cost. Running these loops at any meaningful scale chews through tokens at an alarming rate. That one point three million dollar monthly figure floating around? It belongs to early adopters running complex loops on large codebases. Most teams cannot justify that expense, which means loop engineering remains concentrated among organisations with what you might call infinite token budgets.

The vision for the future is striking though. Engineers gradually transition from prompting agents to designing what some call autonomous factories. You are no longer in the loop manually; you are designing the loop itself. The more ambitious version of this vision involves AI designing its own loops, a form of recursive self-improvement that sounds either exciting or terrifying depending on your tolerance for delegation. That stage is firmly in the realm of speculation at the moment, not deployment.

What makes this worth paying attention to is the implication for software development as a discipline. The skill shifts from writing code to writing systems that write code. Whether that is liberation or obsolescence depends entirely on how quickly you adapt. The tools exist. The costs are coming down. The question is whether you are designing the factory or still manually hammering together prompts.
