A few hundred people on a river in the Brazilian Amazon. No exact numbers, no priests, no creation myth. We have been looking at how indigenous language shapes what the mind can do. Last time, Guaraní outlived an empire in Paraguay. Now we go further, into what happens when a language strips away the cognitive furniture that every expert class insists is non-negotiable.

The Pirahã are hunter-gatherers along the Maici River. They have no number words beyond rough terms for few and many. No pantheon, no abstract deities, no strong tradition of representational drawing or decorative art. Fiercely egalitarian, with little formal hierarchy. And for decades, they have been the subject of a fight that reaches all the way into the foundations of modern linguistics.

The fight centres on a linguist named Daniel Everett. He lived among the Pirahã for decades, first as a missionary, later as a secular scientist. He laid out his most provocative claims in a two thousand eight book called "Don't Sleep, There Are Snakes," and in academic papers including one titled "Recursion and Human Thought: Why the Pirahã Don't Have Numbers." His argument struck at something the entire field had treated as settled.

Noam Chomsky's theory of Universal Grammar holds that recursion, the ability to nest clauses inside other clauses, is the defining feature of human language. It is supposed to be present in every human mind. Everett argued that Pirahã grammar shows no evidence of recursion. No embedded clauses. No syntactic nesting. He linked the absence of recursion to the absence of counting, and suggested both stem from a deeper cognitive commitment to what he calls immediate experience.

The response was swift and hostile. In two thousand nine, three linguists named Nevins, Pesetsky, and Rodrigues published a rebuttal arguing that recursion does exist in Pirahã and that Everett had overread his data. That debate remains unresolved as of the mid-twenty twenties. Treat the recursion and no-numbers claims as Everett's much-debated thesis, not established fact.

But here is what is not debated. In two thousand four, a researcher named Peter Gordon published an experiment in the journal Science showing that Pirahã speakers struggled to match quantities of objects beyond two or three once those items were hidden from view. They track quantities approximately, through relative terms. They do not count. Everett describes them as living almost entirely in immediate experience, anchored to what is directly perceived rather than what is abstractly imagined.

Every time someone tells you society needs managers, gatekeepers, or a grand architectural plan to function, they are asserting that human flourishing has one blueprint. The Pirahã are living proof it doesn't. A society can be cohesive, adaptable, humour-filled, and socially rich without formal numbers, without a priestly class, without the kind of grammar a Chomskyan would predict. If that is possible, then the systems you are told are non-negotiable, institutional, linguistic, economic, are negotiable. The menu of possible human lives is wider than the experts admit.

The Pirahã don't count past two or three. Don't nest their clauses. Don't build hierarchies. Don't seem to miss any of it. A few hundred people on a river, living proof that the blueprint is not the building. So the next time someone tells you the system is load-bearing, ask them whose weight it's holding.
