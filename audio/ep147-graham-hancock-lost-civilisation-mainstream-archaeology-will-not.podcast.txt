Imagine a scientific field that has spent decades building a timeline of human history, only to have a journalist with no formal credentials tell millions of people that the entire discipline is hiding a massive truth about an advanced society wiped out by a comet. Graham Hancock has been making this case for nearly thirty years, most recently on Steven Bartlett's Diary of a Chief Executive podcast. Hancock is the author of Fingerprints of the Gods and the presenter of the Netflix series Ancient Apocalypse. His theories draw enormous audiences and equally fierce resistance from professional archaeologists. The honest way to evaluate his claims is to look at exactly what he argues versus what the physical evidence independently supports.

Hancock's central thesis is that a comet impact during the Younger Dryas period erased a highly advanced global civilisation around ten thousand eight hundred years before the common era. You have to separate two distinct things here. The Younger Dryas itself, a sharp and sudden cold snap that occurred roughly twelve thousand nine hundred years ago, is a real and well-established climatic event that mainstream science fully accepts. What is not established is Hancock's further claim that this climatic shift destroyed an advanced, globe-spanning culture. That second leap is his thesis, not the field's conclusion, and it is where the friction actually lives.

He argues that the Great Pyramid of Giza encodes mathematical and astronomical knowledge that humans should not have possessed for another two and a half thousand years. He also points to old maps that he claims depict the coastline of Antarctica before it was officially discovered by modern explorers. He presents these as evidence that an earlier, highly sophisticated culture passed down information that survived the planetary catastrophe. Professional Egyptologists and historians of cartography have rebutted both of these lines in exacting detail. The maps in particular have well-documented, conventional explanations that do not require a lost civilisation to make sense of them.

But here is the part where Hancock's research actually aligns with mainstream archaeology. He highlights that parts of the Amazon rainforest are a completely human-made landscape. He argues that beneath the forest canopy lie massive earthworks and dark, nutrient-rich soils known as terra preta, left behind by large, organised pre-Columbian populations. This is not fringe science. There is a genuine and growing archaeological consensus that the pre-contact Amazon was far more densely populated and deliberately shaped by human hands than older assumptions ever allowed. Hancock is entirely correct to highlight this, even if he uses it to support a broader theory that the scientific field does not share.

His methods and his motivations draw the heaviest fire. Hancock has openly stated that roughly eighty ayahuasca experiences shaped his worldview. His methodology leans heavily on pattern-finding across distant cultures and alternative interpretations of ancient sites. Critics argue this is precisely the problem. Connecting scattered anomalies into a single global narrative is the kind of reasoning archaeology has learned to distrust, because a framework that can explain anything ultimately explains nothing in a testable way. The mainstream counter-position is not that early humans were primitive. It is that the evidence for a single lost global civilisation simply is not there, and that extraordinary claims require evidence that survives rigorous excavation and carbon dating rather than argument by analogy.

The interview reached a massive audience because Hancock is a gifted storyteller, and because the underlying question is genuinely fascinating. How much of early human history is actually missing, and how would we know if we got it wrong? The gaps in the archaeological record are real. Much of the human past was never written down. Coastlines have shifted dramatically, and entire landscapes were lost when sea levels rose at the end of the last ice age. A careful person can acknowledge how much we genuinely do not know without accepting that a specific, advanced civilisation was wiped out by a comet.

The reason this matters beyond ancient history is the structural question his popularity raises about institutional authority. When an unpopular thesis is dismissed as heresy by the establishment, how do we tell the difference between an idea that is being actively suppressed and an idea that is simply unsupported by the facts? Hancock's answer is that orthodoxy closes ranks to protect its funding and prestige. The alternative is that fields like archaeology have earned their consensus through methods specifically designed to resist exactly the kind of persuasive storytelling Hancock sells. The honest work is holding both of those possibilities open at once and refusing to let either side declare the matter settled.
