When an AI system gives you an answer, do you ever wonder what happened inside that black box to produce it? Most people don't. They see the output, judge it, and move on. But here's a question worth sitting with: two systems could return identical results, yet one might be trustworthy and the other a confidence trick wrapped in statistics. The mechanism behind an answer matters as much as the answer itself. That's the premise driving some fascinating work at Elicit, a research AI company that recently developed their own domain-specific language called Ashpl, and the thinking behind it deserves a closer look.

Elicit wasn't satisfied with just building capable AI. They wanted verifiable AI. When you're dealing with scientific research or high-stakes decision-making, "good enough" isn't good enough. The team realised that if they wanted their systems to be genuinely trustworthy, they needed to control not just what the models produced, but how they produced it. That meant building their own tooling rather than relying on generic frameworks.

Their approach centred on three non-negotiable requirements. First, legibility: the system's reasoning should be readable, almost spot-checkable, by users or other systems. If you can't inspect the process, you can't verify it. Second, fidelity during iteration: as new requirements got layered in or directions changed, the system shouldn't drift from the original intent. Third, faithful execution: the plan on paper should actually match what happened in practice.

Ashpl is the result. It's a reactive, purely functional language that functions as an opinionated subset of Python with research-specific primitives built in. Think commands for fetching academic papers or clinical trials baked into the language itself. Crucially, it has no loops, no recursion, no mutation. This constraint isn't a limitation; it's a feature. By eliminating these common sources of unpredictability, the language becomes far easier to reason about and verify.

The workflow operates as a constant loop. A component called the curator writes Ashpl code based on user input. A Python service then parses, validates, and interprets that code. If errors surface or new requirements emerge, the system redrafts the code and runs through the process again. Each artifact or table generated comes with its underlying Ashpl code visible, and the graphical representation of the workflow is derived directly from that code, so users can always see what they're actually working with.

Technically, they leaned into content addressing for caching and memoization, which means repeated sections execute almost instantly rather than recalculating everything. Event sourcing handles distributed data, and credential isolation through a gateway prevents user input from reaching sensitive systems. They built a wrapper to swap between different model harnesses and invested heavily in session rehydration capabilities so contexts could be reconstructed cleanly.

For organisations considering similar approaches, their recommendations are practical: base your domain-specific language on an existing language so models learn the syntax more easily, implement proper interrupt handling so user input can flow back into running processes, and treat credential isolation as non-negotiable. They also emphasise that a dedicated evaluation team matters enormously, even though it's complex to build.

The broader lesson here cuts through the hype. In an environment where AI capabilities are announced weekly and benchmarks get massaged into marketing, Elicit's bet is that rigour and transparency will eventually win trust where flash cannot. Whether that pays off at scale remains to be seen, but the idea that how results are produced should matter as much as the results themselves feels like the right foundation to build on.
