Dan Martell wants you to build a billion-dollar business alone. We have been tracking what happens when AI leverage meets solo entrepreneurship. We looked at the Kimi founder's approach to artificial intelligence. Now we go one step further into what happens when a single founder tries to automate an entire company.

Martell is a serial SaaS founder, an investor, and the author of "Buy Back Your Time." His framework for the AI-leveraged business is a three-level ladder. Each rung replaces a different kind of human effort with a system. The structure is sound. He integrates Eliyahu Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, a serious piece of operations thinking from the book "The Goal." Goldratt's argument is simple. Every business has exactly one binding constraint at a time. Martell's prescription is to find that bottleneck and remove it.

Level one is the starting move. You do not build software first. You do not build an audience first. You sell a done-for-you service that uses AI to remove a specific bottleneck for a client. The efficiency is real. But here is the catch. AI executes. It does not provide vision, taste, or relationships. Judgement, trust, and context remain the work that is hard for computers and easy for humans.

Level two is what Martell calls the Replacement Ladder. You automate in a specific order. Administrative work goes first. Then service delivery. Then marketing. But sales, the highest-leverage seat and the one most tied to trust, stays deliberately in human hands as the last thing you hand off. This is where the framework earns its keep. It is built around human-in-the-loop quality control. Martell uses a ten-eighty-ten rule. The human does the first ten percent. That is framing and direction. The system does the middle eighty percent. That is execution. The human does the final ten percent. That is review and judgement.

If you are building AI agents, apply that ten-eighty-ten split to your own harness pattern. Have your system read a markdown file called soul dot m d for the initial ten percent framing. Let the model execute the middle eighty percent. Then force a hard human review gate for the final ten percent before any output ships.

Level three is where Martell claims brand is the real asset. His logic is straightforward. Brand is association. Association is trust. Trust allows a business to grow without the founder standing in every single transaction. The limitation here is not the framework itself. No framework accounts for market timing, execution, or the sheer platform risk of building entirely on another company's models and app stores.

Most of the difficulty in building a business is not in knowing the framework. It is in execution, market dynamics, and luck. An AI-leveraged business running entirely on another company's models, cloud infrastructure, and app store has bought efficiency but rented its foundations. The part of Martell's framework that points toward real independence is the piece that resists outsourcing everything. Human judgement in the loop. Direct relationships. The brand you own.

Every founder chasing the one-person billion-dollar company dream is building on infrastructure they do not control. Before you hand the keys to a model you do not own, ask yourself what you are standing on. The efficiency you bought is real. The foundation you rented might not be. Own what the system cannot replace, or do not be surprised when the system replaces you.
