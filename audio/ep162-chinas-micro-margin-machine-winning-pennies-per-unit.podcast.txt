Fifteen cents. That is the retail price of a small manufactured product sourced from a Chinese factory in a city you have probably never heard of. The profit margin on that item is under one cent per unit. That claim comes from a sourcing consultant named Eric who operates in China and posts under the handle Eric Cracks China. He published a video titled "The Fifteen Cent Miracle: The Most Ruthless Supply Chain on Earth." Eric sells sourcing services, so he has a commercial interest in making the system look powerful. The under-one-cent profit figure is a characterization of a business model, not an audited financial statement from a specific factory. But the underlying pattern is real, and it is worth understanding precisely because every product you touch, and every supply chain decision made by the companies you depend on, is shaped by it.

The conventional explanation for Chinese manufacturing dominance is cheap labor. That explanation is roughly fifteen years out of date. Chinese manufacturing wages rose sharply between two thousand five and twenty sixteen. By the mid-two thousands tens, Chinese manufacturing wages had surpassed those in Mexico and Brazil. China retained and grew its manufacturing dominance anyway. If wages determined everything, production would have left. It did not.

Here is what happened instead. Cities like Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Yiwu developed dense industrial clusters where suppliers, component manufacturers, assemblers, and packaging facilities sit within kilometers of each other. That cluster density enables prototyping and iteration measured in days rather than months. Vertically integrated supply chains span from raw materials through to final assembly and packaging within a single region. When you can walk a prototype across the street to a supplier who adjusts a mold overnight, your iteration speed is not competing with another country's wage rate. It is competing with another country's calendar.

Volume does the rest. Massive production runs drive per-unit fixed costs down toward zero on each individual item. A fraction of a cent in profit multiplied across tens of millions of units produces enormous aggregate profit. The margin per unit is not the point. The margin per million units is the point. Add export tax rebates and coordinated industrial planning into the mix, and you have a system where the state is actively subsidizing the conditions that make those micro margins viable.

Now, if you are building agentic AI systems, there is a direct parallel worth stealing here. The Chinese cluster model works because iteration cycles collapse to days. Your agent evaluation harness should do the same thing. Build a continuous evaluation pipeline that runs your agent against a fixed test suite on every single change, so you measure iteration speed in minutes, not in scheduled manual reviews. Volume kills cost in manufacturing, and volume kills uncertainty in agentic engineering. Run hundreds of evaluations per build and the per-run cost approaches zero while the signal quality compounds.

The Chinese model is not immortal. Low-value, labor-intensive manufacturing has already begun migrating to Vietnam and Bangladesh following what economists call the flying geese pattern, where production hops from country to country chasing lower wages. But China is increasingly competitive in medium and higher-value manufacturing, where ecosystem depth matters more than wage rates. The factories that remain are the ones where proximity, integration, and scale create a moat that cheap labor alone cannot replicate.

The fifteen-cent miracle is not magic. It is what happens when an entire region coordinates around one proposition: volume kills cost, and proximity kills time. The next time you pick up something cheap and ubiquitous, remember it cost less than a cent of profit to make. Are you building anything that ruthlessly efficient, or just paying for someone else who did?
