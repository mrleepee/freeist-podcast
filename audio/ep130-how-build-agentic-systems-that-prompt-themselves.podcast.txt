Here's a shocker for you: most of the AI coding assistants sitting on developers' machines right now are essentially blindfolded. They can't see your team chat, your C I slash C D pipelines, your production dashboards, or that runbook you wrote three months ago that explains exactly why a certain architectural decision was made. They're working with a fraction of the information you'd give a human colleague sitting next to you. And the industry keeps pretending this isn't a problem.

The real shift happening in software engineering isn't just adopting AI tools. It's realising your job has fundamentally changed. You're no longer just writing code yourself. You're building "clones of yourself" that can operate at scale across dozens of agents simultaneously. That means thinking seriously about three things: what your agents can access, what they know, and what tools they have at their disposal.

Access is the obvious one that gets overlooked. A developer bounces between Slack threads, GitHub pull requests, monitoring dashboards, and internal wikis dozens of times a day. Each of those interactions carries context that explains not just what the code does, but why it does it. If your agent can't reach those sources, it's working blind. The advice from the Cloud Code team is practical: spend an entire day tracking every tool you actually use while working. Then connect your agent to all of them. You'll be amazed how quickly the quality of suggestions improves.

Knowledge is trickier because institutional memory can't be trained into a model. Fine-tuning sounds appealing, but the frontier models evolve so quickly that the cost-benefit calculation rarely works out. The smarter approach is in-context learning, which is a fancy term for making everything a text file your agent reads. Conventions, recent changes, internal application programming interface definitions, team vocabulary. All of it lives in documents, and that's where it should stay.

Then there's tooling. Think of an agent out of the box like using an ancient text editor versus a modern integrated development environment. Basic edit commands only. No syntax highlighting, no intelligent completions, no red squiggly warnings when something looks wrong. The Cloud Code team makes a useful distinction here: some tools compensate for a lack of intelligence, while others scale with intelligence. You want the second kind. Tools that give agents faster feedback loops do more for performance than swapping in a smarter model.

One constraint that keeps getting ignored is the context window. Frontier models have plateaued around one million tokens, which sounds enormous until you realise every customisation you add eats into that budget. The comparison someone made that stuck with me is running Node Package Manager on an Arduino. Extremely limited space. You have to be intentional about what goes in and what stays out. A related gotcha: if you change anything early in a long prompt, the entire cache for subsequent tokens invalidates, making everything ten times more expensive to process. The rule of thumb is keeping stable, shared information at the front and volatile per-task details near the end.

On the abstraction side, the key primitives worth understanding are the Model Context Protocol, skills, hooks, and agents. The Model Context Protocol works well for simple chatbots or for shipping integrations to non-technical customers. For internal developer tooling, skills are more practical. A skill is essentially a folder with a markdown file inside and a one-line description in the front matter. Easy to create, easy to maintain. The trade-off is that descriptions always load, and reliably triggering a specific skill can take three to four hundred tokens. There's no hierarchy system yet for lazy-loading subskills, though that's apparently on the roadmap.

The fastest way to improve your agent isn't a bigger model. It's tighter feedback loops and intentional context management. Build the infrastructure that lets your agents see what you see, know what you know, and iterate on their own work without waiting for you to catch every mistake.
