The most expensive mistake in software engineering right now is chasing the newest artificial intelligence model while ignoring the scaffolding around it. Developers are obsessing over which model has the longest context window or the best benchmark scores. Matt Pocock's thesis flips that entirely. He argues that you should split your effort down the middle: spend fifty percent of your time on the model, and fifty percent on what he calls the harness.

The harness is everything that touches the model. Your prompts. Your skills. The environment the model runs in. The files you feed it. Pocock's claim is that a mediocre model with an excellent harness will outperform a frontier model with a sloppy one. But let's be clear about what this is. This is Pocock's opinion, and the debate is live. The people actually building these models, the researchers at the frontier labs, argue the opposite. They say raw capability is still the bottleneck, and that once models get smart enough, the harness will not matter. Both sides have a point. But here is the angle that matters to you: whoever owns the harness owns the leverage. A model you cannot steer through your own harness is just rented intelligence. You are paying for access to someone else's brain, and the moment they change the terms, raise the price, or decide your use case violates a policy, you are out of business. The harness is how you build sovereignty into your workflow.

Pocock draws a sharp line between tactical and strategic programming. Tactical programming is syntax. It is writing boilerplate, fixing minor bugs, remembering whether a function takes three arguments or four. Artificial intelligence has eaten tactical programming completely. If you are still doing that work by hand, you are burning time you will never get back. But strategic programming is where the money is. Strategic programming is architecture. It is scoping out interfaces before you write a single line of code. It is designing a codebase that a human, or a machine, can navigate and change without creating a mess. Pocock's point is that developers must shift up. The tactical layer is automated. Your value is in the strategic layer, in the decisions about what gets built and how the pieces fit together.

This is where Pocock gets specific about skills, and it is one of the most practical parts of his framework. He distinguishes between procedural skills and ability skills. Ability skills are things the artificial intelligence invokes automatically. You load up a plugin, and the model decides on its own when to use a web search tool or a code execution environment. Pocock is skeptical of these. His argument is that too many ability skills clutter the context window. The model gets confused about what it is supposed to do and when. Procedural skills are different. These are skills you invoke intentionally. You tell the model to run a specific procedure. Pocock gives the example of a grill me skill, where you tell the artificial intelligence to interrogate you on a topic, to test your knowledge, and to refuse to let you off the hook until you have demonstrated mastery. That is procedural. You are in control. You decide when it runs and what it does. Pocock prefers procedural skills because they keep the human in the driver's seat.

His Teach Skill is the best example of this philosophy in action. It is a custom procedure that turns the artificial intelligence into a stateful, personalised tutor. Stateful means it remembers where you are. It does not just dump information on you. It builds a local curriculum. It figures out what you already know and what you do not know, and it fills in the gaps. It generates lessons as web pages, complete with quizzes, and it adapts as you progress. You could use it to learn how to solve a Rubik's Cube, or you could use it to learn full-stack engineering. The point is that the skill is yours. It runs on your terms. It is not a generic chatbot giving you a generic answer. It is a structured learning environment that you control.

Now, the away-from-keyboard queue. This is where Pocock's thinking gets genuinely interesting, and where it diverges from a lot of the hype. The current trend in artificial intelligence is agentic loops. You give the model a goal, and it runs in a loop, taking actions, checking results, and iterating until it finishes or until it runs out of tokens. Pocock thinks this is a terrible idea in practice. It burns through application programming interface credits. It creates unpredictable side effects. Instead, he advocates for treating artificial intelligence tasks as a managed queue. You set up specific, scoped tasks. You run them in secure sandboxes, like his tool Sandcastle, or through GitHub Actions. The tasks run while you are away from the keyboard. When you come back, you review the results. You are the manager. The artificial intelligence is the worker. But you do not let the worker run unsupervised with an open-ended mandate. You scope the work, you review the work, and you decide what gets merged.

There is a deeper principle here that Pocock touches on, and it is worth pulling into the light. When artificial intelligence finds a deep security bug, the instinct is to celebrate. You found the bug, you fixed the bug, move on. Pocock says no. When you find a deep bug, you build a system to catch it next time. You write a daily scheduled task that checks for that specific vulnerability going forward. This is the difference between fixing problems and building systems. The harness is not just about making the model work better today. It is about creating an environment that gets more robust over time. Every bug the artificial intelligence finds is an opportunity to harden your entire setup.

But here is the thing Pocock is honest about, and it is the thing that separates the hype from the reality. Artificial intelligence does not change the core fundamentals of building a business. You still need to talk to customers. You still need to understand what they actually want. You still need to make strategic decisions about what features to build and what to ignore. The artificial intelligence cannot generate the original product vision for you. It cannot tell you what the market needs. It can help you build it faster, but it cannot tell you what to build. The fundamentals remain. Talk to users. Understand the problem. Build the thing that solves it.

If you want to put Pocock's framework into practice, his advice is to start with a blank slate. Delete every plugin. Delete every external server connection. Delete every configuration file. Strip it all back to the bare model. Watch how it behaves with nothing attached. Then, and only then, start adding procedural skills back in, one at a time, as you identify a specific need. Build the harness deliberately. The leverage is not in the model. The leverage is in the scaffolding you build around it, and the control you exercise over every layer of the stack.
