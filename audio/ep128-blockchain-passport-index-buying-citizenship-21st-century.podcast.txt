On June the first, twenty twenty-six, a company called twenty one C B I published a ranking of eighty-seven countries. They called it the Bitcoin Passport Index, and at the top of the list sat El Salvador with a score of eighty-four point forty-five. Malta came second at eighty point fifty, falling nearly four points behind. If you were scrolling through this index trying to decide where in the world to build your life, those numbers might look neutral, scientific even. They are not.

The index evaluates countries across six categories. Visa-free travel access makes up twenty percent of the total score. Citizenship and residency by investment programmes account for fifteen percent. Dual citizenship posture and personal and economic freedom each carry ten percent. That leaves forty-five percent of the entire ranking dedicated to Bitcoin-specific factors: twenty-five percent for Bitcoin and cryptocurrency tax policy, and twenty percent for the legal and regulatory framework around cryptocurrency. Nearly half the score measures how friendly a country is to one specific digital asset. The other fifty-five percent covers everything else.

Here is what makes that weighting worth scrutinising. twenty one C B I operates as a division of a company called Bitcitizen L L C. Bitcitizen sells citizenship and residency by investment programmes. Its business model depends on people believing that certain jurisdictions are better than others for holding cryptocurrency, incorporating blockchain businesses, and obtaining second passports through investment. The Bitcoin Passport Index does not just report on this world. It shapes it. When El Salvador lands at number one, Bitcitizen can point to that ranking when selling its Freedom Passport programme, which launched in December twenty twenty-three.

The index was not written by an independent academic body. It was written by someone with twelve years of experience shaping United States immigration policy at the Department of Homeland Security. Adam Juchniewicz holds a Master of Laws in European and Comparative Law from the University of Malta and served as a retired United States Air Force veteran before co-authoring this ranking. His expertise in immigration is genuine. His stake in cryptocurrency adoption is not hidden so much as assumed to be irrelevant. It is not.

None of this means El Salvador has no genuine appeal. The nation made Bitcoin legal tender in twenty twenty-one through its Bitcoin Law, becoming the first country in the world to do so. Its state treasury holds roughly seven thousand five hundred Bitcoin. These are real facts with real consequences for people who live and invest there. But an index that allocates forty-five percent of its score to a single asset class will always rank El Salvador favourably, not because the country scores well on broader measures of quality of life, but because the scorecard was designed to reward exactly what El Salvador has done.

The visa-free access figure matters here. El Salvador's passport grants roughly one hundred and thirty-one destinations without a prior visa. That number is respectable but unremarkable. Switzerland, the United Arab Emirates, and Singapore all offer significantly broader access. If visa-free travel were the primary measure, El Salvador would not be first. It is first because the methodology prioritises what the people who built the index want to sell.

Before you use any passport index to guide your decisions about where to live, work, or move your assets, ask who funded it and what they sell. An index is not a mirror. It is a tool, and every tool has a hand behind it. El Salvador landed at number one because forty-five percent of the vote was Bitcoin-shaped, and the ruler was built by people who profit from certain answers. Your freedom to move across borders is being scored by guests who also chose the menu. Next time a ranking tells you where sovereignty lives, find out who is holding the pen. The passport that gets you into one hundred and thirty-one countries came in first because the scorecard was written by the people selling the programme.
