Claude Code has been writing its own code for over six months without a single line from a human developer. That's not a prediction. That's not a thought experiment. That's a fact from Boris Cherny, the creator and architect of Claude Code, and it raises a question that every software engineer listening right now needs to grapple with: if the machine can build itself, what exactly are you for?

The answer is more interesting than you might think, and Cherny has spent the past couple of years watching thousands of developers — from solo freelancers to engineering teams at major corporations — figure it out in real time. This episode is about what he learned.

First, some context on where this tool came from. Claude Code didn't emerge from months of strategic planning or a dedicated product team. Cherny built the first prototype in a couple of days, almost on a whim, just to learn how the Anthropic application programming interface worked. Within five days, half of Anthropic's own engineering team was using it. That's how you know you've built something that actually solves a problem people have.

The concept Cherny calls product overhang explains why this keeps happening. Language models can do things that no existing product lets them do yet. The capabilities exist; the interfaces to access them don't. Claude Code was one of those interfaces, and its rapid adoption among engineers was a signal that the gap between what models could do and what tools let users do was enormous.

Now here's where it gets practical. If you're already using Claude Code, you might think you're getting the full picture. You're probably not. And the reason cuts right to something Cherny has been evangelizing: configuration matters enormously, and most people are leaving the most powerful settings completely untouched.

Let's start with a number that should alarm you. When you open a fresh session in Claude Code and start typing, you've already burned through about fourteen percent of your context window before you've written a single word of your actual request. That context is being consumed by a file called CLAUDE dot em dee, a markdown file that lives in your project directory and contains your instructions. Most users either don't know this file exists or have never opened it. They're letting their most powerful configuration tool sit there doing nothing while they manually re-explain their project structure, their coding conventions, and their preferences in every single conversation. That's not using Claude Code. That's typing with one hand behind your back.

The fix is straightforward. Put meaningful instructions in your CLAUDE dot em dee file. Describe your project. Specify how you want code formatted. State your testing philosophy. If you work on a team, this file becomes a shared understanding that every team member can contribute to. The fourteen percent context cost becomes an investment rather than pure waste, and you start each session with Claude already oriented to your project rather than starting from zero every time.

Beyond that, there's a memory system that eliminates the need to repeat yourself across sessions. Most users don't know this exists. When you reference past conversations or decisions, Claude can store those in a memory bank and retrieve them automatically in future sessions. The result is a system that actually learns how you work rather than treating every conversation as an isolated event. If you've ever found yourself starting a session by saying something like, "remember, we decided to use Postgres instead of MongoDB because," you already understand why this matters. The memory system means you never have to say that again.

Then there's the section most users leave blank. In your project settings, there's a field for custom instructions. This is where you tell Claude who you are, what you care about, and how you prefer to work. The vast majority of users skip this entirely. They treat Claude Code as a generic tool rather than a personalized assistant. When you fill in those custom instructions — your experience level, your preferences for code style, the kinds of projects you typically work on — Claude becomes significantly more useful because it tailors its responses to your specific situation rather than treating you like an average user.

Finally, project settings give you something Cherny describes as permanent context. Unlike a regular conversation that ends when you close the window, project-level settings persist across all future sessions for that specific project. This is different from the per-conversation memory and different from the CLAUDE dot em dee file. Project settings are the layer that tells Claude how to behave for this particular codebase, forever, unless you change them. Once you've configured them properly, you never have to re-establish your context for that project again.

The irony is that most people discover these features after months of use and feel a little embarrassed they didn't find them sooner. Cherny himself has noted that the people who get the most out of Claude Code are often not the most technically sophisticated users. They're the people who bothered to read the documentation and configure their environment properly.

But configuration is only half the story. Cherny's observations about what this tool means for the profession are worth sitting with, because they're not the standard either-or panic narratives you hear from either the "AI will take all our jobs" crowd or the "don't worry, it's just a better autocomplete" crowd.

The reality is more nuanced. Some companies will need fewer engineers because each engineer can produce dramatically more. That's already happening. But other companies will need more engineers because they can now attempt projects that were previously too ambitious or too expensive. The demand signal shifts rather than simply disappearing.

What's more striking is the blurring of boundaries. Non-engineers are now writing code. Managers, designers, product people — they're all using tools like Claude Code to build things that used to require a software developer. At the same time, engineers who previously spent most of their day writing code are spending more of their time thinking, reviewing, and directing. The job title is staying the same while the job description is quietly mutating.

Cherny's own usage is a case study in this shift. Having Claude Code write its own code for six months didn't make Cherny obsolete. It freed him to focus on the harder problems: the architecture decisions, the edge cases, the things that require judgment rather than pattern matching. That's probably the most honest illustration of what the transition looks like from inside rather than from the outside looking in.

For people entering the field now, Cherny's advice splits into two paths. If you're entrepreneurial, go build something. He describes the current moment as a golden age for startups because the leverage available to a small team — or even a single person — is greater than it's ever been. If you're not ready to go solo, entry-level positions still exist at large companies. The transition is real, but it's not instantaneous, and there's a window where you can build skills and experience even as the landscape shifts beneath you.

The broader implications extend beyond individual careers. Business models built on high switching costs are vulnerable. When tools like Claude Code make it easier to understand, modify, and extend codebases, the defensibility of certain products decreases. At the same time, tiny teams are building things that would have required large organisations five years ago. The startup landscape is going to look very different in three years, and the reason is precisely the productivity gap that tools like this create.

There's a human element that Cherny has been careful to preserve. Despite every opportunity to automate responses to user feedback, he still reads and replies personally because he considers it the favorite part of the job. That feels worth noting. The people building these tools are making choices about how to use them, and those choices shape what the technology becomes.

The transition away from traditional coding is not going to be smooth for everyone. Some people will be displaced, retraining will be inadequate, and the societal conversation about how to manage the shift is nowhere near mature enough. That's not a problem any single company or tool can solve. But for the people listening right now who are trying to figure out how to work with this technology rather than against it, the practical starting point is straightforward: configure your environment properly, use the tools that are already available, and stop leaving the most powerful settings blank.

The context window is finite. Don't waste fourteen percent of it on nothing.
