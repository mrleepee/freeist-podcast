Here's a surprising fact: most people using Claude Code are essentially gambling with their own productivity. They open a session, start typing, and keep going until something breaks — and then they blame the model when it starts hallucinating or losing the thread entirely. What they don't realise is that Anthropic's own team has published the definitive guide to fixing this, and the core insight is almost counterintuitive. Having a one million token context window doesn't mean you should fill it. In fact, doing exactly that is probably the single biggest mistake most users are making right now.

Let me explain why. Claude Code can hold about a million tokens in its working memory at once — every instruction, every file it has read, every tool call and its output, all of it sitting there for the model to reference. Think of it like a very large desk. But even the biggest desk gets cluttered, and eventually you cannot find the thing you need under the pile. This degradation has a name: context rot. As your session grows longer, the model's performance quietly declines. Attention gets spread across more tokens. Older, irrelevant content starts to distract from what you are actually working on right now. The model does not crash or throw an error — it just gets fuzzier, less precise, more likely to hallucinate or miss important details.

Research across leading models shows this starts well before you hit the limit. Some benchmarks show accuracy dropping at just twenty five percent of the context window. The practical threshold most experienced users have settled on is somewhere between fifty and sixty percent — once you pass that, quality starts slipping. Session management is the skill of keeping your context clean, focused, and relevant. And according to Anthropic's own team, it is the single biggest factor separating people who love Claude Code from people who think it is unreliable.

Here is the good news. After every response Claude gives you, you have five options for managing what comes next. Most people just hit Enter and keep going, but the right choice depends on what just happened. The most powerful is called rewind — you press Escape twice and jump back to a previous message, discarding everything that came after. Thariq Shihipar, a member of Anthropic's Claude Code team, calls this the number one professional habit, and I think he is right. Imagine Claude has read five files, tried an approach that did not work, and now you want to redirect it. If you just type a correction, your context now contains the failed attempt, the correction message, and probably another failed attempt before you reach the solution. But if you rewind to before the failed attempt, type your redirect, and try again, your context contains only the file reads, the informed redirect, and the fix. All that noise disappears. You can even type "summarise from here" before rewinding, and Claude will write a quick handoff note — a message from its past self that tried something and learned it did not work. Then you rewind and paste that summary in. All the learnings, none of the context bloat.

Beyond that, you have a few other tools. Compact asks Claude to summarise everything so far and replace the full history with that summary — quick and automatic, though it is lossy, meaning you are trusting the model to decide what mattered. You can guide it by saying "compact, focus on the authentication refactor, drop the debugging" so it knows what to preserve. Then there is clear, which wipes everything — but you write down what matters first, creating a fresh session with exactly the context you decided was relevant.

Subagents are probably the most underrated tool. When Claude spawns a subagent, it gets its own fresh context window, does all its work in isolation, and only sends the final result back to you. Everything it read, every dead end it hit, all of it disappears. Use the mental test: will I need this tool output again, or just the conclusion? If it is just the conclusion, that is a subagent job.

One more habit worth adopting: compact proactively at fifty percent, not reactively when you are hitting ninety. Multiple practitioners report better stability and fewer hallucinations when they stay ahead of context rot rather than waiting for the model to start struggling under the weight of it all.

The bottom line is simple. A clean context beats a long context every time. The million token window is a safety margin, not an invitation to dump everything in. Before you type your next message, take one second to ask whether continuing is really the best move — or whether a rewind, compact, or fresh start would serve you better. That one second of thought is the difference between a session that ships clean code and one that ends in frustration.
