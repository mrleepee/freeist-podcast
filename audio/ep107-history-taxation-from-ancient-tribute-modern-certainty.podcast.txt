Imagine being told, five thousand years ago, that if you did not hand over a fifth of every bushel your land produced, men with clubs would come to your farm and make you regret your reluctance. You probably do not have to imagine it too hard. Around three thousand B C E, the pharaohs of Egypt did exactly that, and they called it civilisation. The scribe of the treasury, the sesh per hedj, would assess how much grain your plot could yield, take his cut, and store it in the royal granaries. Tomb paintings from the Old Kingdom at Saqqara show these scribes tallying grain while farmers watch with expressions that have not changed much in five millennia. Failure to pay meant corporal punishment. The justification was divine right — the pharaoh was a god, the Nile was his gift, and your harvest was effectively his already. Consent was not sought. The legal basis was straightforward: I have soldiers, you have grain, let us discuss terms.

Herodotus, writing in the fifth century B C E, noted something fascinating when Persia conquered Egypt around five twenty-five B C E. The Persian administration actually improved tax collection. They introduced a systematic census and fixed the tribute at seven hundred talents of silver annually from Egypt alone, plus the produce of the fish markets of Lake Moeris. The Romans later copied this model and scaled it across three continents. So even the ancient world had its efficiency experts, and they all reached the same conclusion: centralised extraction beats scattered banditry, at least from the extractor is point of view.

Rome did not just tax. Rome industrialised it. The tributum was originally a wealth tax levied on Roman citizens to fund wars, and here is the detail that should sound familiar: it was explicitly temporary. The Roman state promised to repay it when the war was over. Sometimes it did. Often it did not. By the second century B C E, Rome had developed a system that would make modern private equity firms look amateur. The publicani were private contractors who bid at auction for the right to collect taxes in a province. They paid Rome upfront, then extracted whatever they could from the provincials. The profit margin was theirs to keep. If they collected double what they had bid, excellent. If the locals starved, not Rome is problem.

Cicero, when he was governor of Cilicia in fifty-one and fifty B C E, wrote extensively about the abuses of the publicani. In his letters to Atticus, he described tax collectors who extracted from the provincials not merely money but their blood. The provincial communities had no representation, no appeal, and no legal recourse against contractors backed by Roman legions. The system was technically governed by Roman law. In practice, the law served the extractor, not the extracted. When Augustus reformed the system around twenty-seven B C E, replacing tax farmers with direct imperial officials, he did not do it out of mercy. He did it because the publicani had become so rapacious that provinces were revolting and revenues were actually declining. Even emperors understood that killing the goose requires a certain amount of restraint.

Now jump to medieval England, where the names changed but the game stayed the same. Danegeld, literally Danish money, was a land tax first levied in nine ninety-one C E to pay off Viking raiders. King Aethelred the Unready is government collected ten thousand pounds of silver in the first payment. By ten eighteen, the Danegeld had reached eighty-two thousand five hundred pounds. The Vikings kept coming back because, somewhat predictably, paying ransom encourages further ransom demands. The tax persisted long after the Viking threat faded, because no monarch voluntarily surrenders a revenue stream. Then there was tallage, arbitrary levies imposed by the king on his subjects with no pretence of consent, and scutage, introduced by Henry the first around eleven hundred, which let knights pay cash instead of providing military service. That sounds reasonable until you realise it was a military service tax that the king could set at whatever level he wanted, and if you could not pay, you still had to serve.

The breaking point came in twelve fifteen. Magna Carta is Clause twelve remains one of the most consequential sentences in the history of government: no scutage or aid may be levied in our kingdom without its general consent. King John agreed to this not because he believed in democracy but because his barons had captured London and were about to depose him. Tax consent was born not from legal philosophy but from armed rebellion. This principle was reinforced in sixteen eighty-nine by the English Bill of Rights, Article four, which declared that levying money for or to the use of the Crown by pretence of prerogative, without grant of Parliament, is illegal. This was not abstract constitutional theory. It was the direct consequence of a century of Stuart kings trying to rule without Parliament, and Charles the first literally losing his head over it in sixteen forty-nine.

And then there is the window tax, introduced under William the third in sixteen ninety-six. The logic was simple: more windows meant a wealthier householder. Six windows or fewer, you were exempt. Ten to nineteen windows, four shillings. Twenty or more, eight shillings. The result was entirely predictable. People bricked up their windows. You can still see the bricked-up window spaces in buildings across England today. The tax persisted for over one hundred fifty years, finally repealed in eighteen fifty-one, not because anyone decided it was unjust but because it was reducing property values and the medical profession had begun arguing that lack of ventilation caused disease. A tax so counterproductive it literally made people live in the dark for a century and a half, and nobody in Parliament had the political will to kill it until doctors started complaining.

William Pitt the Younger introduced the first British income tax in seventeen ninety-nine to fund the Napoleonic Wars. It was explicitly temporary. The rate was ten percent on incomes above two hundred pounds, with lower bands down to sixty pounds. Pitt promised it would end when the war ended. It was repealed in eighteen sixteen, a year after Waterloo, one of the rare instances where a temporary war tax was actually temporary. But Robert Peel brought it back in eighteen forty-two to plug a budget deficit. The rate was lower, three point two percent on incomes above one hundred fifty pounds, and again it was introduced as a temporary measure. It has never been repealed since. Every British chancellor who has stood at the dispatch box since eighteen forty-two and claimed a tax measure is temporary has been lying, whether they knew it or not.

The United States followed an almost identical trajectory, with an American flavour. The first federal income tax was introduced in eighteen sixty-one to fund the Civil War, three percent on incomes above eight hundred dollars. It was repealed in eighteen seventy-two. A flat tax was briefly reinstated in eighteen ninety-four but struck down by the Supreme Court in Pollock versus Farmers' Loan and Trust Company in eighteen ninety-five, because it was a direct tax not apportioned among the states. This constitutional roadblock was removed by the sixteenth Amendment, ratified in nineteen thirteen. The initial rate was one percent on incomes above three thousand dollars, with a top rate of seven percent on incomes above five hundred thousand dollars. Fewer than two percent of households paid it.

Then came the First World War. By nineteen eighteen, the top marginal rate hit seventy-seven percent. The Revenue Act of nineteen eighteen was supposed to be a war measure. The war ended in November nineteen eighteen. The tax did not. Rates came down in the nineteen twenties under Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon, the top rate falling to twenty-four percent by nineteen twenty-nine, but the mechanism was permanent. The federal government had discovered a revenue source it could never give up.

The Second World War completed the transformation. The Revenue Act of nineteen forty-two increased the number of Americans paying income tax from thirteen million to fifty million. Withholding was introduced in nineteen forty-three, and this is the detail worth dwelling on. The government no longer had to wait for you to pay. It took the money before you ever saw it. Withholding was the single most important innovation in tax compliance. You cannot miss what you never had. The psychological shift from paying a bill to having money removed before it reaches your pocket is enormous, and it was deliberate.

The pattern is relentless across every era and every jurisdiction. The Spanish-American War of eighteen ninety-eight prompted a federal excise tax on telephone service. The war lasted four months. The telephone tax lasted one hundred eight years, finally repealed in two thousand six. For over a century, every American who made a phone call was paying for a war that ended before any of them were born. The United Kingdom is similarly inventive. Stamp duties, introduced in sixteen ninety-four to fund the war against France, were temporary. The United Kingdom still collects stamp duty on property transactions and share purchases in twenty twenty-six. That is three hundred thirty-two years of temporary. Air passenger duty, introduced in nineteen ninety-four as an environmental measure supposedly revenue-neutral, now raises over four billion pounds annually. The insurance premium tax, introduced at two point five percent in nineteen ninety-seven, now stands at twelve percent for most policies. Every single one was introduced as temporary, targeted, or proportionate. None of them stayed that way.

Here is the uncomfortable thread running through all of this. The Magna Carta established in twelve fifteen that taxation required consent. The English Bill of Rights reaffirmed this in sixteen eighty-nine. The American Revolution was fought over the principle of no taxation without representation, though the actual tax burden that triggered it was remarkably light. The Stamp Act of seventeen sixty-five imposed duties ranging from two shillings on legal documents to ten shillings on playing cards. The Townshend Acts of seventeen sixty-seven placed duties on glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea. The total additional tax burden on the American colonies was estimated at roughly one to two percent of income. In Britain itself, taxpayers were bearing effective rates of twenty to thirty percent. The revolution was not fought over the amount. It was fought over the principle. Parliament was taxing people who had no representatives in Parliament.

And yet here we are, centuries later, with income tax rates in most developed countries between twenty and fifty percent, value added tax at twenty percent in the United Kingdom, payroll taxes, capital gains taxes, corporation taxes, fuel duties, alcohol duties, tobacco duties, stamp duties, council taxes, inheritance taxes, and the consent mechanism is a vote every four or five years between two parties that both support the overall structure.

The sixteenth Amendment was ratified by the states in nineteen thirteen. Thirty-six of the then-48 states approved it. But the income tax it authorised bore no resemblance to what it would become within five years. The people who consented to a one to seven percent tax did not consent to a seventy-seven percent top marginal rate within a decade. The people who consented to a tax affecting fewer than two percent of households did not consent to a tax on the majority of the working population. The legal basis exists. The constitutional amendments were passed, the laws were enacted, the courts upheld them. But the legal basis for the Roman tributum also existed. It was authorised by the Senate and enforced by the praetors. The legal basis for tallage existed. It was the king is prerogative. Legality and legitimacy are not the same thing.

The history of taxation is not a history of consent. It is a history of expedience becoming permanent, of crisis measures becoming baseline expectations, and of populations being told that what was once an emergency measure is now the natural order of things. Every major tax in existence was sold as temporary. Every one became permanent. The mechanism is always the same: a crisis creates urgency, the tax is introduced with promises of a sunset clause, the crisis passes, the revenue proves too convenient, and the sunset clause is quietly allowed to expire. The legal framework adjusts to normalise what was once exceptional. Then a new crisis arrives, and the process repeats at a higher baseline. It is not natural. It is just old. And it is very, very good at wearing the right clothes.
