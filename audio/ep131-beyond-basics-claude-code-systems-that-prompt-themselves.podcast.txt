Imagine you could clone yourself, send that clone off to handle a pile of tedious tasks, and have it come back having done the work exactly the way you would have done it. That is the promise sitting at the heart of AI-assisted software development right now, and it is closer to reality than most people realise. The catch is that your clone is only as good as the information you give it. If it cannot see what you see, read what you read, and access the same systems you use every day, you end up with something that looks like a junior colleague who somehow also missed the entire brief. That is the core idea driving a lot of the thinking around building better agentic systems, and it is what I want to unpack today.

The fundamental shift happening in software engineering is this: your job is no longer just to write code yourself, but to create versions of yourself that can operate at scale across many agents. Think of it as setting up a small team, except every team member has your context, your coding style, and your understanding of the project. The question is not whether AI can help you, because it clearly can, but whether you have built the infrastructure for that AI to actually behave like a competent extension of you rather than a well-meaning stranger reading a bad summary of your codebase.

To get there, you need to think about customisation in three distinct areas. The first is access. An agent that cannot connect to your team chat, your C I slash C D pipelines, your production dashboards, or your internal documentation is working with one hand tied behind its back. Those Slack threads explaining why a particular architectural decision was made, the runbooks nobody has updated in six months but everyone still references, the meeting notes that capture context nobody wrote down anywhere else: all of that lives outside the model by default. The advice from people who have thought hard about this is surprisingly practical. Try working an entire day without leaving your AI coding environment. Note every tool you switch to, every document you open, every system you query. Then go and connect your AI to those things. That exercise alone will reveal just how much of your daily workflow depends on context the model simply does not have.

The second area is knowledge. Institutional memory, the kind of stuff that lives in peoples heads and Slack histories rather than documentation, cannot be trained into a model through fine-tuning. It is not cost-effective and frankly it does not work well because frontier models evolve so quickly that whatever you spent weeks training becomes outdated almost immediately. Instead, you want to use in-context learning, which is really just a fancy way of saying that all your customisation lives in text files that get loaded into the prompt when they are needed. Conventions, recent changes, internal application programming interfaces, team vocabulary: all of this can be handed to the model as plain text rather than baked into the model weights themselves.

The third area is tooling. Out of the box, most AI coding environments have pretty basic edit tools, the kind of thing that is fine for a quick demo but does not scale to real production work. You are missing syntax highlighting, language server protocol support, code completion, and critically the little red squiggly underlines that tell you something is wrong before you even run anything. For agents, the equivalent is what people call post-tool-use hooks, which are essentially non-blocking reminders that flag things without hard-stopping the agent. The key insight here is that the fastest way to make your agent better is a tighter feedback loop, not a smarter model. And on the subject of tools, it is worth distinguishing between those that compensate for a lack of intelligence, which you should generally avoid, and those that scale with intelligence, which you should actively prefer. A tool that hard-blocks an agent from doing something is a workaround for a limitation. A tool that gives the agent better information so it can make a better decision is the right approach.

Now, here is where things get genuinely tricky for anyone building these systems at scale. Context windows have plateaued at around one million tokens for the most capable models. That sounds enormous until you start filling it with system prompts, tool definitions, knowledge files, and everything else you need to give an agent enough context to be useful. One analogy that stuck with me is running Node Package Manager on an Arduino. You technically have enough compute to do it, but you are constantly bumping against constraints that force you to be ruthlessly intentional about what you include. There is a principle people call zero-overhead abstraction, which means you should not pay for what you do not use. If something is not relevant to the current task, it should not be eating into your context window.

There is also a subtlety around something called the KVCache. When you change anything early in a long prompt, the entire cache of previously processed tokens gets invalidated, which means every token that comes after becomes roughly ten times more expensive to process. The practical advice here is counterintuitive but important: put stable, shared information at the front of your context, and keep volatile, per-task information near the end. That way, when the volatile stuff changes between tasks, you are not paying the price of invalidating everything that came before it.

On the subject of how to actually wire these systems together, there are four primitives worth knowing about. There is the Model Context Protocol, which was designed for simple chatbots without shell access and works well for shipping integrations to non-technical customers. But here is the thing: it does not scale particularly well because every tool's name, description, and schema gets loaded into the system prompt, and those add up fast. For internal developer tooling, skills are usually the better choice. A skill is essentially a folder with a markdown file inside it and a one-line description in the front matter. They are easy to create, but at scale you can run into quality control issues because nothing forces consistency across different people creating skills. The description of a skill is always loaded, which costs a small amount, and reliably triggering the right skill can take several hundred tokens. The team working on this is apparently looking at hierarchy systems that would let subskills lazy-load, which would help with that problem.

So where does all of this leave you if you are thinking about building these systems for your own team? A few concrete things seem worth taking away. First, connect your AI to every information source you use in a normal workday, even if it feels like overkill at first. Second, prefer tools that give agents better information over tools that hard-block their behaviour. Third, use skills for internal developer tooling and the Model Context Protocol for external integrations that non-technical people will interact with. Fourth, treat your context window as a constrained resource, because it is. Fifth, implement hooks and linters as reminders rather than blockers, because the goal is better decisions, not slower ones.

The people building these tools are already using them to develop the next version of themselves, which is either a sign that the approach is working or a sign that we are entering a phase of development that is going to get very strange very quickly. Either way, the underlying principles around access, knowledge, and tooling are not going away. The specifics will evolve, but the discipline of being intentional about what your agents can see and do is probably the most important skill in software engineering right now.
