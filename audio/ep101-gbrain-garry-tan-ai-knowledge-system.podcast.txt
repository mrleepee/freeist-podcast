Imagine waking up every morning with a colleague who has read every email you have ever sent, every meeting note you have ever taken, and every thought you have ever jotted down, and who can tell you exactly what you need to know before you even ask. That is essentially what Garry Tan built for himself. Tan is the President and chief executive of Y Combinator, the startup accelerator that has backed companies like Airbnb, Dropbox, and Reddit. He personally developed an open-source system called GBrain, and his current production version indexes over one hundred and forty-six thousand pages, tracks nearly twenty-five thousand people, and monitors more than five thousand companies. It runs sixty-six automated background jobs without any intervention from him. He describes it as making him wake up smarter than when he went to bed.

Most personal knowledge tools like Notion or Obsidian essentially give you a fancy search box. You type in a name and get back a list of documents that mention it, and then you have to read through them yourself. GBrain does something fundamentally different. It gives you the actual answer. Suppose you have a meeting with someone tomorrow. A traditional tool returns five pages mentioning their name. GBrain returns a synthesized response: they run engineering at a particular company, a Series B fintech, you last spoke on a specific date about pricing, three action items remain open, and here is what it does not know. It tells you the gap. No new information has been added about this person in six weeks, so they may have replied through channels the system cannot see. That gap analysis changes everything. Instead of walking into a meeting with potentially stale information, you know precisely what to ask about.

The system rests on three pillars. First, a hybrid search layer that combines vector similarity matching, keyword matching, and a synthesis engine that composes coherent answers with proper citations. Benchmarks show this approach improves precision by over thirty percentage points compared to standard retrieval methods. Second, a knowledge graph that grows automatically. Every time you write something, the system extracts references to people, companies, and connections and builds typed relationships between them, with zero calls to the underlying language model. This graph component is responsible for most of that precision improvement. Third, what Tan calls the Dream Cycle, an overnight maintenance routine that deduplicates information, fixes broken citations, scores relevance, and flags contradictions. While you sleep, your brain cleans itself.

Technically, GBrain runs on a version of Postgres compiled to WebAssembly for personal deployments up to about fifty thousand pages. No server, no Docker, no configuration. Setup takes roughly thirty minutes and the system handles most of the work. The data lives as plain markdown files in a git repository that you own and control. For teams, there is a company brain mode where each person sees only the information they are authorized to access, with no cross-contamination between users.

The broader significance is worth sitting with. Tan open-sourced this tool, and it has attracted nearly twenty thousand stars on GitHub. That matters because Y Combinator sits at the gateway to startup funding, and its president publicly shared his own solution to a problem every knowledge worker faces. The question GBrain raises is uncomfortable. If an AI agent can remember everything you have ever written, synthesised it into actionable intelligence, and tell you what you are missing, what does that do to the value of experience itself? A twenty-five-year-old with this running for two years might have better institutional memory than a fifty-year-old relying on their own recall. That is not a rhetorical point. It is a structural one, and it deserves serious thinking.
