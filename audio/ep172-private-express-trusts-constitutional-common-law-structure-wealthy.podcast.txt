There is a legal structure that, if you believe the websites selling it, the ultra-wealthy have used quietly since the eighteen hundreds to shield their assets, dodge probate, and keep their affairs out of public databases. It is called a private express trust. The pitch is seductively simple. You create a three-party common-law trust with a grantor, a trustee, and a beneficiary. The claim is that this entity is formed under constitutional contract law rather than state trust codes. It supposedly requires no public filing, offers near total privacy, and operates entirely outside the statutory regulation that governs ordinary revocable and irrevocable trusts. One website, learnprivatetrusts dot com, lays out this case in detail. They ground the whole structure in the Constitution's Contract Clause, which forbids states from passing any law impairing the obligation of contracts. If a trust is just a private contract, the argument goes, the government cannot reach inside it. But before we unpack that, understand that this episode is educational only. It is not legal, tax, or financial advice. Nothing here recommends forming, avoiding, or dismantling any trust. Trust and tax law is jurisdiction-specific and fact-specific. You must consult a qualified attorney and a certified public accountant before acting on anything discussed.

Now, the private express trust itself is not invented. It is a genuine common-law vehicle with deep roots in English and American law. It requires three legally distinct parties. The grantor creates the trust and transfers assets into it. The trustee holds legal title and manages the property under strict fiduciary duties. The beneficiary holds the equitable interest. Once properly funded, meaning assets are transferred via deeds and title assignments, the trust legally owns the property instead of the individual. That much is real. The common law of trusts is centuries old and is the foundation of modern estate planning.

But the marketing site's framing starts to drift when it contrasts these trusts with statutory trusts. They tell readers that revocable living trusts and irrevocable trusts are creatures of state law requiring public filing, while private express trusts are based on constitutional contract principles and therefore are not automatically subject to state trust code provisions. This is the load-bearing claim of the whole pitch, and it does not survive scrutiny. Here is what the Contract Clause does. The clause genuinely forbids state legislatures from passing laws that retroactively impair existing contract obligations. But the Supreme Court of the United States has never held that it creates a zone of private contracting immune from regulation. The opposite is true. In nineteen seventy-seven, the Supreme Court explicitly rejected the idea that parties can obtain immunity from state regulation by making private contractual arrangements. The clause limits state legislative impairment of contracts. It does not let private parties contract their way out of the tax code or the state trust law that courts apply to determine whether a trust even exists. When a court looks at a trust, it applies state trust law and federal tax law regardless of how the trust was formed or what its documents claim about being constitutional and non-statutory. The Cornell Law Institute annotation of the Constitution confirms this.

This is where the marketing becomes dangerous. The Internal Revenue Service maintains an entire enforcement toolkit devoted to arrangements that use this vocabulary. The tax agency explicitly classifies pure trust, constitutional trust, and unincorporated business organization schemes as abusive tax-evasion vehicles. In the agency's own words, such arrangements provide no tax relief. Federal courts have consistently collapsed these types of trusts. They apply sham theory, assignment of income rules, and grantor-trust statutes to tax the individual anyway. People have been criminally prosecuted for selling and using these exact structures to evade taxes.

Draw a sharp line here between illegal schemes and legitimate asset-protection tools. Real asset-protection planning exists and works entirely within the law. An irrevocable trust, properly drafted and funded, can remove assets from your estate and put them beyond the reach of future creditors, subject to rules about fraudulent conveyance and grantor-trust status. Domestic Asset Protection Trusts, available in roughly two-thirds of United States states, offer a statutory shield for assets transferred in good faith. Properly structured trusts also avoid probate, keeping your family's affairs private and moving assets to heirs efficiently. But the protection is not near total. Courts pierce sham trusts routinely. They apply alter-ego doctrine when you keep treating trust assets as your own. They subpoena trust records. Federal tax law applies to every trust. Irrevocable trusts file tax returns, pay tax on undistributed income at compressed brackets, and you cannot contract around grantor-trust status.

The marketing site claims traditional attorneys avoid private express trusts because of a business model built on ongoing statutory trust maintenance. The honest version is that competent attorneys avoid the constitutional variant because it does not work. Relying on it exposes clients to sham-trust and tax-evasion risks. The legitimate version uses ordinary statutory irrevocable trusts and domestic asset protection trusts with decades of case law behind them. The source site analyzed today is a lead-generation operation that sells trust templates and services priced from roughly two hundred and fifty dollars to ten thousand dollars. They have a direct commercial interest in promoting this trust category. The authoritative government tax-enforcement guidance and constitutional annotations contradict their sales pitch.

The impulse here is wanting privacy over your assets and wanting to pass wealth to your heirs on your own terms. Those are real freedoms, and the law provides real, tested tools for securing them. The trap is the magic-words theory. Real independence is built on what holds up when a judge or a tax examiner comes asking.
