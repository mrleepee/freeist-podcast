Ninety percent of what AI Twitter tells you to learn will be dead in six months. That is not a prediction from a pessimist. That is not hot takes from someone with an agenda. That is the estimate that emerges when you look at the structural incentives driving the AI education industrial complex, and it turns out the most honest person to admit this is someone who has probably done more to teach people about AI than anyone else alive.

Andrej Karpathy, the man who quite literally wrote the textbook millions used to learn deep learning, stood on stage at Sequoia AI Ascent in twenty twenty-six and said he has never felt more behind as a programmer. Let that sink in for a moment. The person who taught the world how neural networks work feels like he cannot keep up. That is not false modesty. That is a system signal. If the man who built the curriculum is struggling to stay current, what does that tell you about the people selling you five hour courses on the framework that dropped last Tuesday?

The uncomfortable truth is that most of what circulates on AI Twitter is not education. It is content. And content has a very different lifecycle than knowledge. A framework goes viral not because it solves hard problems reliably, but because it gives people something dramatic to announce. Someone posts that a new framework has dropped, the engagement algorithm rewards novelty over stability, and suddenly thousands of developers abandon whatever they were building to spend a weekend on something that will itself be abandoned by next month.

You can see this pattern playing out in real time with LangChain. The subreddit dedicated to it went from fan club to something closer to a public complaint desk in under a year. There are threads on that forum titled things like why is everyone suddenly ditching LangChain, and similar conversations on Hacker News where teams explain why they have stopped using it for building their AI agents. Developers who were evangelising the tool twelve months ago are now writing threads about why they left. This is not unusual. This is the rhythm of the hype cycle, and it is driven by incentives that have nothing to do with your productivity.

Here is the part that most people miss though. Not every tool that generates buzz is equally hollow. Occasionally something breaks through that actually solves a real engineering problem, and it tends to stick around not because it went viral but because the people building serious production systems keep reaching for it. The Model Context Protocol, for instance, is emerging as a genuine standard for how AI agents discover and use tools. It is not a framework that dropped with a slick announcement. It is infrastructure that emerged from actual engineering needs, which means it is likely to still be relevant when the next wave of viral tools has already been forgotten.

So what should you actually do with this? Build a filter before you invest your time. Ask a simple question whenever someone tells you to learn something new. Who profits if you adopt this? If the answer is content creators who need fresh material, venture capitalists who need adoption metrics for their portfolio companies, or Twitter's engagement algorithm which literally rewards new framework announcements over stable solutions, then the tool is not really for you. It is for them. Your time is the only resource you have that you cannot print more of, and it is being spent to generate engagement for someone else's platform.

You have been sold anxiety disguised as education. The people hyping the next big thing are not using it in production. They are writing threads about it. And there is a fundamental difference between a tool that is trending and a tool that will still exist next year. So the next time you see a dramatic announcement that something revolutionary has just dropped, ask yourself one question before you invest a single hour. Will this still matter when the next announcement arrives, or is it already building someone else's engagement metrics while yours quietly expire?

Your time is not a renewable resource. Spend it accordingly.
