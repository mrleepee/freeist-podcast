The finding that stopped me in my tracks was this: when the team tried to improve performance by evolving only the system prompt, performance actually regressed by about two point three percentage points. The team found that a frozen harness — one designed for one task and one model family — could be dropped into a different task and a completely different base model, and it still added between five and ten percentage points on S W E dash bench dash verified and three alternate model families. The benefit survived the change. That is a remarkable finding. It suggests the performance gain is not specific to a clever prompt or a particular dataset. It is baked into the infrastructure itself.

The researchers call this approach Agentic Harness Engineering, and they break it down into three pillars of observability: component observability, which means understanding what each part of the system is doing; experience observability, which means tracking how the agent behaves across multiple interactions; and decision observability, which means being able to audit why the agent chose one action over another. These are not abstract concepts. They are engineering practices, the kind you would recognise if you have ever worked on backend systems or distributed architecture. The irony is that the AI industry has been so dazzled by the language model at the centre that it has largely ignored everything surrounding it.

So what does this mean for you? If you are building AI agents, the evidence suggests you should stop treating the prompt as the lever. Stop hiring people to spend six hours crafting the perfect instruction set while the tools the agent can actually use remain bolted on as an afterthought. The prompt is the interface, not the engine. The harness — the tools, the middleware, the long-term memory architecture — that is where the performance actually lives. And that is not a soft insight or a theoretical claim. It is benchmarked, repeated, and sitting in an open repository you can download today.

Which raises a question worth sitting with: if the scaffolding is the moat, then what exactly are you selling when you market a prompting technique as a competitive advantage? Because the researchers at Foo-dan and Peking have just quantified exactly how much runway that advantage has — and it is not enough to build a business on. The real engineering challenge is underneath the words you type, in the architecture most people never look at. That is where the work is. That is where the gains are. And that is where you should be looking.
