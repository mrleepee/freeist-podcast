Three hundred thousand people on a volcanic rock in the North Atlantic looked at the global financial establishment and said no. In October two thousand eight, while the United States, the United Kingdom, and Ireland were scrambling to hand your tax money to failing banks, Iceland let its three largest banks collapse. Kaupthing, Landsbanki, and Glitnir went down. The sky did not fall.

Here is the setup. After banking privatisation in the early two thousands, those three Icelandic banks expanded aggressively. By two thousand eight, their combined balance sheets had swollen to nearly ten times Iceland's entire annual economic output. They funded this growth with short-term foreign borrowing. When global credit markets froze, they could not roll over their debts. They were insolvent. Not theoretically insolvent. Mathematically insolvent.

Every other government told you the same story in two thousand eight. Bail out the banks or the entire economy dies. Ireland and the United Kingdom chose to have taxpayers absorb bank losses to make creditors whole. The public inherited generation-crushing debt so that the people who made reckless loans would not lose a penny. Iceland called that bluff. The government took its banks into receivership and carved out new, smaller domestic banks to handle deposits and everyday lending. Foreign creditors who had lent to Icelandic banks took the losses. Icelandic taxpayers did not inherit the bill.

The cost was real and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Iceland's currency, the króna, collapsed. Inflation surged. The stock market fell by around ninety percent. Savings and pensions were hit hard. Iceland imposed strict capital controls that stayed in place for years. Ordinary people suffered. But here is the difference. Instead of forcing those ordinary people to pay off the gambling debts of bankers who had already lost the money, Iceland wrote down a portion of household mortgage debt between twenty ten and twenty fourteen. Public protests known as the pots and pans revolution forced out the government and the head of the central bank. The political class that had overseen the mess was removed.

Then came the accountability. Iceland established a special prosecutor's office and pursued criminal cases against bankers and executives. Dozens were prosecuted. Senior figures at all three failed banks went to prison, convicted of fraud, market manipulation, and breach of trust. Iceland was one of the only developed countries to treat the financial crisis as a matter for criminal justice. Elsewhere, bankers received bonuses. In Iceland, they received sentences.

The results are worth examining. Iceland's recovery was faster and more complete than in countries that chose austerity. Ireland spent years bleeding under the weight of bank debt socialised onto the public. Iceland took the hit, restructured, and moved forward. The króna collapse was brutal. The capital controls were restrictive. But the debt did not get passed to the next generation. The bankers who caused the crisis were not rewarded with promotions and consulting contracts. They were prosecuted.

Now the counterpoint. Iceland is tiny. Three hundred thousand people with its own currency it could devalue and capital controls it could impose. You cannot copy and paste this playbook onto the United States or the eurozone. The scale is different. The currency dynamics are different. The institutional constraints are different. The króna collapse wiped out savings and pensions. This was not a painless solution. It was a painful solution that worked better than the alternative.

But the core lesson holds. When a bank threatens to collapse, the choice is not between bailout and apocalypse. The choice is between who absorbs the loss. The bankers and their creditors, or the taxpayers who had no say in the bets. Iceland chose the people who made the bets. Everyone else chose the taxpayers.

The banks that were too big to fail were never too big to fail. They were too connected to prosecute. Iceland let its banks collapse, and the recovery came faster. The bankers went to prison. The taxpayers kept their money. The next time a politician tells you a bailout is unavoidable, ask yourself a question. Unavoidable for whom?
