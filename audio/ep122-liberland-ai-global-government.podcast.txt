Eight hundred thousand people have applied for citizenship in a country that technically does not exist. Let that number settle for a moment. Among them are thirty-five thousand Americans and sixty thousand Turks, individuals who looked at their own passports and decided the terms were no longer acceptable. The country is called Liberland, and it occupies a sliver of disputed territory between Serbia and Croatia along the Danube, a patch of land nobody particularly wanted until someone claimed it for a project that sounds like a thought experiment until you realise people are already living inside it.

The core idea is straightforward: buy merits, and those merits represent actual ownership shares in the country. Ten thousand dollars purchases five thousand merits. There is no income tax, no corporate tax, and no value added tax, which already makes Liberland more interesting than most places with actual borders and embassies. But the taxation structure is almost secondary to what the merit system is designed to prove. Citizenship, in this framework, is not a birthright or a favour bestowed by a government. It is a financial instrument you purchase because you believe in what the issuer is building. If the country succeeds, your shares appreciate. If it fails, you have lost ten thousand dollars and learned something about the limits of consent-based governance.

That is the thesis, and it is either deeply idealistic or deeply cynical depending on how you feel about the intersection of sovereignty and capitalism. The evidence, though, is worth examining before you decide which camp you occupy. Liberland has a constitution written as a smart contract on a blockchain, which means the founding document cannot be amended by a legislature because no legislature exists in the traditional sense. Elections happen every three months, also on a blockchain, which removes the possibility of centralised manipulation of vote counts. Citizens use a mobile application that tells them precisely what legislation is being proposed, when it will be voted on, and what the likely implications are. There is no parliamentary procedure buried in committee where bad ideas go to quietly become law.

The technology layer extends further. Liberland is building AI advisors for both citizens and elected representatives, tools designed to synthesise the relevant information and present it in plain language before any vote takes place. This is governance as product design, which should immediately raise questions about who programs the AI and what biases are baked into the recommendation engine. The people building this are aware of that problem, or at least they claim to be, which is more than can be said for most governments rolling out digital infrastructure without any equivalent level of transparency about their own algorithmic assumptions.

There are obvious counterarguments, and they deserve their weight. Liberland does not control its claimed territory. The land it occupies is technically disputed, and the actual mechanisms of governance operate through partnerships and digital infrastructure rather than any functional state apparatus. You cannot walk into a Liberland embassy because there are no embassies. What exists instead is a diaspora network of villages and resorts on every continent, a parallel structure designed to provide the services a physical country cannot yet deliver. There is a partnership with Paraguay offering tax residency, which means Liberland citizenship is currently most useful as a lever to negotiate better terms with actual nation-states rather than as a standalone sovereign proposition.

The economic figures suggest something is working. The claimed two hundred percent economic growth in the past year sounds extraordinary until you remember that starting from a very small base makes percentage gains misleading. Still, the trajectory matters more than the absolute number at this stage. What matters more is who is paying attention. Justin Sun, founder of Tron, became a citizen, which brought cryptocurrency credibility and a certain amount of legitimate scepticism about whether this is a serious project or an elaborate brand.

Here is where the stakes become personal, and this is the part most coverage misses. The eight hundred thousand applicants are not all cranks and utopians. Some of them are individuals who have read the fine print on their own passports and found the terms unacceptable. You hold a share in the country that issued your birth certificate. Nobody asked you if you wanted it. Nobody presented the terms for your review or gave you the option to opt out and take your labour elsewhere without consequence. That is not a hypothetical inconvenience. That is the foundational arrangement under which most of human civilisation currently operates.

Liberland has not solved this problem. It may be impossible to solve completely, because some form of collective coordination requires some form of collective obligation, and those obligations will always feel coercive to someone. But it is asking the question out loud in a way that demands a response. Did anyone ask you? And if they did not, what does that say about what you actually own when you hold a passport?
