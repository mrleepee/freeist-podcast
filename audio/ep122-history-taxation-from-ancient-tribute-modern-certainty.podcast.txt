Around three thousand years before the common era, Egyptian pharaohs began collecting a twentieth of every grain harvest. They called it a temporary emergency measure to fund pyramid construction. That twentieth became the foundation of the most coercive institution humanity has ever devised, and it never went away.

The official responsible for tracking these assessments was called the sesh per hedj, and he came with a reed stick. Tomb reliefs at Saqqara show tax collectors beating farmers who fell behind on their payments. Failure to pay meant corporal punishment. The state took your grain, and if you could not provide it, it took something else. This was not taxation as we recognise it today. It was extraction backed by force, dressed in the language of necessity.

The Persians who conquered Egypt later improved the administration. Herodotus noted this without much surprise. Empires learned fast when money was at stake. The Persians demanded seven hundred talents of silver each year from Egypt, along with the produce of the fish markets around Lake Moeris. They had conquered the territory, and they intended to profit from it.

The Romans copied the Persian model. When Rome needed funds for wars and expansion, it levied a tributum, a temporary wealth tax. The word temporary did a lot of heavy lifting. By the second century before the common era, Rome had developed tax farming, a system where private contractors called publicani bid for the right to collect taxes across the provinces. They paid the state an agreed sum upfront, then extracted whatever they could from the local population. The margin was theirs.

Cicero, who served as governor of Cilicia in fifty-one and fifty before the common era, described what this felt like from the receiving end. He wrote that the collectors extracted from the provincials not merely money but their blood. That is a direct quote, and it is worth sitting with. Not their money. Their blood. The system was so brutal that provinces began revolting, and when provinces revolted, revenue collapsed. The contractors had optimised themselves into a crisis.

Augustus reformed the entire arrangement around twenty-seven before the common era. He replaced the rapacious publicani with a permanent imperial bureaucracy. The language changed. The extraction did not. Something cleaner replaced something brutal, and the take remained.

Move forward more than a millennium to the coast of England in the year nine hundred and ninety-one of the common era. Viking raids had become unbearable, and King Ethelred needed money to buy peace. He levied the Danegeld, a tax on land, to raise silver for tribute. The first payment was ten thousand pounds of it. Temporary, of course. A crisis response. By the year one thousand and eighteen, the Danegeld had reached eighty-two thousand five hundred pounds of silver. It had become a permanent line item in English fiscal life. When Henry the First introduced scutage around eleven hundred, monetising the obligation to provide military service, he followed the same pattern. Emergency measure, permanent fixture, next generation inherits the bill.

The structure has not changed in five thousand years. A crisis arrives. The state needs money. It promises the measure is temporary. The crisis passes, the money stays, and the next crisis provides the template for the next temporary measure. Egyptian officials beat farmers who could not pay. Roman collectors extracted blood from provincials. The Danegeld ate into English land until the silver ran out or the patience did. Every time the system became so brutal that people chose revolt over compliance, revenue collapsed, and the state rebuilt it with better marketing.

Your grain, your silver, your attention. That is what they are asking for when they call something temporary. The pharaohs needed your grain to build monuments to their own glory. The Romans needed your blood to sustain an empire that was never yours. The Danegeld needed your silver to fund wars you did not choose. Read that tax bill carefully. Five thousand years of receipts say it will not be temporary.
