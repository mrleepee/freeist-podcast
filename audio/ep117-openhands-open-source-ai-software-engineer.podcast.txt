Few projects have accumulated seventy-five thousand GitHub stars within a few years and emerged from academic research at Carnegie Mellon, but OpenHands has done exactly that. Originally launched under the name OpenDevin, this open source project has quietly become one of the most consequential tools in the autonomous coding space, and a recent partnership with the chip manufacturer AMD is pushing it firmly into territory that should matter to every software engineer who has ever worried about what happens to their code when a cloud service goes down.

The numbers tell a story of serious infrastructure. OpenHands raised eighteen point eight million dollars in venture funding, which signals not a hobby project but a company building for production. Its architecture was formalised in a paper presented at the International Conference on Learning Representations in twenty twenty-five, and a subsequent paper on a software development kit redesign appeared on the open archive arXiv later that same year. The project can run against Claude, GPT, Gemini, Llama, Mistral, or any large language model that supports function calling, which means you are not locked into a single provider or a single pricing model.

What makes the AMD partnership particularly interesting is the shift it represents toward local inference. Through integration with the Lemonade Server on Ryzen AI hardware, OpenHands can now run entirely on your own machine. No API calls to a distant data centre, no per-query costs, no Terms of Service to read before you paste in your proprietary codebase. The OpenHands Index, launched in January twenty twenty-six, provides community benchmarking across five task domains, measuring accuracy, cost, and runtime, so teams can make informed choices rather than relying on vendor marketing.

A community benchmark on a popular forum showed that depending on how you manage the context window, the cost of a task can vary by a factor of three, which is the kind of detail that matters when you are running hundreds of automated tasks per week. One customer, a vice president of data science at a company called C three, reported that OpenHands autonomously fixed eighty-seven percent of their bug tickets on the same day they were filed. The team has even demonstrated migrating legacy COBOL systems to Java, which is the kind of unglamorous but high-value work that actually pays salaries.

Of course, local inference alone does not solve every problem. OpenHands still struggles with ambiguous requirements, user interface work, and tasks that demand deep domain knowledge. The benchmark numbers are community-sourced, not peer-reviewed, and you should verify them against your own workloads before restructuring your engineering team. But the direction of travel is clear.

Here is what I want you to do: download OpenHands, point it at a model you already own, and give it a task from your actual backlog, not a demo and not a blog post benchmark. If it works, you have just built a coding agent that costs nothing per query and answers to no one but you. If it does not work yet, you will know exactly where the ceiling sits, and that is worth more than any vendor's marketing page. Either way, you stop being a subscriber and start being an operator. Your next task is waiting. What are you going to ask it to build?
