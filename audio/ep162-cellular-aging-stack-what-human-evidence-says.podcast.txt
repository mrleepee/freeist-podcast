Forty pounds. That's roughly what a month's supply of a premium cellular aging supplement costs. Now triple it, because nobody sells just one anymore. They sell stacks. Three bottles, one neat package, one confident promise. Here's the confidence trick. They blur the line between one compound with gold-standard human data and two others with preliminary open-label scraps, then charge a premium price for all three as though the evidence sits at the same level. It doesn't.

Doctor Josie Burridge put this to the test. She created a short video ranking three cellular aging stack supplements by the strength of their human evidence, deliberately hiding the brand names. The moment you see the labels, the stack stops looking like a stack. It starts looking like one real thing riding shotgun with two gamblers.

Take the first compound. Urolithin A works through a process called mitophagy, the selective recycling of worn-out mitochondria. Mitophagy slows with age, causing dysfunctional mitochondria to accumulate, which drives declines in muscle strength and endurance. The trial that matters here is Liu and colleagues, published in Cell Reports Medicine in twenty twenty-two. It was a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in adults aged forty to sixty-five. The results showed statistically significant improvements in hand-grip strength and six-minute walk distance, alongside measurable changes in mitochondrial biomarkers and mitophagy-related gene expression. A twenty twenty-four systematic review confirmed that Urolithin A shows potential to reduce inflammation and improve muscle strength and endurance in humans with a good safety profile. A twenty twenty-five paper in Nature Aging extended the picture to immune function. A larger confirmatory trial is ongoing. Burridge describes the results as real but modest. That's exactly what good science looks like. Not miraculous. Measurable.

Here's the catch. The Liu trial was funded by Amazentis, the makers of the Mitopure brand. Industry funding doesn't automatically invalidate results, but it means you read the methodology twice. The design holds up. The effect is there. It's just smaller than the marketing implies.

Now compound two. GlyNAC combines glycine and N-acetylcysteine to feed the body's synthesis of glutathione. Glutathione levels fall with age, reducing cell defenses against oxidative stress. The evidence comes mainly from Sekhar and colleagues at Baylor College of Medicine, publishing in Journals of Gerontology and Clinical Translational Medicine in twenty twenty-two. Their pilot trial was open-label in older adults around seventy years old, running sixteen to twenty-four weeks of supplementation. The results are genuinely striking. GlyNAC raised glutathione toward youthful levels and improved multiple aging hallmarks: oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial function, muscle strength, exercise capacity, cognition, and metabolic function. The Baylor team described it as reversing multiple age-associated abnormalities.

But the trials are small. They are open-label with no placebo control and no blinding. A larger follow-up trial did not raise glutathione as cleanly as expected, which Burridge flags directly. Weak evidence isn't the same as no evidence. Dismissing early-stage research because it lacks blinding would kill promising interventions before they can be properly tested. But paying a premium price for unblinded pilot data from the same lab that invented the supplement isn't optimism. It's a donation to someone else's research budget.

The third compound is almost certainly sulforaphane, derived from broccoli sprouts. The evidence there sits somewhere behind GlyNAC, which itself sits well behind Urolithin A.

Before you spend your money on any supplement marketed for aging, look up the trial design yourself. If the main study is open-label with no placebo control from the same lab that invented the supplement, your confidence should drop accordingly. Three bottles, one with a real trial behind it, two with hope and a Baylor phone number. Read the fine print before you swallow anything.
