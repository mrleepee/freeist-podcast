Roughly twelve thousand nine hundred years ago, the Earth experienced a sudden, violent return to ice age conditions known as the Younger Dryas. Mainstream science completely accepts this global climatic event. But what if that deep freeze was actually triggered by a massive comet impact that wiped out a highly advanced, globe-spanning human civilisation? That is the question journalist and author Graham Hancock has been asking for nearly three decades. In a long-form interview on the Diary of a Chief Executive podcast with Steven Bartlett, Hancock laid out his thesis that this lost society was erased from history. He argues that mainstream archaeology has spent years actively refusing to take the idea seriously.

Here is the catch. You have to separate the established geological facts from the narrative. The Younger Dryas cold snap is an established, dated fact. The existence of an advanced, lost global culture is Graham Hancock's thesis. He links the two. He claims a comet impact during this period around ten thousand eight hundred Before Common Era destroyed a sophisticated prehistoric society and erased thousands of years of human progress. The physical evidence for a society of that calibre simply is not there.

Hancock points to the Great Pyramid of Giza as proof. He argues it encodes advanced mathematical and astronomical knowledge that humans should not have possessed for another two and a half thousand years. He also points to old maps that he claims depict the coastline of Antarctica before it was officially discovered by modern explorers. He presents these as evidence that an earlier, sophisticated culture passed down information that survived the catastrophe. But professional Egyptologists and historians of cartography have rebutted both lines in detail. The maps in particular have well-documented, conventional explanations that do not require a lost civilisation to make sense of them.

But Hancock is not entirely wrong about everything. He highlights the argument that parts of the Amazon rainforest are actually a human-made landscape. He points out that beneath the forest lie massive earthworks and dark, fertile soils known as terra preta, left by large, organised pre-Columbian populations. This is not fringe science. There is a genuine and growing archaeological consensus that the pre-contact Amazon was far more densely populated and deliberately shaped than older assumptions allowed. Hancock is right to highlight this, even if he uses it to support a broader theory the field does not share.

His methods draw intense pushback. Hancock has openly stated that roughly eighty ayahuasca experiences shaped his worldview. His research leans heavily on pattern-finding across distant cultures and alternative interpretations of ancient myths. Critics argue this is exactly the problem. Connecting scattered anomalies into a single global narrative is the kind of reasoning archaeology has learned to distrust. It can explain anything, which means it explains nothing in a testable way. The mainstream counter-position is not that early humans were primitive. It is that extraordinary claims require evidence that survives excavation and physical dating rather than argument by analogy.

People listen to Hancock anyway because he is a gifted storyteller. The underlying question is genuinely interesting. How much of early human history is actually missing? The gaps in the record are real. Much of the human past was not written down. Coastlines have changed dramatically. Entire landscapes were lost when sea levels rose at the end of the last ice age. A careful person can acknowledge how much we genuinely do not know without accepting that a specific advanced civilisation was erased by a comet.

The real issue here is institutional gatekeeping. When an unpopular thesis is dismissed as heresy by the establishment, how do we tell the difference between an idea that is being suppressed and an idea that is simply unsupported? Hancock claims orthodoxy closes ranks to protect its funding and prestige. The alternative view is that fields like archaeology have earned their consensus through methods specifically designed to resist persuasive storytelling. The discipline comes from verifying the evidence, not just believing a compelling narrative.

Hold both of those possibilities open at once. Resist the temptation to declare the matter settled just because an institution or a charismatic author told you to. Doubt the claims, verify the evidence, and change your mind only when the data warrants it.
