In February twenty twenty-six, Block cut nearly half its workforce — roughly five thousand people from a company of ten thousand. Jack Dorsey, the chief executive, had predicted this would happen across the industry within a year, and he was right. But here is what nobody is asking: was that a failure of corporate judgment, or was it the market working exactly as designed? A new paper by two economists suggests it is the latter, and the implications are deeply uncomfortable for anyone who earns a living.

The paper runs to fifty-three pages and appeared on arXiv in March twenty twenty-six. Brett Hemenway Falk from the University of Pennsylvania and Gerry Tsoukalas from Boston University built a game-theoretic model to answer a deceptively simple question. When companies start replacing human workers with AI, does competition make things better or worse? Their answer: competition makes things catastrophically worse, and no amount of retraining, universal basic income, or taxation is going to fix it.

The mechanism is elegant and brutal. Imagine you run one of ten companies in a sector. You lay off workers and replace them with AI. Your costs fall. But here is the catch: when your customers lose their jobs, they stop buying things — not just from you, but from everyone. You caused that demand destruction, but you only bear one-tenth of it. The other nine companies absorb the rest. That gap between what you save and what you destroy is the over-automation wedge, and the math shows it grows larger the more competitors there are. A monopolist would internalize the full cost and stop well short of full automation. Ten competitors barely internalize any of it. Twenty competitors push even further into the red.

The numbers bear this out. Over one hundred thousand tech workers lost their jobs in 2025, and in more than half those cases, AI was explicitly cited as the reason. Salesforce replaced four thousand customer-support agents with agentic AI. Cognition's tool called Devin, deployed at Goldman Sachs and at Infosys, allows a single senior engineer to do the work of a five-person team. These are not edge cases. They are the opening moves in a game that every firm is forced to play.

And here is the devastating part: the paper proves that talking to each other does not help. Even if every company in a sector agreed to hold back, each one would still automate first, because being the last to automate means absorbing the full demand destruction that the others caused. Mutual restraint produces a better outcome for everyone collectively, but it produces a worse outcome for any individual firm that tries to be the one who honors the agreement. The Nash equilibrium — the stable outcome that nobody can profitably deviate from — gives every firm lower profit than cooperation would have. They are trapped, and they know it.

Now consider the policy solutions that get floated. Upskilling and retraining sound reasonable until you examine them closely. The model shows they only eliminate the automation wedge if displaced workers get reabsorbed at higher wages than they lost. That has zero historical precedent. Universal basic income raises the floor beneath everyone, but it changes payoff levels, not payoff differences, so it does not alter any firm's incentive to automate. A tax on capital income sounds clever until you realize that a proportional tax on profits cancels out of the first-order condition entirely, leaving the equilibrium automation rate unchanged. The authors put it plainly: the externality is a structural feature of competitive markets deploying a cost-saving technology. Communication cannot fix it. Policy cannot fix it. Only a level of coordination that antitrust law currently forbids might fix it.

Roughly eighty percent of workers in the United States hold jobs with tasks that large language models can perform to some degree. That is not a prediction about the future. That is a description of the present vulnerability, and the competitive dynamics described in this paper apply regardless of how capable the technology becomes.

So what does this mean for you personally, if your livelihood depends on skills that a machine can now replicate? Retrain if you want. The model shows that helps only if the economy absorbs you at higher wages than you lost, and the evidence from every previous wave of automation suggests it will not. The market is not asking your permission. It is not consulting your preferences. It is simply following the math, and the math says that in a competitive sector, every firm has an incentive to replace you before the next firm does.

Jack Dorsey did not announce layoffs at Block. He published a game-theoretic prediction, and the market confirmed it. The question is not whether this is sad or fair. The question is whether an economy that values your replacement more than your employment is the economy we want to keep building. The market has answered that question clearly. The market always does.
