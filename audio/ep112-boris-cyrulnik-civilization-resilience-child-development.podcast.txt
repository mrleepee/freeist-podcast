An estimated two hundred thousand children in orphanages worldwide developed measurable cerebral atrophy not from malnutrition or disease, but from a deprivation far more insidious: no one talked to them.

That striking finding comes from the work of Boris Cyrulnik, a French neuropsychiatrist who coined the term resilience and spent decades exploring how early experience shapes the developing brain. His research sits at the intersection of what he calls civilization, that realm of conventions and language encompassing rituals, presentations, and speech patterns, and the raw biological machinery of a child's growing mind.

Cyrulnik's central insight is both simple and devastating: the first one thousand days of a child's life, from conception to the emergence of speech, fundamentally structure temperament. During this critical window, affective and sensory stimulation are not luxuries but necessities. When babies are not spoken to, the prefrontal lobes actually atrophy. These same regions normally regulate the amygdala, the brain's impulse control centre. Boys particularly suffer from this verbal deprivation. Without early language interaction, they struggle to master aggressive, sexual, and other impulses that girls, who typically develop language skills earlier, learn to regulate more easily.

Here is where the implication becomes uncomfortable for modern parents. Modern childhood increasingly means professional childcare from infancy. In France, babies enter collective creches remarkably early, cared for by rotating trained staff. In contrast, Russia historically required mothers to care for their own infants. Contemporary children may encounter four or five different parental couples and paediatricians over their development. These institutional innovations promised efficiency and standardisation. Cyrulnik's research suggests they may have extracted a neurological price.

The screen presents an even more pervasive threat. Research indicates that over three to four hours of daily screen time multiplies depression risk by four. Screens provide sensory input but deny verbal interaction. A child watching a tablet receives stimulation without the responsive dialogue their prefrontal cortex desperately requires.

Finland offers an intriguing counterpoint. Children there face no formal grading until age eleven, allowing neurological development to proceed without premature institutional pressure. Yet Finland also maintains robust parental leave policies and cultural expectations of verbal engagement.

Two hundred thousand children lost grey matter not to hunger or disease, but to silence — the simple absence of a voice that turned toward them. We have built the most information-rich world in history and aimed every screen at the youngest among us, mistaking input for attention. A child's brain does not grow on data. It grows on being spoken to, answered, and seen. The voice is not a nice-to-have. It is the architecture. So put the device down, and use yours.
