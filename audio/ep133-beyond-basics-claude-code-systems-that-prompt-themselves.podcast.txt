Imagine if every time you switched from your code editor to check Slack, your most capable colleague simply stopped working. That is essentially what happens when you hand a software development task to an AI coding assistant without giving it the same access you have. The moment you need to verify a decision made three months ago in a chat thread, or check a dashboard to understand why a deployment failed overnight, your AI partner goes blind. The engineering team behind Cloud Code have been thinking hard about this problem, and what they've discovered challenges the whole way we think about what a developer's job actually is.

Here is the central idea that emerged from their work. If an AI cannot access everything you can access, it genuinely cannot do your job alongside you. That sounds obvious when stated plainly, but the implication is quietly revolutionary. Your role as a software engineer is shifting from writing code yourself to creating scaled versions of yourself, what one engineer memorably called building clones. You are no longer a coder who occasionally consults an AI. You are becoming an architect of AI systems that carry your knowledge and judgment into dozens of parallel tasks.

To make this work, three layers of customization are essential. The first is access. Your AI needs to be connected to every source of information you rely on. Team chat threads that explain why a particular technical decision was made. Continuous integration and deployment systems, because AI is genuinely excellent at diagnosing why a build failed at two in the morning. Production monitoring dashboards. Internal documentation, design specifications, runbooks, and meeting notes. The practical exercise the team recommends is simple. Try working an entire day without leaving your AI coding tool. Note every single time you switch to another application, then connect your AI to those applications. You will be surprised how few tools genuinely require a human in the loop.

The second layer is knowledge. Institutional memory cannot be trained into a model through fine-tuning. The conventions your team follows, the recent changes that happened in the codebase, the quirks of your internal application programming interfaces, the vocabulary that means something specific within your organisation. Fine-tuning sounds appealing in theory but it is not cost-effective because frontier models evolve so rapidly that any training investment becomes outdated quickly. Instead, the Cloud Code team advocates for in-context learning, which simply means everything you want the AI to know gets passed to it as text. All customisation is just text files, and that is actually a feature, not a limitation.

The third layer is tooling. Think of it as building the agentic integrated development environment. Out of the box, an AI coding tool has basic edit capabilities, and the team draw a vivid comparison. It is like working with an old text editor from the nineteen seventies called ED while the rest of the world uses modern editors with syntax highlighting and intelligent code completion. The missing piece is what developers visually recognise as red squiggly underlines, those non-blocking warnings that something looks off without stopping you from running the code. For an AI agent, the equivalent is post-tool-use hooks that provide reminders rather than hard blockers. One insight from the team is particularly worth sitting with. The fastest way to make your AI agent better is a tighter feedback loop, not a smarter model. When you give an AI a tool that lets it verify its own work quickly, you get better results than when you spend money on the latest frontier model and leave it working in isolation.

Context windows have plateaued at around one million tokens for frontier models, and this constraint reshapes everything. Every piece of customisation you add must fit inside this fixed window. The team use a memorable analogy. It is like running a package manager on an Arduino, an extremely limited device where every byte of memory matters. They call this the zero-overhead abstraction principle. Do not pay for what you do not use. There is also a subtle but critical complication with the key-value cache. If you change anything early in your prompt, you invalidate the entire cache, which makes all subsequent tokens ten times more expensive to process. The practical guideline is to put stable, shared information at the front of your context, and keep volatile, per-task information near the end.

On the question of how to structure your tools and plugins, four primitives matter. Model Context Protocol was designed for simple chatbots that do not have shell access. The rule of thumb is to use skills for internal developer tooling and Model Context Protocol for shipping integrations to non-technical customers. Model Context Protocol does not scale efficiently because each tool's name, description, and schema gets loaded into the system prompt, consuming your precious context window. The solution in development is tool search, which lazy-loads tool definitions so only generic tools like edit and bash stay in the system prompt. Skills are simpler. A skills folder contains a markdown file with a one-line description in the front matter. They are easy to create, which is both their strength and their weakness, because at scale quality control becomes difficult. The description text is always loaded, which has a small cost, and reliably triggering a skill can require three hundred to four hundred tokens. The team is currently working on hierarchy systems that would let sub-skills load only when needed.

The recommendations that emerge from all of this are surprisingly practical given the abstract nature of the problem. Connect your AI to all your information sources, every one of them. Prefer tools that scale with intelligence over tools that hard-block behaviour. Use skills for your internal developer ecosystem and Model Context Protocol for external integrations. Be intentional with your context window, treating it like a constrained resource you are spending, not an unlimited canvas. Implement hooks and linters as reminders rather than barriers.

What makes this moment interesting is that the people building these tools are already using them to build the next version of the tools themselves. The team is exploring hierarchical systems for skills and using Cloud Code to develop Cloud Code, which means the feedback loop is accelerating. The engineers who understand these systems best are already living in them full-time, and that immersion is producing insights that no amount of external analysis could replicate. If you have been treating AI coding assistants as a faster autocomplete, you are probably leaving the most valuable part of the equation on the table.
