What if I told you that rental yields in parts of Kampala run between 7 and 9 percent gross? Try finding that in London, New York, or even Nairobi. Yet foreign investors consistently overlook Uganda, and the reason isn't lack of opportunity — it's lack of awareness about how the game actually works.

Uganda has nearly 50 million people, a median age that makes Europe look ancient, and a capital city urbanising at over 5 percent a year. That's not a trend. That's a structural demand engine. A decent two-bedroom apartment in a suburban area like Kira or Najjera costs roughly 70 to 95 thousand dollars. Entry-level pricing in a city absorbing rural migrants at breakneck speed.

But here is the catch that nobody puts on the brochure: foreigners cannot own freehold land in Uganda. The 1995 Constitution reserves freehold, mailo, and customary tenure for Ugandan citizens only. What you can get is a leasehold — up to 99 years — and a registered leasehold gives you development rights, mortgage access, and the ability to sell. The problem is resale value decays as the lease shortens. You are not buying land. You are buying a diminishing time slice of land.

The cleanest structure is a condominium unit under the Condominium Property Act of 2001. You get a separately registered title, the building is subdivided into individually owned units, and common areas are jointly held. Most Kampala apartment developments are structured this way. Target those. And a word of warning: title fraud is endemic. Fake titles, forged documents, the same plot sold to multiple buyers — concentrated in exactly the fast-growing areas a foreign investor would target. You need a thorough title search, an independent local lawyer, and physical site verification. Skip any of these and you are gambling, not investing.

Now the tax squeeze. Rental income tax sits at 12 percent of gross annual rent — and here is the kicker — individuals cannot deduct expenses. Not maintenance, not management fees, not repairs. The 12 percent applies to gross rent. Capital gains tax is 30 percent of the gain when you sell. Budget accordingly.

Do the honest math. You buy a two-bedroom for 85 thousand dollars. Gross yield comes in at 7 to 9 percent. Subtract the 12 percent rental income tax on gross. Subtract property management at about 8 percent — and this is not optional for an overseas owner. Subtract maintenance, service charges, vacancy allowance of roughly 8 percent, and a sinking fund for generators. What you are left with is a net yield of about 4 to 6 percent in shillings. In dollars, accounting for gradual currency depreciation, your net return lands somewhere in the 5 to 9 percent range in a good outcome. Respectable, but not spectacular given the risks you are carrying.

President Museveni won a seventh term in January 2026, extending nearly four decades in power. Constitutional term and age limits have been removed. He is 81 years old and there is no transparent plan for succession. A disorderly transition is the single largest tail risk to property values, the currency, and the rule of law. That is not background noise. That is the variable most capable of revaluing the entire market in either direction.

First oil is targeted for the second half of 2026, and the pipeline project is about 75 percent complete. This is material and already spilling into demand for industrial space and expatriate housing. But property demand tied to oil is a medium-term cyclical boost, not a permanent structural shift.

Practical playbook: buy condominium units with clean registered titles from established developers. Never land, never on a nominee. Target the mid-market suburban belt — Kira, Najjera, Naalya, Bukoto, Ntinda, Kisaasi, Kyanja. Assume all-cash purchases because local-currency mortgage rates run 18 to 24 percent. Engage an independent local lawyer and a credible property manager before committing. Budget 8 to 10 percent of rent for management, and treat a standby generator as mandatory — power reliability is the top tenant concern. Model net, not gross. Size it small within a diversified, multi-jurisdiction portfolio. Plan the exit at entry.

Uganda rewards local knowledge, active oversight, and patience. It punishes the passive and anyone who trusts the wrong local contact. The yields are real, the demographics are undeniable, and entry prices are genuinely low. But the government's hand is heavy, and the market's infrastructure for protecting foreign capital is thin. Know what you are buying, and know what you are not buying — which is freehold land, liquidity, and institutional protection.
