A Microsoft engineer once shipped six months of work in a single week using artificial intelligence. He called it a problem.

David Fowler, a distinguished engineer at Microsoft who leads the dot net Aspire project, recently published an article with a title that sounds counterintuitive: "AI Made Us Faster. That Was the Problem." Fowler is not anti-AI. His team built Aspire, an open-source platform for orchestrating application stacks from code, and they used AI extensively during the TypeScript support work, which Fowler describes as a massive architectural change. The result was striking: six months of code produced in roughly one week. That should sound like a triumph. Fowler sees it as a warning.

The core issue, he argues, is not that the AI-generated code was wrong. It is that the maintenance burden it created was never properly accounted for. The cost of writing code has dropped dramatically. The cost of maintaining it has not dropped in step. This mismatch creates a hidden debt that compounds over time, and it does not show up in sprint velocity or quarterly reports.

Artificial intelligence makes it easier to produce code, tests, documentation, and surface area. It does not automatically make those things coherent, durable, or worth owning. Fowler puts it plainly: AI solves the production bottleneck, but the bottleneck does not disappear. It moves. In most teams, it moves straight into maintenance, review, and debugging. The speed gains feel good in the moment. The costs arrive months later when someone has to understand what was built, why it works the way it does, and how to change it without breaking everything else.

For experienced engineers, working with AI agents can feel surprisingly uncomfortable. Fowler describes it as akin to putting training wheels back on a bicycle. You know how to ride. You are capable of doing the thing without assistance. But the tool demands you work in a specific way, often a slower way, and that constraint feels like regression even when it is temporary. Fowler acknowledges that adapting to AI tooling can feel slower, frustrating, and uncomfortably like stepping backwards. He frames this as part of the transition rather than a sign that something is broken. Strong engineers are being asked to temporarily change how they work in order to eventually operate with different leverage. That is a fair description of what adoption curves look like, but it does not make the adjustment easy.

Leaders have a role here that goes beyond mandating new tools. Fowler is explicit that good leaders should meet people where they are and help them move forward. That means acknowledging the friction rather than dismissing it, and building the conditions under which the transition actually makes sense. For his own team, those conditions include fast continuous integration pipelines, comprehensive test coverage, and harnesses that let an AI agent verify its own work before submitting changes. Without that infrastructure, AI assistance creates more problems than it solves.

This is where the idea of agent-readiness becomes interesting. Fowler introduces it as a measure of codebase health, and the definition is practical. Agents work best when a repository has clear structure, repeatable commands, good tests, useful documentation, and consistent patterns. None of that is glamorous. It is just disciplined engineering. The uncomfortable truth is that a messy codebase was already expensive for human developers. With AI agents in the loop, that mess scales faster. The same debt accumulates at a higher velocity.

So where does that leave teams building software today? The teams that thrive will not necessarily be the ones shipping the most code. They will be the ones building with AI-readiness in mind from the start, investing in the structural quality that makes AI assistance sustainable rather than just fast. Speed means nothing if your codebase becomes unmaintainable by the time the next feature request arrives.

Next time someone boasts about how much code AI shipped this quarter, ask what the test coverage looks like and who will be debugging it at two in the morning next year. That is where the real cost lives, and it never shows up in the velocity charts.
