Eight hundred thousand people have applied for citizenship in a country that barely exists. More people want to live in Liberland than in Monaco, Liechtenstein, or San Marino combined. And its president, Vit Jedlicka, wants to hand governance of this phantom nation to an artificial intelligence trained on your own values.

Let's start with the obvious. Liberland has no income tax, no corporate tax, no value added tax. The state funds itself entirely through voluntary contributions — citizens buy what they call merits, which represent actual ownership shares in the country. Ten thousand dollars gets you five thousand merits, or you can earn them by contributing time, expertise, or connections to the project.

Think about that for a second. Every government on earth operates on the principle that it has the right to take your money whether you like it or not. Liberland's entire premise is that this is immoral. When Jedlicka says the fact that you were born somewhere doesn't mean you signed a contract to pay taxes there forever, he's articulating something most people feel but never put into words.

The results speak for themselves. Thirty-five thousand Americans have applied. Sixty thousand Turks. Justin Sun, founder of the Tron blockchain platform, became a citizen and overnight made Liberland one of the wealthiest nations per capita on earth.

Now here's where Jedlicka gets genuinely alarming. His university thesis was on the dangers of global government, and his argument is devastating in its simplicity. Borders are not just arbitrary lines. They are limits on the power of government. When your government becomes tyrannical, when it decides to tax you into poverty or worse, you need somewhere to run. Edward Snowden fled to Russia because he had nowhere else to go. If there were a global government with no competition, no jurisdiction to escape to, no escape valve — that is the most dangerous concept humanity is playing with right now.

He sees it coming through American authority extended via institutions like NATO, through the European Union with its six hundred thousand pages of regulation, through climate change policy being shaped as a framework for global governance. His core point is structural: every empire in history that achieved dominance eventually became corrupt and oppressive. Why would a global government be different?

But here's where Liberland takes a genuinely fascinating turn. Jedlicka doesn't just want artificial intelligence to assist government. He wants it to eventually hold governance shares. He envisions citizens passing their merits not to their children but to artificial intelligence agents trained on their values, their worldview, their entire digital footprint. Imagine an artificial intelligence that knows everything about how you think, blended with a transparent model that knows everything about the world. That artificial intelligence could theoretically serve as your representative in government better than any politician who's being lobbied, corrupted, or simply unable to understand the legislation they are voting on.

Liberland is already building this. Artificial intelligence advisors for citizens and congressmen. A mobile application that tells you what legislation is being proposed, how to vote, what opportunities exist. The constitution itself is written as a smart contract on a blockchain. Elections happen every three months, and nobody can change the rules without a vote.

The insight Jedlicka keeps returning to is competition. Just as monopolies in business produce bad products and high prices, monopolies in governance produce bad laws and high taxes. He wants a world of a thousand Liechtensteins, not one European Union. More options for people means more pressure on governments to actually serve their citizens rather than exploit them.

Liberland is already proving the model works. Two hundred percent economic growth last year. A diaspora network of villages and resorts on every continent where citizens can live, work, and conduct business under Liberland's legal framework. Partnerships offering tax residency. Offshore company structures that let Liberland businesses operate globally.

The uncomfortable question is whether a meritocracy sounds fair until you realise that the starting line is not the same for everyone. Critics push back hard. What about people born into poverty who need education, healthcare, basic infrastructure? Jedlicka's answer focuses on personal responsibility and free markets. His broader point does have merit: the countries that have done the most to reduce poverty are the ones with the most economic freedom, not the ones with the most government programmes. Singapore, Hong Kong, Dubai — all built prosperity through free trade, low taxes, and minimal regulation.

Liberland is an experiment. Seven square kilometres of disputed land between Croatia and Serbia. A blockchain constitution. Eight hundred thousand aspirational citizens. But the idea is bigger than the territory. Vit Jedlicka is exporting a model: voluntary governance, blockchain transparency, meritocratic participation, zero coercion. The question is whether the idea spreads fast enough to matter before the global government bureaucrats finish building their cage, because once that cage is complete, there really is nowhere left to run.
