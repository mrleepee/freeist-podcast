In June two thousand ten, the F B I arrested a man living in suburban Massachusetts who had been running a successful consulting business, raising two American-born children, and blending seamlessly into Cambridge intellectual life for nearly a decade. His name was Donald Heathfield. His real name was Andrey Bezrukov, a career officer in Russia's foreign intelligence service, the S V R. He was what intelligence agencies call an illegal, a deep-cover operative trained to assume a completely fabricated identity and wait, sometimes for years, for activation. What made his exposure remarkable was not just the spy swap that followed, but the fact that he had been publicly speaking at conferences for years beforehand. The F B I caught him because a Russian defector fed his name to American authorities, not because anyone noticed.

So when this same man steps onto a stage at the Saint Petersburg International Economic Forum in June twenty twenty-six and starts talking about the future of global conflict, it commands attention. Not because he is necessarily right, we will get to that, but because few people have lived inside multiple intelligence architectures as intimately as he has.

Bezrukov opened with a thesis that might sound familiar from any number of think tank presentations, but he grounded it differently. He called it the new war. The distinction he made was sharp: this is not a war fought primarily on conventional battlefields, with armies meeting across demarcated lines. It is a technology-driven and society-driven conflict that spans seas, rivers, and the broader Middle East simultaneously. He framed it as a generational struggle, comparable in scale to the two World Wars of the twentieth century. The first phase, he argued, is already underway, destroying the old system. The next phase will define a new order, and that order, in his view, will likely be centered in Asia rather than the Atlantic.

Now, this framing is worth sitting with. It positions Russia not as a declining regional power being squeezed by sanctions and isolation, but as a central actor in a pivotal historical transition. Whether you find that convincing or self-serving depends on what you think about his motives for saying it at a forum where the Russian government is the host. More on that tension shortly.

What he described as Western strategic aims were stark. He argued that NATO-aligned powers are pursuing three interconnected objectives. First, neutralizing Russian nuclear deterrence, using space-based systems or covert operations to render Russia's nuclear capability effectively null. Second, creating what he called traps in the Middle East, accelerating escalation to limit Russian strategic options across multiple fronts. Third, and crucially, avoiding direct nuclear exchange while still constraining Russian power through every other available means. This is hybrid warfare in its fullest expression: the battlefield is everywhere, but nobody's finger touches the nuclear button directly.

The threats he identified deserve careful treatment, because they reveal how a former intelligence officer thinks about vulnerabilities. He listed four major categories.

The first was nuclear vulnerability. He pointed to the possibility of space-based interception systems and, more troublingly, sabotage, the prospect of infiltrating agents who could disable nuclear assets before any missiles fly. Given his own history as someone who spent years as an infiltrator, this is not abstract theorising. He knows exactly how that kind of operation works because he lived it.

The second was state destabilisation. He described AI-enhanced attacks operating simultaneously on ideological, physical, and military fronts, aimed at breaking Russia's vertical decision-making system, the chain of command that lets a state act coherently under pressure. The goal is not to win a battle, he argued. It is to make the government unable to act at all.

The third was critical infrastructure exposure. He noted that drones can now reach any point in the country, and that current protection is inadequate. This is a threat he applied broadly, but listeners from any nation should recognise the implications. If a non-state actor or a state actor using proxies can strike anywhere, the concept of strategic depth evaporates.

The fourth was biological and biotech threats. He mentioned laboratories operating in proximity to Russian territory, budget-funded research into what he termed future weapons, and emerging technologies that could eventually allow individuals to create destructive pathogens. This one sits at the intersection of pandemic risk and geopolitical competition, and it is a category that most conventional defence thinking is still catching up to.

What struck me about his presentation was not just the threats themselves, but the policy recommendations he derived from them. He argued for accepting prolonged conflict, preparing for what he called a hot or chaotic war lasting several decades. Not a Cold War with proxies and brinksmanship, but something more continuous and more dangerous. Two generations, he said, would need to maintain near-continuous military posture. That is a staggering proposition when you actually sit with it.

On infrastructure, he was specific. New critical assets, missile communications systems, data centres, command centres, should be built deep underground or shielded like nuclear power plants. He was not speaking in generalities. He was describing a rebuilding of national resilience from the physical ground up.

His call for reforming command and control was interesting from someone who spent years as a clandestine operative. He argued for shifting to a culture of trust and rapid delegation, enabling coordinated action at all levels while maintaining central oversight. This is essentially a critique of rigid hierarchy, the very structure that made the Soviet system both powerful and brittle.

He also pushed hard for boosting biodefence, calling for heavy investment in monitoring, detection, and response systems to counter bioterrorism and emerging biological risks. And he advocated integrating military and civilian economies, developing dual-use technologies like drones and medical advances, and aligning military procurement with the broader economy rather than treating defence as a separate silo.

The most provocative recommendation was arguably his call for a more assertive posture. Stop being good, he said, his word choice is worth noting. Demonstrate willingness to enforce red lines. And specifically, leverage energy exports like gas as a deterrent, comparable in strategic effect to a small nuclear strike. That is not a metaphor. He was drawing a direct parallel between energy leverage and nuclear deterrence.

Now, here is where multiple perspectives matter. Bezrukov is speaking at a forum hosted by the Russian government. The message, that Russia faces existential threats requiring a permanent war footing, that Western powers are engaged in an active campaign to neutralise Russian security, that only strength and assertiveness can ensure survival, serves a particular narrative. The audience includes government officials, business leaders, and foreign dignitaries. Every thesis he advances also functions as a justification for policies those officials already want to pursue.

But that does not mean the underlying analysis is wrong. The vulnerabilities he described, nuclear infrastructure exposure, critical system fragility, biological risk, AI-enabled destabilisation, are real. They are not uniquely Russian problems. Any nation that depends on centralised command structures, integrated supply chains, and civilian infrastructure is exposed to the same threat vectors. The United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, France, all of them operate systems that a sufficiently determined adversary could disrupt catastrophically.

What differs is the conclusion drawn from those vulnerabilities. Bezrukov's answer is more state power, more military integration into civil society, more assertiveness, more willingness to use leverage as a weapon. An agorist perspective, and this is where Señora Freedom's analysis comes in, would note that every solution he proposes concentrates more control in fewer hands, integrates more of daily life into the war machine, and assumes that the state is the appropriate entity to manage these risks. That assumption is exactly what deserves scrutiny.

The real question is not whether these threats exist. They do. The real question is whether embedding defence readiness into every aspect of the economy, infrastructure, governance, and culture makes a nation more resilient or more brittle. History suggests that societies mobilised for permanent war tend to excel at certain kinds of innovation and fail spectacularly at others. They also tend to become very good at suppressing dissent in the name of security, which, as Bezrukov himself demonstrated during his years in Massachusetts, is one of the most effective forms of destabilisation available.

What he did not address, and perhaps could not, given his audience, is what happens to the citizens of a nation that accepts his framework. They become, in effect, the infrastructure. Their labour, their taxes, their freedoms all flow toward the war effort. That is the bargain being offered, whether it is dressed up in talk of national resilience or strategic sovereignty. The question worth asking is whether the people making that bargain ever get a genuine say in its terms.

Bezrukov's presentation at Saint Petersburg was, above all, a document of institutional anxiety. It reflected how a former intelligence professional, now operating openly, understands the world he inhabits. Whether you read it as a warning, a sales pitch, or a piece of strategic communication aimed at foreign audiences, it deserves to be engaged on its merits rather than dismissed or amplified without analysis. The threats are real. The solutions require scrutiny. And the conversation about what kind of security is actually worth having is far from over.
