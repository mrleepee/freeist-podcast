In February 2026, a company called Block cut loose nearly half its workforce — roughly five thousand people — because, in the words of its co-founder Jack Dorsey, artificial intelligence had made their roles unnecessary. That single move captures something much bigger than one company's restructuring. Two economists, Brett Hemenway Falk from the University of Pennsylvania and Gerry Tsoukalas from Boston University, have now proven mathematically why Block's decision wasn't a choice at all — it was an inevitability baked into the structure of competition itself.

Their paper, published in March 2026 on the preprint server arXiv, runs to fifty-three pages and carries a title that has started surfacing in boardrooms and policy circles alike: The AI Layoff Trap. Using game theory, Falk and Tsoukalas model what happens when every firm in a market faces the same calculation. When you replace human workers with artificial intelligence, you capture the full private benefit — lower costs, higher margins, happier shareholders. But the damage to consumer demand gets split across every firm in the market. You bear only one divided by N of the total cost, where N is the number of competitors. The incentive to automate is always too strong.

This is what economists call a demand externality. Each company's automation decision destroys purchasing power across the entire economy, but each company only feels a tiny slice of that damage. The result is predictable. Every firm's profit-maximizing automation rate exceeds what would be collectively optimal if firms cooperated. The wedge between private incentive and social optimum doesn't shrink as competition increases — it grows. With ten competitors in a market, the externality is barely internalized at all. Only a monopolist would fully account for the damage automation does to the people whose spending ultimately sustains every business.

The cold arithmetic gets worse. When integration costs finally disappear — when it becomes trivially easy to deploy artificial intelligence across an organisation — the game transforms into what game theorists call a pure Prisoner's Dilemma. Defection, meaning aggressive automation, becomes strictly dominant. You cannot talk your way out of it. Communication cannot fix the problem because each firm knows that every other firm has the same incentive to defect, regardless of what anyone promises. The paper's first corollary makes this explicit: once firms cross a critical threshold, full automation becomes the dominant strategy for everyone.

Here is where the numbers become uncomfortable. The total deadweight loss from this trap equals N times L times the difference between demand loss and cost saving, where L represents the number of tasks being automated. As the number of competitors grows, as the variety of tasks that can be automated expands, the losses compound. If the cost saving from automation is less than the damage done to consumer demand per task — and for many industries it is — the cooperative optimum is zero automation. Not a slower pace, not thoughtful adoption, but none at all. That is the economically rational outcome for society. And it is completely incompatible with what each individual firm is being forced to do.

Jack Dorsey predicted that the majority of companies would reach Block's conclusion within a year. He was not being cynical. He was being game-theoretically honest. The competitive pressure leaves no room for altruism. Over one hundred thousand tech workers lost their jobs in 2025 alone, and in more than half of those cuts, artificial intelligence was cited as the primary driver. This is not a blip, not a correction, not a temporary disruption while the market finds a new equilibrium. This is a structural trap. The people building the systems understand what they have created, even if the people regulating them do not.

Block did not shed five thousand people because Jack Dorsey is cruel. It shed them because, as Falk and Tsoukalas proved on paper, defection is the dominant strategy — and a game where everyone is forced to defect has no winners, only a last mover standing in the rubble. No pledge, no hearing, no executive hand-wringing fixes a structure where refusing to automate simply means a competitor automates you first. Either we write enforceable rules instead of voluntary promises, or we automate our way to an economy with no one left who can afford to buy. So stop asking whether your job survives the cut. Ask whether anyone will still be able to afford what you make once it does.
