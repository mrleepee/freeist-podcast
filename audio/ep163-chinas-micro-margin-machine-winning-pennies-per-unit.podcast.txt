A sourcing consultant who posts under the handle Eric Cracks China recently published a video he called "The fifteen-cent miracle, the most ruthless supply chain on Earth." His central claim is that Chinese manufacturing operates on less than one cent of profit per unit. Western hardware founders, by contrast, typically chase margins around fifty percent. That gap sounds like a story about cheap labour. It is not. It is a story about what happens when an entire supply chain lives within driving distance of itself.

Here is the catch. Eric sells sourcing services. The video is lead generation. That figure of under one cent per unit is a characterisation of a business model, not an audited accounting. Treat it as a marketing pitch with a kernel of structural truth buried inside.

The structural truth matters more than the number. Chinese manufacturing wages rose sharply between two thousand five and twenty sixteen. By the mid-two thousands tens, those wages had surpassed manufacturing pay in Mexico and Brazil. If cheap labour were the whole story, China should have lost its edge. Instead, it retained and grew its manufacturing dominance. The advantage migrated up the value chain, not away from it.

The reason is ecosystem density. Cities like Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Yiwu host dense industrial clusters where vertically integrated supply chains run from raw materials through to final assembly within a single region. That proximity lets you prototype and iterate in days rather than months. When a toolmaker, a mould shop, and a final assembler are all within an hour's drive, the cost of changing a design approaches zero. The cost of waiting does not exist.

Massive volume then drives per-unit fixed costs down toward zero. A fraction of a cent in unit profit, multiplied across tens of millions of units, produces enormous aggregate profit. Automation has substituted for rising wage costs. Export-tax rebates function as industrial policy. The system is not winning because labour is cheap. It is winning because the iteration loop is tight and the scale is enormous.

For anyone building agentic systems, the principle transfers directly. When you design an AI agent harness, the same dynamic applies. The value is not in any single model call. It is in the density of the feedback loop. If your agent can retry, re-prompt, and self-correct within seconds rather than waiting on a human review cycle, you have built the software equivalent of a Shenzhen supply chain. Compress the iteration loop and the per-call cost stops mattering.

But the picture has limits. Low-value, labour-intensive manufacturing has already begun migrating to Vietnam and Bangladesh. That movement follows what economists call the flying geese pattern, where production shifts to lower-cost regions as wages rise in the originating country. China is increasingly competitive in medium- and higher-value manufacturing precisely because that is the tier where ecosystem depth matters more than wage rates. The cheap labour war is over. The ecosystem depth war is the one being fought now.

The fifteen-cent miracle is not magic and it is not theft. It is what happens when an entire supply chain lives within driving distance and iterates in days instead of months. That is not a Chinese trick. It is a blueprint anyone can read. The question is whether you are still competing on price while your competition has already moved on to speed.
