El Salvador sits at number one on a brand new passport index, and you can buy that spot for a cool million dollars. No, this isn't a travel freedom ranking. This is the Bitcoin Passport Index, and it measures something far more revealing: which countries have built their entire national identity around catering to people who already have enough money to buy their way out of problems the rest of us just live with.

The index landed on June first this year, published by twenty-one C B I, which sits inside a company called Bitcitizen limited liability company. It evaluates eighty-seven countries across a formula that splits roughly between Bitcoin-specific perks and what you might call the traditional ingredients of a good passport. Forty-five percent of your score comes from how friendly a country is to Bitcoin itself. The remaining fifty-five percent pulls from ordinary measures: where you can travel without a visa, how easy it is to become a citizen, whether the country lets you hold multiple passports, and of course the tax regime waiting for you if you actually move there.

El Salvador claims the top spot with a score of eighty-four point four five. That's nearly four full points ahead of Malta in second place, and Switzerland comes third. Now, El Salvador made Bitcoin legal tender back in twenty twenty-one, which was certainly a statement. The country's state treasury also holds roughly seven thousand five hundred Bitcoin, accumulated through various government purchases and initiatives. But here's where it gets interesting for our purposes.

The country offers something called the Freedom Passport for one million dollars. That programme accepts just one thousand new applicants per year, and the whole process takes about six to eight weeks. You get your new citizenship, your new passport, presumably a photo with the President if you want one, all wrapped up in under two months.

Saint Kitts and Nevis sits at number four on this same index. That country has offered citizenship through investment for decades, and it charges in US dollars rather than satoshis. One hundred and fifty-seven destinations accessible without a visa, and zero capital gains tax. The model isn't new. El Salvador simply took an existing scheme and rebranded it with a Bitcoin logo.

The index weights tax policy at twenty-five percent, Bitcoin's legal status at twenty percent, visa-free travel at twenty percent, residency and citizenship pathways at fifteen percent, dual citizenship rules at ten percent, and personal and economic freedom at ten percent. What emerges is essentially a ranking of which countries have designed themselves most attractively for people who already have substantial wealth and want to optimise their relationship with governments going forward.

Crypto enthusiasts will tell you this proves money can buy sovereignty. The rest of us might notice it proves something rather different. When the state starts competing for citizens the way a loyalty programme competes for customers, the people who benefit are the ones who were already winning. El Salvador didn't embrace Bitcoin to free its people. It built a brand, priced the exits accordingly, and called it sovereignty.

For those of us who can't afford the million-dollar buy-in, this index functions as a map of exactly which governments are optimising for the wrong people. Those choices ripple outward: tax policies designed for the wealthy affect domestic services. Housing markets absorb investors chasing favourable jurisdictions. Democratic accountability frays when the people most able to contribute are also most able to leave. You don't need a million dollars to notice what's happening around you. You just need to watch which direction everyone's running.
