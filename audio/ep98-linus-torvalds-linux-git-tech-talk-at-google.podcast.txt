What if the most important piece of software in the world wasn't designed by a committee, didn't have a corporation behind it, and fundamentally changed how humanity collaborates? That's Git, and the person who created it once spent an entire decade managing code the way some of us manage our photo albums — with tarballs, patches, and sheer force of will.

Linus Torvalds. The name itself carries weight. He's the Finnish software engineer who, in nineteen ninety-one, wrote the kernel that now runs Android phones, supercomputers, servers across the globe, and arguably the entire internet's infrastructure. But in two thousand seven, when he found himself suddenly without access to BitKeeper — the proprietary source code management tool he'd been relying on — he did what any reasonable person would do. He built his own replacement in two weeks. Git emerged not from academic theorizing or corporate planning, but from raw, practical necessity and a deep dissatisfaction with every alternative available.

The talk he gave at Google a short while later offers something rare: a direct, unfiltered look inside the mind of someone who thinks about collaboration, trust, and distributed systems in fundamentally different ways than most organisations ever will. There's enough material here for several episodes, so today I'm focusing on the philosophy — the "why" behind Git's design. A future episode can tackle the technical implementation and what it means for companies.

Here's the thing about Linus: he doesn't suffer tools he considers broken. When asked about C V S and Subversion, his contempt was immediate and public. "C V S done right" was, in his view, an impossible slogan — because the foundation itself was flawed. He had spent ten years using tarballs and patches, which sounds archaic until you realise that approach at least had one virtue: it didn't lie to you about what was happening to your code. Centralised systems, he argued, introduce politics where there should be none. They create bottlenecks. They make offline work impossible. They treat branching — creating parallel versions of a project — as something only experts should do.

Git flips this entirely. In Git, branching is trivial. Creating a new branch requires about forty-one bytes of disk space — essentially nothing. The real innovation isn't that branching is fast; it's that branching becomes a first-class operation, something every developer does constantly without thinking about it. You want to experiment with a new feature? Create a branch. Something breaks? Create a branch. You want to try a completely mad idea that will probably never make it into the main project? Create a branch. The tool stops getting in your way.

But branching is only half the equation. The other half is merging, and this is where Linus's philosophy becomes genuinely interesting. In a centralised system, merging is painful, slow, and usually reserved for specialists who know what they're doing. In Git, merging is what actually matters — and it should be fast. The Linux kernel, with over twenty-two thousand files and contributions from thousands of developers, averages four and a half merges per day. Four and a half merges every single day. If merging were difficult, if it took hours, the kernel would have collapsed under its own weight years ago. Instead, merges happen in seconds.

This connects to what Linus calls the network of trust model. He doesn't trust everyone — he trusts five to fifteen specific people. But here's the clever part: trust propagates naturally. If Sarah trusts Tom, and Tom trusts Linus, then Sarah's work can flow to Linus without Linus having to verify every individual commit. The system is designed around the assumption that trust is local and personal, not institutional. There's no central gatekeeper who must approve everything. Conflicts — situations where two people changed the same code in incompatible ways — are resolved by the people who created them, not by a maintainer who has never seen that specific part of the codebase.

This brings us to performance, but not in the way you might expect. Linus doesn't talk about performance as a bragging point or a spec sheet metric. He talks about it as an enabler of workflows that wouldn't otherwise exist. Git can diff the entire Linux kernel — every single file — in under a second on a five-year-old laptop. That speed isn't impressive because it's impressive; it's impressive because it changes how developers interact with their history. You stop being afraid to look at changes because looking is instant. You start treating your project's history as something alive, something queryable, something you can actually understand.

Performance also matters for merging. The faster merges happen, the more willing people are to do them. If merging took an hour, developers would avoid it. They'd work in isolation longer, they'd accumulate bigger conflicts, they'd drift further apart. Speed isn't about impatience; it's about encouraging the behaviour you actually want.

Behind all of this is a technical decision that seems minor but has profound implications: Git tracks content, not files. It doesn't care about individual files. It cares about the entire state of the project at any given moment. Every version of your codebase is identified by a SHA one hash — essentially a digital fingerprint — and that fingerprint represents everything, everywhere, all at once. If you rename a file, Git can still follow it because it knows the content hasn't changed. If part of your project lives in three different directories across four different branches, Git can still trace its history because it's not watching individual files. It's watching the content itself.

This design choice has an important security implication that often gets misunderstood. Git uses SHA one hashing for integrity, not cryptography. It's not trying to keep your code secret from adversaries. It's trying to guarantee that your data hasn't been quietly corrupted — whether by hardware failure, software bugs, or something more sinister. If the hash doesn't match, something went wrong. Always. You will know.

What strikes me, listening to Linus talk about all this, is how deeply the design of Git reflects values that often get dismissed as idealistic: decentralisation, individual sovereignty, trust networks rather than hierarchical approval. The tool was built by someone who clearly believed that removing friction from good behaviour matters more than adding barriers to bad behaviour. Make branching easy, and people branch. Make merging fast, and people merge. Build a system where no single point of failure exists, and the system becomes resilient in ways that centralised systems simply cannot match.

That's the philosophy worth sitting with. Git didn't succeed because it had corporate backing or marketing budgets or institutional adoption. It succeeded because it was designed according to principles that made sense, and it spread because people who understood those principles preferred working that way. The kernel grew to twenty-two thousand files and thousands of contributors not despite Git's distributed nature, but because of it.

Next time, we'll dig into the practical side — what this means for companies, how repository structure actually works, and where the tool still shows its rough edges. But the philosophy is the foundation. Build on the wrong foundation, and no amount of polish on top will save you.
