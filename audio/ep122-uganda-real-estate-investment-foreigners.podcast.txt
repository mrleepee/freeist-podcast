Seven to nine percent gross rental yields in Kampala — that is the headline that lands in your feed, promising a healthy return in a city growing at over five percent a year. Uganda's population sits near fifty million, and its capital is urbanising faster than most of its peers. A two bedroom apartment in Kira or Najjera typically costs between seventy and ninety-five thousand dollars, and the spreadsheet shows a seven to nine percent gross return. The headline looks good; the fine print is where the story changes.

Under the nineteen ninety-five Constitution, foreigners cannot hold freehold title to land in Uganda. The law does allow a leasehold of up to ninety-nine years, and the Condominium Property Act of two thousand one opened a legal route for foreigners to purchase units in registered condominium developments. The catch is that title fraud is endemic and the land registry's computerisation has moved at a glacial pace. A leasehold that looks clean on paper can be contested by multiple claimants, and disputes concentrate in the very mid market suburbs where foreign investors typically buy.

Before you collect a single shilling of rent, the transaction itself takes a bite. Stamp duty is one percent of the purchase price, and as a buyer you must withhold six percent of the gross price as an advance tax on the seller. That is money you must set aside at closing.

Once the unit is tenanted, professional property management typically eats eight percent of the rent, and a realistic vacancy allowance for mid market units is another eight percent. On a gross rent of eight hundred dollars a month, those two costs alone cut your gross yield by more than one-sixth before the tax man arrives.

The Uganda Revenue Authority levies a rental income tax of twelve percent on gross annual rent, and if you sell, a capital gains tax of thirty percent applies to the profit. Uganda also asserts the right to tax its residents on worldwide income, so if you spend enough nights in Kampala you may be deemed a tax resident and your foreign rental earnings could face Ugandan rates. The Revenue Authority has been actively pursuing diaspora owners and high net worth individuals for undeclared offshore income, making a compliance visit a real possibility.

Do the math. Seven to nine percent gross yields collapse to roughly four to six percent once you subtract rental income tax, management fees, vacancy, and the upfront withholding. Converting those shillings back to dollars, the net dollar return lands between five and nine percent per year in a good outcome — respectable until you factor in the shilling's gradual depreciation, which runs at historically low single digits each year.

The Condominium Property Act does provide a legitimate path, and a net dollar return of five to nine percent still beats many developed markets where yields hover around three or four percent. The catch is that you cannot cherry pick the return without also accepting the legal architecture, the tax regime, and the enforcement reality that sit underneath it. Those three layers are the actual product you are buying, and they must pencil out before the percentage does.

For the listener who treats jurisdiction as a tool rather than a cage, this episode is not a verdict on Uganda. It is a reminder that gross yield is the most dangerous number in any investment pitch. The legal structure, the tax regime, and the enforcement reality are the true variables. If those variables do not line up, the spreadsheet is just a fantasy.

So before you wire seventy to ninety-five thousand dollars to a Kampala condominium chasing headline yields, ask yourself three questions: what exactly am I buying, who actually controls it, and what happens when the government decides it wants my share? In Uganda the answers might cost you more than the property is worth. The shilling is depreciating, the registry is a mess, and they tax worldwide income if you spend too many nights there. Seven percent gross means nothing if the net is a liability. Do not confuse a spreadsheet with a title deed.
