A flat that rents for sixteen hundred United States dollars a month, sitting in a jurisdiction that does not tax your foreign income, while handing you a second passport for writing a single cheque. That is the pitch drawing expats to Panama City right now. But before you pack your bags, we need to look at the actual mechanics, the historical context, and the glaring conflicts of interest driving this current narrative.

The Panama City real estate market suffered a spectacular bust in the early two thousands due to rampant overdevelopment. For fifteen years, the market stagnated, slowly absorbing that bloated inventory. Now, the market has finally caught up. According to a recent analysis heavily promoted by an investor with direct marketing agreements and financial interests in these properties, the market is entering a new bull run. On their read of the market, transaction volumes are climbing, buyer inquiries are surging, and inventory is tightening. Over the last eighteen months, they cite pre-construction prices jumping to between three thousand five hundred and five thousand United States dollars per square metre. They note a ten percent rise in secondary market purchase prices, alongside a twenty to twenty-five percent surge in rental rates.

Rental demand is undeniably firm. The demographic shift is real. People are relocating from Dubai, Europe, and North America. There is a notable increase in American and Canadian retirees seeking better climates, alongside a reported fifty percent rise in Israeli expats seeking a safe community. These groups are driving occupancy rates consistently upward. 

But let us look at the numbers behind the hype. In the secondary market, you can find older properties priced around one thousand eight hundred United States dollars per square metre. New constructions cost twice as much, yet they do not generate double the rent. Consider an illustrative worked example from the source. A one-bedroom loft purchased for two hundred and five thousand United States dollars. It rents for one thousand six hundred dollars a month at a conservative ninety-two percent occupancy rate. That delivers a five percent net pre-tax yield. The math works because older buildings have drastically lower homeowners association fees. But remember, the people presenting this example are effectively licensed referrers who benefit financially if you buy. This is educational information, not financial or investment advice.

The residency route is equally seductive on paper. Panama sells residency for a fixed real estate cheque. The source cites a two hundred thousand United States dollar threshold for temporary residency, or three hundred thousand for immediate permanent residency. Treat these figures as possibly outdated. Panama's immigration laws have tightened repeatedly. The Qualified Investor visa real estate tier was set around five hundred thousand dollars in twenty twenty, with later adjustments. You must verify the current twenty twenty-six figure against official Panamanian immigration sources before acting.

The marketing calls Panama the last viable tax haven. The reality is more nuanced. Panama uses territorial taxation, meaning foreign source income is generally not taxed locally. But Panama has signed the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development Common Reporting Standard. Financial account information is now exchanged automatically. It is a territorial tax jurisdiction, not a sealed vault. A territorial tax jurisdiction that still sells residency for a fixed real estate cheque remains a textbook exit option. The real question is how many of these doors stay open as the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development net tightens, and whether Panama's is a genuine door or a closing window.
